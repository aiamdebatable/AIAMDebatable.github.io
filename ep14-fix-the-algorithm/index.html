Algorithm Bias: How Do You Fix an Algorithm That Decides About You?

The question: How do you fix an algorithm that decides about you?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

Two designs for a decision you can reverse — and the government is already running one of them on Medicare.

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in September 2026, Medicare had been testing a system in six states since January where a machine may only approve and a person must review every denial. The week before, a civil liberties group released more than 400 pages of the agency's own records about it.

The fight

Green arguesAutomate the edges, review the middle

Only the company running the system sees every case, so its own reviewer can sample the 99 denials in 100 that nobody appeals. Training on appeals means training on the people who can afford to fight. Let the machine settle the easy cases and send people only the middle, so they have time to really look.

Gold arguesThe contest is the training data

A reviewer who works for the vendor and is asked to confirm the machine's pick is only countersigning, and people shown the machine's answer first anchor on it. When appeals happen, they reverse three in four denials. So learn from the appeals, keep reviewers independent and blind to the answer, and count errors by group.

What both sides quietly agree on

A machine may approve, and nothing in the record asks anyone to review those approvals. A denial must be reversible by someone other than the model. Appeals reverse most of the denials they touch, and only about one denial in a hundred is appealed, mostly by people who can afford to.

What the shouting gets wrong

Both teams ended up building a system with a machine and a human in it. The record undercuts one popular fix: a human on every denial is no safeguard by itself, and the one error anyone caught in Medicare's test was in the reviewer. What's left is who owns the correction and who has to ask for it, because 99 denials in 100 are never looked at again.

Kate Both teams accepted the same settled ground. A machine may approve, and no instrument in the record asks anyone to review the approvals. A denial must be reversible by someone who isn't the model. And the correction signal reverses most of what it touches, and is pulled about one time in a hundred, by the people who can afford to pull it.

Kate What survived from green. The band. Send the human only what the machine can't settle, because a reviewer buried in everything becomes a stamp. And the denominator. Only the deployer sees the ninety-nine cases nobody appeals, so only the deployer can sample them.

Kate What survived from gold. The reviewer must be blind to the machine's answer, because the experiments say a reviewer shown the answer first is captured by it. And the errors must be counted by group, because the one algorithm found wrong on a subgroup was invisible case by case.

Kate What fell. A human on every denial as a safeguard in itself. The one running example asks its clinician to confirm, pays the vendor per denial, and the one error anyone caught was in the reviewer. And green's own engine, the band that narrows as the model learns. No deployed system anywhere has been shown to do it.

Kate So here's the design that survives both teams. A band, with the auto-deny side audited by outcome and by group, because that's the side nobody ever appeals. A reviewer who reads before being told. And two loops, not one. The deployer's review narrows the band. The contest can widen it.

Kate That leaves two dials neither team could settle for you. Who owns the correction, the deployer's reviewer or the person's contest. And the one both teams walked past. Who pays to ask. Because right now, ninety-nine denials in a hundred are never looked at again, and neither design changes that number.

Kate So the question we're handing you isn't algorithm or human. Both teams just built you a system with both. It's this. When the machine says no about you, who should have to pull the signal? You, or the system? That one's yours.

What our fact-check caught

2 of the 37 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidTwo vendors in Medicare's test denied more than 20,000 requests.

    The record saysThe records show about 20,000 decisions by those two vendors. Just under 6,000 of them were denials.

  2. Draft saidIn the lawsuit over one insurer's algorithm, 0.2% of denials were appealed and 90% of appeals were won.

    The record saysThe 0.2% came from a report on a different market in 2021, and the 90% was an allegation with no source. The measured figures: about 1 in 100 appealed, 3 in 4 reversed.

  3. Draft saidMedicare's test vendors included Optum, naviHealth and EviCore.

    The record saysThe released records name Zyter, Virtix, Innovaccer, Genzeon, Cohere Health and Humata. Optum and EviCore appear on no page as vendors in the test.

  4. Draft saidSending people only the cases the machine is unsure about is how real systems work.

    The record saysThe real systems found send a case to a person on fixed triggers, like missing data or a random audit. None routes on a confidence score.
