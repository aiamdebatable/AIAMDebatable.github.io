Algorithm Bias: How Do You Fix an Algorithm That Decides About You?

How do you fix an algorithm that decides about you?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

The verdictYour dialNumbers you heardWhat the fact-check caughtUp next

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

Two designs for a decision you can reverse — and the government is already running one of them on Medicare.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in September 2026, Medicare had been testing a system in six states since January where a machine may only approve and a person must review every denial. The week before, a civil liberties group released more than 400 pages of the agency's own records about it.

Settled

What both sides accept

A machine may approve, and nothing in the record asks anyone to review those approvals. A denial must be reversible by someone other than the model. Appeals reverse most of the denials they touch, and only about one denial in a hundred is appealed, mostly by people who can afford to.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesAutomate the edges, review the middle

Only the company running the system sees every case, so its own reviewer can sample the 99 denials in 100 that nobody appeals. Training on appeals means training on the people who can afford to fight. Let the machine settle the easy cases and send people only the middle, so they have time to really look.

▶ Hear Green at 3:40 on YouTube
Gold arguesThe contest is the training data

A reviewer asked to confirm the machine's pick is only countersigning, and people shown the machine's answer first anchor on it. When appeals happen, they reverse three in four denials. So learn from the appeals, keep reviewers independent and blind to the answer, and count errors by group.

▶ Hear Gold at 9:17 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

Both teams ended up building a system with a machine and a human in it. The record undercuts one popular fix: a human on every denial is no safeguard by itself, and the one error anyone caught in Medicare's test was in the reviewer. What's left is who owns the correction and who has to ask for it, because 99 denials in 100 are never looked at again.

The verdict

What survives both teams
A band, with the auto-deny side audited by outcome and by group, because that's the side nobody ever appeals. A reviewer who reads before being told. And two loops, not one. The deployer's review narrows the band. The contest can widen it.
What didn't hold up
A human on every denial as a safeguard in itself. The one running example asks its clinician to confirm, pays the vendor per denial, and the one error anyone caught was in the reviewer. And green's own engine, the band that narrows as the model learns. No deployed system anywhere has been shown to do it.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: When the machine says no about you, who owns the correction?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1The deployer's reviewer
    What it buys
    Only the deployer, the company running the system, sees the ninety-nine cases nobody appeals, so only the deployer can sample them. The judge kept this from the Green team: the denominator.
    What it costs
    In the one running example, Medicare's test, the clinician is asked to confirm, the vendor is paid per denial, and the one error anyone caught was in the reviewer. And the promise that the reviewed band shrinks as the model learns didn't hold up: no deployed system anywhere has been shown to do it.
    Not measured
    Whether Medicare's reviewing clinician sees the machine's answer first: nothing in the rules says either way.

    On the record: row 3 of the fact-check log, row 5 of the fact-check log, row 6 of the fact-check log, row 14 of the fact-check log, row 35 of the fact-check log

  2. 2The person's contest
    What it buys
    The correction signal reverses most of what it touches: plans overturned three in four of their own denials when someone appealed, and 95% of appealed nursing-home denials in 2024. In Medicare's test, the one error anyone caught was found by a provider who complained, not by the audit or the quality score. The contest can widen the band.
    What it costs
    It is pulled about one time in a hundred, by the people who can afford to pull it. Disability claimants with a lawyer were approved nearly three times as often as those without.
    Not measured
    How often an outside reviewer overturns a denial: the federal data hides any number under ten, so the rate can't be computed.

    On the record: row 18 of the fact-check log, row 19 of the fact-check log, row 14 of the fact-check log, row 22 of the fact-check log, row 21 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pick: Ninety-nine denials in a hundred are never looked at again, and neither design changes that number. That leaves the second dial both teams walked past: who pays to ask.

What our fact-check caught

Our drafts got these wrong before they aired. Every correction is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidTwo vendors in Medicare's test denied more than 20,000 requests.

    The record saysThe records show about 20,000 decisions by those two vendors. Just under 6,000 of them were denials.

  2. Draft saidIn the lawsuit over one insurer's algorithm, 0.2% of denials were appealed and 90% of appeals were won.

    The record saysThe 0.2% came from a report on a different market in 2021, and the 90% was an allegation with no source. The measured figures: about 1 in 100 appealed, 3 in 4 reversed.

  3. Draft saidMedicare's test vendors included Optum, naviHealth and EviCore.

    The record saysThe released records name Zyter, Virtix, Innovaccer, Genzeon, Cohere Health and Humata. Optum and EviCore appear on no page as vendors in the test.

  4. Draft saidSending people only the cases the machine is unsure about is how real systems work.

    The record saysThe real systems found send a case to a person on fixed triggers, like missing data or a random audit. None routes on a confidence score.
