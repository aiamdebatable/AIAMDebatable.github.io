The pitch, in one line

**A mail ballot that lands after Election Day has to pass two checks: when was it mailed, and who sent it.** About 15 states will count it if a postmark says it was mailed in time, and the Supreme Court said in June they may; the Postal Service says the postmark was never a promise about the day you mailed it. The who check is the same signature, witness, notary or ID check an early ballot gets. So the real setting is what proves a late ballot is on time and genuine, and only then how many days to wait.

The viewer's moment

One envelope: yours. (Invented and unnamed; never a real person.)

Your ballot came this month. You fill it in, seal it, sign the outside, and on the Monday night before Election Day you drop it in the blue box on the corner. It reaches the election office on Thursday. Is it real, and does it count?

  • Where you live decides first. In 35 states it must arrive by Election Day; in 15 (plus DC and three territories) it counts if it is postmarked in time and arrives within a window that runs from one day to about three weeks (log 2.1–2.3). Your state's rule is yours to look up, from your state or county election office. The episode never states it for "your state".
  • "When" rests on a postmark that is applied at a processing plant, not at the box, and dated by the plant's first automated run, which "may be later than the date that the mailpiece was first accepted" (log 4.2). Some ballots get no postmark at all (log 4.6).
  • "Who" rests on the envelope. The office pulls your voter record from the envelope's barcode and checks your signature against the one on file (32 states), or that it is signed (about ten), or a witness, a notary or an ID (log 13.1–13.5). A Thursday ballot gets exactly the same check as one that came in October; what's extra is a timestamp, a log and a look at the postmark (log 13.44–13.45).
  • If it is late, it is not rare to be late. Of every 100 on-time-postmarked ballots Washington voters mailed in a general election (2020–24), about 95 arrived by Election Day and about 5 after (log 5.3).

The episode follows that envelope through the plant, the signature table, the sealed late-ballot bin, the count and the dial, and ends on what the viewer can do with their own ballot this month.

The question

When a mail ballot shows up after Election Day, how do we know it's real — and should it count? (Lucas's wording, ruling 7.) After Watson, the live version is a design question for legislatures: what should prove a late ballot was mailed on time and came from the voter, and how long should a state wait for the mail? Proof leads; days are the secondary setting (ruling 1).

Why now

On 2026-10-01 the Postal Service sent every employee its "2026 Guidance on Postmarking Ballots" memo, signed by three executive vice presidents. It says USPS's "longstanding policy" is to try to postmark every return ballot; that "normally … we do not postmark or 'cancel' every piece of mail"; that some ballots will still "reach a Delivery Unit without a postmark" (it calls these "rare instances" and gives no rate); and that downstream units may not add one. It orders early cancellations in the week before Election Day, ballot monitors and hand-cancelling, and tells counter staff to hand-cancel a ballot when a customer asks (USPS memo, 2026-10-01). The same week's Postal Bulletin tells voters to mail "at least 1 week before the deadline" and that a manual postmark is applied "free of charge" when asked (Postal Bulletin 22712).

Behind it is a rule that took effect on 2025-12-24: the postmark shows the date of the first automated processing, which may be later than the day USPS took the envelope, and USPS "has never guaranteed that the postmark date would align with the date of mailing" (90 FR 52883, FR Doc 2025-20740).

And the legal floor moved in June. In Watson v. RNC (No. 24-1260, decided 2026-06-29, 5–4) the Supreme Court held that the federal Election Day statutes do not preempt Mississippi's law counting absentee ballots postmarked by Election Day and received up to five business days later: "The election-day statutes say nothing about ballot receipt." The holding is permissive: states may count late-arriving ballots; nothing requires them to. The majority called the appearance of results flipping after Election Day "a significant concern" and sent it to "the democratic process"; four justices argued the window invites both fraud and the suspicion of it (slip opinion). NCSL's table, updated 2026-09-25, shows every state's rule standing after the ruling (NCSL Table 11).

One live state: in Kansas, a court order restored a three-day grace period for this election. The state Court of Appeals denied the Secretary of State's renewed stay motion on 2026-10-02; the merits appeal is pending (order, 2026-10-02; Kansas SoS). ⚠ Re-check before air.

So the Court said states may wait for the mail, and the Postal Service says the stamp that proves a late ballot was on time is not a dated receipt. That is the when half of the question, and it is why the integrity argument (is a late ballot real?) is live this month.

Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

The law

  • States may choose either rule. Watson (5–4, Barrett for Roberts, Sotomayor, Kagan, Jackson) reversed and remanded the Fifth Circuit; it decided only preemption: "The question before us is a narrow one about timing," and "we do not consider the scope of Congress's authority" (log 1.1–1.4, 1.6). The opinion does not decide what counts as proof of timely mailing; only the dissent raises it (log 1.8).
  • The dissent: Alito, joined by Thomas and Gorsuch, and by Kavanaugh except Parts II-C-2 and III. Its core: late acceptance "effectively postpones the date on which the electorate's choice is made" (log 1.9). Its fraud and trust argument is Part IV, which Kavanaugh joined, so "four justices" is right for that argument (log 1.10).
  • The map (NCSL, 2026-09-25): 35 states require mailed ballots to arrive by Election Day; 15 states plus DC, Guam, Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands count a ballot received later if postmarked in time: AK, CA, IL, KS, MD, MA, MS, NV, NJ, NY, OR, TX, VA, WA, WV (log 2.1, 2.2). Windows: TX one day (5 p.m.); KS, MA, VA three; NV four; WV before the canvass (about day five); MS five business days; NJ 144 hours; CA, NY, OR seven days; AK, DC, GU, MD ten; IL fourteen; WA the day before certification, 21 days after a general (log 2.3). The grace states cut across the political map: Texas, Mississippi, West Virginia and Alaska are on the list with California, New York and Illinois.
  • No state counts a hand-delivered ballot after Election Day (log 2.4). The fight is entirely about the mail.
  • The trend since 2023 is toward receipt by Election Day: North Carolina (2023), Ohio (signed 2025-12-19), Utah (2025), North Dakota (approximate details) ended or shortened windows; Kansas tried, and a court blocked it for this election (log 3.1–3.6). Name the states (ruling 2), never a party.
  • Military and overseas voters: federal law sets a 45-day send floor and no return deadline (log 2.6). Thirty states and DC accept at least some of their ballots after Election Day, which is why Watson counts "roughly 30 States" (log 2.5, 1.7).

The postmark (the "when" check)

  • USPS does not postmark every piece of mail; for ballots it tries to (log 4.5).
  • A plant postmark's date can be later than the day USPS took the envelope, and a missing postmark does not mean USPS didn't have it (log 4.2, 4.3). The rule changed no postmarking practice (log 4.4).
  • USPS's own wording moved. In 2021 it told Oregon the postmark date "will reflect the date that the mailpiece is collected"; in 2025 it said it "has never guaranteed" that (log 4.9, 4.10).
  • An Intelligent Mail barcode scan is not proof of the mailing date, in USPS's own words (log 4.11).
  • A voter can get proof at the counter: a free manual postmark on request; a Certificate of Mailing (paid) (log 4.7, 4.8).
  • The postal record has failures, at anecdote scale: in the 2026 primaries the Inspector General found hand cancellers set to the wrong date at 5 of 9 plants, and at least 56 ballots in Harrisburg postmarked with the wrong date, with no policy to correct a misdated postmark; in 2020, about 4,200 ballots in one New York county got no postmark (log 4.12–4.15). No source gives a rate of on-time ballots with a late or missing postmark.
  • Identified ballot mail is fast: in fall 2024, 97.73% of identified ballots from voters reached officials in under three days, 99.88% within seven (USPS's own report) (log 4.17). Ordinary First-Class mail is slower (log 4.18).

The identity check (the "who" check), and how a late ballot is handled

  • Two checks, one envelope. The postmark answers when; the signature, witness, notary or ID answers who. Washington's statute puts both in one sentence: the board "shall examine the postmark on the return envelope and signature on the declaration before processing the ballot." Failing either sinks the ballot (log 13.28, 13.44).
  • How states check who (NCSL Table 14, 2026-05-20): 32 states compare the signature with the one on file; about ten (and DC) check only that it is signed; 8 require a witness; 3 (Mississippi, Missouri, Oklahoma) a notary; Arkansas and North Carolina a copy of ID; Georgia an ID number. The categories overlap (log 13.2–13.7).
  • Before it is opened, every envelope is tied to a voter: the barcode pulls the voter's record and reference signature, the envelope is date-and-time stamped, the record is marked returned; a duplicate, a deceased voter or an ineligible one is screened (log 13.1, 13.10).
  • The signature check is a human judgment, and it errs mostly against real voters. Washington's State Auditor re-checked more than 7,200 signatures: 98.7% of county decisions were appropriate, but "even experienced reviewers can come to different conclusions", and some counties were 4 to 7 times likelier than others to reject (log 13.11–13.13). Lab studies (abstracts only; not election error rates) find untrained reviewers far more likely to reject a genuine signature (26%) than to accept a fake (6%) (log 13.14–13.15, APPROXIMATE). Guidance and state rules require a second reviewer before a rejection (log 13.16).
  • The scale, 2024 (EAVS): about 198,000 mail ballots (197,978) were rejected for a signature that didn't match: about 4 in every 1,000 returned, the most common reason for rejection; 53,510 more for no signature (log 13.9). ⚠ "About two-fifths of rejections" is the EAC's state-by-state (casewise) share; by count it is about a third (log 13.8, corrected).
  • Cure: 35 states and DC require by law that a voter be told of a missing or mismatched signature and given a chance to fix it (log 13.17). In 2024, 585,457 ballots were set aside for cure; 317,191 (54%) were fixed and counted (log 13.19); voters are notified by mail, email or phone, with deadlines from before Election Day (Louisiana) to December (California) (log 13.20).
  • ★ The worked example: Pennsylvania, 2020. About 10,000 mail ballots arrived in that year's three-day window (log 13.27). Each was logged by name, address, delivery date, whether the postmark was legible and its date (log 13.25); each got the same declaration-and-voter-list check as any other ballot, plus timing screens (a postmark after Election Day, or no postmark and a declaration dated after it, meant set aside) (log 13.26); and under a Supreme Court order of 2020-11-06 they were kept "in a secure, safe and sealed container separate from other voted ballots" and, if counted, counted separately (log 13.24). Pennsylvania is a receipt state today (log 2.1). Keeping them sealed in their envelopes is what kept them identifiable: once a ballot leaves its envelope it can't be tied to a voter (log 13.35).
  • Other states' late-ballot handling (as read): Illinois endorses each late arrival with the day and hour of receipt; Nevada runs every returned ballot, late or not, through the same signature check and keeps it locked; Washington, Illinois and California let USPS data or the voter's signed date stand in only when the postmark is missing (log 13.29–13.32). No state found cross-checks a legible postmark against tracking scans, and none publishes how often its fallback is used (log 13.34, UNRESOLVED). Mississippi's post-Watson procedure was not found (log 13.33).
  • Custody (guidance, not law): two-person handling, often from opposing parties; every seal broken is logged and signed by two people; a daily count of envelopes received, balanced against the log (log 13.36–13.37). Almost every state lets the public or party watchers see some of the process; Colorado lets them watch signature checks (log 13.38).
  • Audits check the count, not the voter. All 50 states and DC run some post-election audit; 7 require a risk-limiting audit by statute; 31 and DC finish it before certification (log 13.39–13.41). A tabulation audit compares the paper with the machine count; it cannot tell whether a ballot in the pile came from an eligible voter. That is decided at the envelope. About 7 states report running an "eligibility audit" (log 13.42–13.43).

The integrity record

  • Proven mail-ballot fraud is real, and it runs through people handling other people's ballots.
  • North Carolina's 9th District, 2018: the State Board voided a US House result. A paid operation collected request forms and voted ballots (some "unsealed and unvoted"), signed as witnesses for ballots it hadn't seen marked, filled in blanks, and mailed them in small batches from nearby post offices. The ballots it touched exceeded the 905-vote margin (log 14.9–14.10). It was caught by the paper trail: about 26% and 36% of absentee requesters in two counties never voted, against 10% elsewhere in the district (log 14.11).
  • Bridgeport, Connecticut, 2023: a court threw out a mayoral primary after video showed people not allowed to handle absentee ballots putting stacks of them into drop boxes (log 14.13).
  • Paterson, New Jersey, 2020: a judge invalidated a city-council election over how mail ballots were handled ("rife with mail in vote procedural violations") and expressly made no finding of fraud, leaving that to the criminal courts. Charges followed; six years on, one case has been dismissed, prosecutors moved to drop another, and there is no conviction on record (log 14.12, corrected). Say exactly that.
  • Across 2016–2025 the cases that changed or voided a result are a handful, almost all local; NC-9 is the only federal race (log 14.15, APPROXIMATE).
  • The largest public list of proven cases has none tied to late arrival. Heritage's Election Fraud Map holds 131 absentee-fraud entries dated 2016–2025; searched in full, it contains no case about a ballot that arrived after Election Day or a false postmark (log 14.14, 14.16). It calls itself a sample ("not an exhaustive or comprehensive database"; "nobody really knows the extent of election fraud"), so: none found, never "it never happened", and no rate is computed from it (log 14.37). Neither side in Watson cited one either (log 8.1, 8.3). The nearest thing found is a 2022 county judicial race where a court counted late or unsigned mail ballots among illegal votes: office error, not fraud (log 14.17, APPROXIMATE).
  • The 2005 bipartisan commission (the Commission on Federal Election Reform, co-chaired by a former president and a former secretary of state): "Absentee ballots remain the largest source of potential voter fraud" (log 14.33, corrected: never drop "potential"). In the same report: Oregon's vote-by-mail "appears to have avoided significant fraud" through safeguards including signature checks, and the fix it proposed was to bar anyone but the voter, family, the post and officials from handling absentee ballots — the exact rule NC-9 and Bridgeport broke (log 14.34–14.35).
  • The post-2020 reviews found no fraud at scale, and said what they checked. Legitimacy checks: Georgia's investigators reviewed a random tenth of one county's absentee envelopes (15,118) and found "no fraudulent absentee ballots"; Michigan's Senate Oversight Committee, chaired by the majority party, found "no evidence of widespread or systematic fraud", its 200-plus "dead voters" came to two cases, and officials had removed about 3,500 ballots from voters who died before Election Day; Wisconsin's Legislative Audit Bureau found paperwork gaps, not fake ballots; a federal appeals court noted the challenger "doesn't plead fraud" (log 14.20, 14.23–14.25). Counting checks: Georgia's full hand tally and the Arizona legislature's contractor hand count of 2.1 million ballots matched the machines (log 14.19, 14.21) — they prove the count, not legitimacy.
  • Why results move after election night. Votes counted after Election Day have grown since 1992, about 10% of all votes in 2016, driven by mail and provisional ballots (log 14.27). The size of the late count tracks that volume; which side it favours tracked state partisanship, not mail volume (log 14.28, corrected), and has run both ways: toward one party in 2004–2016, toward the other in 1960 and 1968 (log 14.29). Which side gains depends on who voted by mail that year and when a state lets officials start counting (log 14.30). A grace period makes the late count bigger: in 2016, Washington counted 31.3% of its votes after Election Day, Oregon (receipt by Election Day) 6.0%; both vote entirely by mail (log 14.31; date it on screen).
  • Public confidence, as measured: Pew: confidence that mail ballots were counted accurately rose from 59% (2020) to 75% (2024), and swung with who won: the winning side's voters went from 19% to 72%, the other side's from 95% to 78% (log 14.1). MIT's survey names this the winner-loser effect, with one mail-specific residue: in heavy-mail states one side's confidence lagged even after accounting for which party dominates the state (log 14.2–14.3). AP-NORC (Oct 2024): about a third are confident in mailed ballots, among the least-trusted ways to vote (log 14.7). Sept 2026: 63% concerned mail ballots won't be counted accurately, yet 82% of likely mail voters confident their own will be (log 8.19).

The outcome record

  • Nationally (EAVS 2024): 584,463 of 47.96 million returned domestic mail ballots were rejected (1.2%); late receipt was 17.8% of rejections (casewise), behind signature problems and "other"; a missing required postmark was 0.9% (log 5.1, 5.2, 13.8).
  • The Washington arrival-by-day curve (UW Elections Database, 2020–24, mailed ballots postmarked by Election Day) (log 5.3–5.5): see the dial.
  • Counting speed tracks pre-processing, not the deadline: in 2020, states that barred pre-processing had reported 63% of votes four hours after polls closed vs 78%; the gap was gone by 24 hours (log 7.1). Los Angeles County had counted 97.5% of its final ballots by day eight in 2024 (our arithmetic, log 7.2).
  • Military and overseas: nearly half (47.3%) of their rejected ballots in 2024 were late (log 9.1).

Contested (the real fight)

GREEN — "Mailed on time and checked like any other: it counts" (postmark plus fallback proof, plus a window). A voter controls when they mail, not how fast the mail moves. Of every 100 Washington ballots mailed and postmarked on time in a general, about 5 arrived after Election Day; in a primary, about 11 (log 5.3). Those voters are not random: 14.7 per 100 of voters aged 18–25 vs 2.2 of those 66 and older, and rural voters nearly twice as often as metro voters (log 5.6). On "is it real": a late ballot gets the same identity check as an early one, plus a timestamp, a log and a postmark check (log 13.44–13.45); Pennsylvania 2020 shows late ballots can be logged one by one and kept sealed apart (log 13.24–13.26). The proven fraud cases ran through ballot handling, which a deadline doesn't touch, and the largest public list of proven cases contains none tied to late arrival (log 14.9–14.16). When the postmark fails, written fallbacks exist (Illinois, California, Washington, Nevada, New Jersey) (log 11.1–11.4, 13.29). When North Carolina ended its three-day window, 2,382 ballots that arrived in those three days after the 2024 general were rejected (log 6.4). Military and overseas voters lose nearly half their rejected ballots to lateness (log 9.1). Counting speed is driven by pre-processing, not the deadline (log 7.1).

GOLD — "Election Day means arrival" (receipt by the close of polls, plus tracking and a cure). A bright line needs no proof of mailing: every ballot that will count is in the office's hands, stamped by the office, when the polls close. It doesn't depend on a stamp USPS says it does not always apply, does not guarantee to date, and forbids anyone to fix (log 4.5, 4.6, 4.10, 4.13). On "is it real": no state cross-checks a legible postmark against tracking, and no state publishes how often its fallback proof is used (log 13.34); audits check the count, not who sent each ballot (log 13.42); the 2005 bipartisan commission named absentee ballots "the largest source of potential voter fraud" (log 14.33); a grace period makes the late count bigger (Washington 31% vs Oregon 6%, 2016; log 14.31), and counts that move for days look like what a suspicious voter expects. In the only randomized test found, telling people a race took six days to call cut trust in the result by 6.5 points (log 8.5). Voters adapt to the rule they are given: in 2024, receipt states rejected 0.233% of returned mail ballots as late, grace states 0.207%, a difference that is not statistically significant on the pooled count (log 5.7). The Court's own majority called results appearing to flip after Election Day "a significant concern" (log 1.5). The Bipartisan Policy Center recommends receipt by Election Day paired with reliable tracking and a chance to cure (log 7.4). Most democracies we checked (Germany, the UK, Switzerland, Canada) use arrival rules (log 10.1–10.5).

The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:

  • Gold at green: the stamp you rely on is not a receipt (log 4.10, 4.11), and the fallbacks are unmeasured (log 13.34); the curve is pre-2025 and one state, and Washington's late-postmark rejections rose about a quarter in the 2026 primary (log 5.5, 4.19); voters adapt (pooled rates nearly equal, log 5.7); grace windows hold the count open, and a bigger late count costs trust (log 14.31, 8.5); a late ballot with a signature problem may have less time to cure (an inference, not measured; log 13.21).
  • Green at gold: arrival by Election Day doesn't make a ballot more genuine: the identity check is identical under both rules (log 13.46), and the proven cases (NC-9, Bridgeport) happened through handling, not timing; the pooled tie hides a tail: averaged state by state, receipt states run higher (log 5.8, APPROXIMATE: direction only); "adapting" has a price — 2,382 rejected in North Carolina's first general without its window (log 6.4); the trust evidence tests a slow call, not a grace period, and its vignette mixes "slow count" with "my side lost" (log 8.6); which side the late count favours has nothing to do with fraud (log 14.28–14.30).

The rest, unchanged:

  • On "do grace periods rescue ballots that would otherwise be lost": yes, by construction, about 5 per 100 mailed in a Washington general. Whether those voters would have mailed earlier under a receipt rule is the open part; the pooled national comparison says many would.
  • On "do grace periods lower late rejections overall": the pooled numbers are close and not significant; the state-by-state average says receipt states run higher. Neither is a before-and-after study (log 5.9).

The design: "What proves a late ballot is on time and genuine, and how long should a state wait?"

  • Shape: a design episode. Green steelmans postmark plus fallback proof plus a window; gold steelmans receipt by Election Day plus tracking and cure. The verdict is what survives both, plus the one dial the viewer sets: what a state accepts as proof that a late ballot was mailed on time (and how long it waits for one). Proof is the main axis; days are the secondary setting (ruling 1).
  • Rests on (settled): the law, the postmark, the identity check, the integrity record, the outcome record above.

The floor every setting shares: the "who" check

Whatever the dial says about when, every mail ballot, early or late, passes the same who check: signature match (32 states), signed envelope (about ten), witness (8), notary (3), ID copy (Arkansas, North Carolina) or ID number (Georgia); a second reviewer before rejection; cure by law in 35 states and DC (log 13.2–13.5, 13.16–13.17). No state read adds a stronger identity test for late arrivals (log 13.45). The dial does not move this floor.

Verdict framing

"The design that survives both teams' objections, and the dial you still have to set." Candidates, each to be earned in the debate and dropped if it is not:

  • Survives: **a late ballot is checked for who exactly like an early one; the weak link is proof of when, and both sides' best rule protects the voter who acts early.** Green's point survives because a real share of on-time mail arrives late, the voter can't control it, and nothing in the record ties fraud to late arrival. Gold's survives because the postmark is not a dated receipt, the fallbacks are unmeasured, a longer count measurably costs trust, and voters do adapt to a firm line. The integrity record survives on both sides: mail ballots are where proven fraud has happened, through ballot handling, which neither deadline touches. The curve survives as a fact both must live with.
  • Fell: "Late ballots are fraud" (no case found tied to late arrival). "Mail-ballot fraud is a myth" (a US House result was voided over it). "The postmark proves when you mailed it" (USPS: never guaranteed). "Audits prove every ballot is legitimate" (they check the count). "Results that move after election night are a sign of fraud" (the shift tracks counting rules and who voted by mail, and has run both ways). "Grace periods are why counts are slow" (pre-processing drives speed, though a grace period makes the late count bigger). "Grace periods obviously cut rejections" (pooled rates are nearly equal). "The Court required states to count late ballots" (it allowed it). "This is one party's rule" (Texas and California both count late ballots).
  • The one dial the viewer sets — named, never set: what should your state accept as proof that a late ballot was mailed on time — and how long should it wait for one? Four proof settings (A–D) with their price tags, and the days curve beneath them. The research earned the price tag on each setting. It did not earn a right answer.

The reframe

The shouting is about whether late ballots are real, and it is fought as if the answer were the number of days. The record splits the question in two.

  1. "Is it real?" is mostly answered upstream of the deadline. Whether a ballot came from an eligible voter is settled at the envelope — signature, witness, notary or ID, a second reviewer, a cure — and that check is the same for a ballot that lands in October or on Friday. The proven fraud cases broke through handling (who collects, witnesses and delivers ballots), not through timing.
  2. "Was it on time?" is the weak link, and it is unmeasured. A grace period is only as good as the evidence that a ballot was mailed on time, and USPS says the postmark is the date of a machine run, not of mailing, that some ballots get none, and that no one downstream may fix it. Fallbacks exist and nobody measures them. Washington's late-postmark rejections rose about a quarter in the 2026 primary while the postal network changed, and USPS says its practice didn't. Both can be true: the practice is unchanged, the transport changed, so the date drifts (log 4.19, 4.21).
  3. Days have a price tag. One day rescues more than half the stragglers, three days three in four, seven nearly all; every extra day also keeps the count, and the public's doubt, open longer.
  4. Where the reframe does not hold: in the 35 receipt states proof of mailing doesn't matter at all; there the only question is delivery time. And nobody has measured how many on-time ballots get a late or missing postmark; USPS calls them "rare" and the examples are anecdote-scale. So the episode says "proof is the unmeasured half", never "proof is the real problem and days don't matter".

Both sides quietly agree

Nobody in the record defends the postmark as proof. USPS says it never guaranteed the date. The receipt side's case rests on that. The grace side's states have written fallbacks for when it fails (Illinois, California, Washington, Nevada, New Jersey). Kansas's own law accepts what USPS "otherwise" shows. Even the Watson dissent asks it out loud: "May States use something other than a ballot's postmark to determine when someone voted?"

Nobody proposes a different identity check for late ballots. Both rules rest on the same signature, witness, notary or ID check, and both lean on cure and tracking.

Both live with the same weak point for fraud: ballot handling. The 2005 bipartisan commission's fix (only the voter, family, the post and officials may handle a ballot) applies under either deadline.

And every official voice gives the voter the same advice under every rule: don't count on the mail in the last week. USPS says mail at least one week before your deadline and ask for a free hand postmark; BPC's receipt-by-Election-Day design is built around tracking so the voter can see the ballot land. The Watson majority and dissent also agree on one thing: this is for legislatures to decide.

Make it Matter — the viewer's own ballot this month

  • The story, ep21-style: one envelope, followed. The kitchen table (you sign the outside) → the blue box on Monday night → the truck → the plant, where the postmark is stamped (maybe tomorrow) → the election office on Thursday: the barcode pulls your record, someone compares your signature, a second person looks if it seems off → the late-ballot log and the sealed bin → the count → the dial. The ringmaster asks what the viewer is thinking, twice: "So the date on my envelope isn't the day I mailed it?" — "Not necessarily." And: "So who checks it was actually me?" — "The same person who checks everyone's, the same way." Hold both.
  • The fear against the record, by role, no candidates: "late ballots flip elections" vs: small local races have reversed in post-Election-Day counts that include late-arriving mail, but no audited case isolates the late arrivals (log 7.5); the biggest moves in 2024 came from ballots received on Election Day being counted afterward (log 7.6). "Mail ballots get faked" vs: they have been, through people collecting and filling them, and those cases were caught by the paper trail (log 14.9–14.13).
  • The trust beat (ruling 3): in the only randomized test found, being told a race took six days to call cut trust by 6.5 points; a 40-second explainer won most of it back. Caveat on air: it tested a slow call, not a grace period, and its vignette mixed a slow count with the respondent's side losing (log 8.5–8.6).
  • What the viewer can actually do with their ballot this month (information, never instruction about how to vote; every line points to an official source): 1. Look up your own state's rule at your state or county election office: arrival by Election Day, or postmark plus a window. In 2020, only 39% of mail voters in postmark states knew their rule, and 17% in receipt states wrongly thought a postmark would save a late ballot (log 8.10, 8.11; 2020 data, say so). 2. Mail at least a week before your state's deadline (USPS Postal Bulletin). For a state that needs the ballot in hand on Election Day, a week before is Tuesday, October 27 (our arithmetic). 3. Or skip the mail on the way back: a drop box or the election office. In 2024, 41.8% of returned mail ballots came back through drop boxes (log 8.13). No state counts a hand-delivered ballot that arrives after Election Day, and six want it in before Election Day (log 2.4): the grace period is for the mail only. Check your own state's cutoff. 4. If you mail it in the last week, hand it over at a post office counter and ask for a hand postmark. It is free; USPS's memo tells counter staff they "must hand-cancel" when asked (log 4.7). 5. Track it, and answer a cure notice. Online ballot tracking exists statewide everywhere except Illinois, Missouri and Wyoming (some counties there track; 2024 press roundup, log 8.15). If your signature is questioned, 35 states and DC must tell you and let you fix it; in 2024 more than half of those ballots were fixed and counted (log 13.17, 13.19). Tracking catches signature problems; it doesn't beat a deadline (log 8.16).
  • Military and overseas viewers are the voters this hits hardest (nearly half of their rejections are late), and their rules differ from civilians' in many states, including Kansas (log 2.8). Point them to their state's rule, not ours.
  • The pictures: one envelope with two stamps on it, "WHEN" (the postmark) and "WHO" (the signature line); a 100-ballot grid for the curve (95 filled, then 2.6, 0.6, 0.3, 1.0, 0.2); a ruler from one day (Texas) to 21 (Washington) with the units printed as the states state them; Pennsylvania's sealed bin with its four-column log.

The verification chain (top-up D1)

  • ⛔ "About two-fifths of rejected ballots were signature problems" is wrong by count. 40.7% is the EAC's state-by-state (casewise) share; by count it is about a third. Air "about 198,000, about 4 in every 1,000 returned" or "the most common reason".
  • ⛔ Never call "already voted", "deceased" or "not eligible" rejections fraud. They are catches, usually a voter who also voted in person; the first accepted ballot stands.
  • ⛔ Never say audits "verify" that mail ballots are legitimate. They check the count. About 7 states run an eligibility audit; 7 require a risk-limiting audit by statute (different lists).
  • ⛔ Never say states check postmarks against tracking. Washington, Illinois and California use USPS data or the voter's date only when the postmark is missing; none cross-checks a legible one.
  • The lab error rates (3.4% / 19.3%; 26% / 6%) are abstracts of lab tasks, not election error rates; air them, if at all, as "studies of trained and untrained reviewers", and only for the direction (untrained eyes mostly reject real signatures).
  • "A late ballot gets the same identity check" is confirmed for the states read (Pennsylvania 2020, Illinois, Washington, Nevada), not each of the 15: say "in the states we read".
  • Pennsylvania 2020's window is that year's; Pennsylvania is a receipt state today. "About 10,000" is the Department of State's estimate. The order said ballots "if counted, be counted separately": don't say how many were counted.
  • The "about ten" signed-only states: NCSL lists Nebraska in two categories; air "about ten". Cure: 35 + DC is "the law requires it"; the EAC's 43 is "it was allowed".
  • Naming: the 2020 Pennsylvania order may be named as a justice's dated official act (Justice Alito, 2020-11-06, as circuit justice) or said as "a Supreme Court order"; the Secretary of State by role.

The integrity record (top-up D2)

  • ⛔ Paterson: never "voided for fraud". "A judge threw out the result over how mail ballots were handled, and said in so many words he was making no finding of fraud; fraud charges followed, and six years later none has produced a conviction."
  • ⛔ **Carter–Baker: "the largest source of potential voter fraud".** Never drop "potential". Pair it with the same report's Oregon line when time allows; on air, "a bipartisan commission in 2005, co-chaired by a former president and a former secretary of state".

  • ⛔ Hand counts (Georgia, Arizona) prove the count, not legitimacy. Only Georgia's signature audit and the case records test legitimacy.
  • ⛔ Never "mail ballots cause the blue shift". The late count's size tracks mail volume; its direction tracked state partisanship and has run both ways. Say "the side that gains depends on who voted by mail that year and when the state lets counting start".

The outcome record (hand pass B)

  • **The UW 4.7% / 11.4% are shares of ballots mailed and postmarked by Election Day,** not of all ballots; drop-box ballots are excluded.
