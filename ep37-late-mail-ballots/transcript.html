NarratorWhen a mail ballot shows up after Election Day, how do we know it's real? And should it count?

The research · Whose rule is your mail ballot under, and how many arrive late

LenaWhere you live decides first. The National Conference of State Legislatures keeps the table, updated last month.

WaltSo what are the rules?

LenaIn thirty-five states, a mailed ballot has to arrive by Election Day. In fifteen, plus the District of Columbia, it counts if it was postmarked in time and arrives within a window.

WaltHow long a window?

LenaFrom one day in Texas to three weeks in Washington, after a general election. California, New York and Oregon give seven days. Mississippi, five business days. The units don't even match: days, business days, hours.

WaltIs this one side's rule?

LenaNo. Texas, Mississippi, West Virginia and Alaska count late mail. So do California, New York and Illinois.

WaltAnd that's settled law now?

LenaSince June. In a case from Mississippi, the Supreme Court ruled five to four that federal law doesn't stop a state from counting a ballot postmarked by Election Day that arrives later. It allowed it. It didn't require it.

WaltSo states are moving toward longer windows?

LenaThe other way. Since twenty twenty-three, North Carolina, Utah, North Dakota and Ohio have ended their windows. Kansas tried too, and a court put its three days back for this election, while the appeal goes on.

WaltAnd if I just drive it over on Wednesday?

LenaThen it's late everywhere. No state counts a ballot hand-delivered after Election Day. The window is only for the mail.

WaltSo how many ballots does a window actually catch?

LenaOne state has measured it, day by day. Washington, twenty twenty to twenty twenty-four, ballots that were mailed and postmarked on time. Out of every hundred in a general election, about ninety-five arrived by Election Day. About five came after.

WaltFive in a hundred. Who are they?

LenaMore often young, and rural. Among voters eighteen to twenty-five, about fifteen in a hundred arrived late. Sixty-six and older, about two. Rural voters, nearly twice as often as city voters.

WaltAnd how many days does it take to catch them?

LenaOf the late ones, one day catches more than half. Three days, about three in four. Seven days, almost all. And that's one state, measured before the Postal Service changed its network last year.

WaltSo where you live sets the rule, and about five in a hundred on-time ballots land after the day. Hold that.

WaltHold it there. Before we go further, Lena: what does a postmark actually prove?

The research · The when check: what a postmark proves

LenaFollow your envelope. Monday night, the blue box. The postmark doesn't happen there.

WaltWhere does it happen?

LenaAt a processing plant, by machine. Under a Postal Service rule that took effect last December, the postmark shows the date of the first machine run. That can be later than the day the Postal Service took your envelope.

WaltSo the date on my envelope isn't the day I mailed it?

LenaNot necessarily. In twenty twenty-one, the Postal Service told Oregon the date would reflect the day your envelope was collected. In twenty twenty-five, it wrote that it has never guaranteed the postmark would match the date of mailing.

WaltDoes every ballot even get one?

LenaIt tries. On October first, the Postal Service told every employee its policy is to try to postmark every return ballot. It also said that normally it doesn't postmark every piece of mail, and that some ballots will still reach the delivery office with no postmark. It calls those rare, and gives no number. And no one downstream is allowed to add one.

WaltHas it gone wrong?

LenaIn small numbers we can see. In this year's primaries, the Postal Service's Inspector General found hand stamps set to the wrong date at five of nine plants it checked, and at least fifty-six ballots in Harrisburg postmarked with the wrong date. There's no policy to correct one. In twenty twenty, about forty-two hundred ballots in one New York county got no postmark at all.

WaltSo how often does an on-time ballot get a late postmark, or none?

LenaNobody knows. No source we found gives a rate.

WaltThen what happens when the postmark fails?

LenaSome states wrote a fallback. California and Washington go by the date the voter signed. Illinois takes the voter's date, or a barcode scan where the county tracks one. New Jersey counts an unpostmarked ballot that arrives within forty-eight hours of the polls closing. But the Postal Service says a barcode scan isn't proof of the mailing date. No state we found checks a readable postmark against the tracking. And none publishes how often its fallback is used.

WaltAnd if I want proof myself?

LenaHand it over at a post office counter and ask for a hand postmark. The Postal Service's own bulletin says it's free.

WaltSo the postmark answers when, and it isn't a dated receipt. Hold that.

The debate · Team Green — fix the proof, let the curve set the days

ErinLena just told you the postmark isn't a receipt. Agreed. So don't make it carry the whole load. Take the postmark, and when it's missing, the date the voter signed, the way California and Washington do. Log every late arrival, and keep it sealed apart.

ErinAnd let the curve set the days. One day catches more than half the late ones. Seven catches almost all. Fix the proof. Don't throw out the ballot.

WaltHold that thought, Cole. Before you answer, the other check: who sent it.

The research · The who check: the same as October

LenaThursday. Your envelope reaches the election office, and it's checked before anyone opens it.

WaltChecked how?

LenaA scanner reads the barcode on the envelope and pulls up your voter record, with the signature on file. The envelope gets a date and time stamp, and your record is marked returned.

WaltSo who checks it was actually me?

LenaThe same people who check everyone's, the same way. In thirty-two states, someone compares your signature with the one on file. About ten just check that it's signed. Eight want a witness, three a notary, and a few want a copy of your I D, or its number.

WaltAnd a late ballot gets a tougher check?

LenaNo. In the states we read, it gets exactly the same identity check as one that came in October. What's extra is a timestamp, a log, and a look at the postmark.

WaltCan someone comparing signatures really spot a fake?

LenaIt's a judgment call. Washington's State Auditor re-checked more than seventy-two hundred signatures and found ninety-eight point seven percent of county decisions were appropriate. But some counties were four to seven times likelier than others to reject one. Lab studies point the same way: untrained reviewers reject real signatures far more often than they accept fakes. That's why a rejection needs a second reviewer.

WaltHow many get rejected?

LenaIn twenty twenty-four, about a hundred and ninety-eight thousand mail ballots were rejected for a signature that didn't match. About four in every thousand returned, and the most common reason of all. But thirty-five states and the District of Columbia must tell you and let you fix it. Of the ballots set aside to be fixed, more than half were fixed and counted.

WaltShow me a late ballot, handled start to finish.

LenaPennsylvania, twenty twenty. It had a three-day window that year, and about ten thousand mail ballots arrived in it. Each one was logged: name, address, delivery date, whether the postmark was readable, and its date. Each got the same identity check as any other ballot. And under a Supreme Court order, they were kept in a sealed container, apart from every other ballot. They were to be counted separately, if they were counted at all.

WaltWhy keep them in the envelope?

LenaBecause once a ballot leaves its envelope, it can't be tied to a voter anymore. Sealed, it can still be pulled out if a court rules it out. Pennsylvania wants ballots in hand by Election Day now.

WaltAnd the audits after the election catch fake ballots?

LenaNot as designed. All fifty states run some kind of audit, but an audit checks the count: the paper against the machines. It can't tell whether a ballot in the pile came from an eligible voter. That's decided at the envelope. About seven states report running an eligibility audit.

WaltSo who sent it is checked the same way for every ballot, early or late, and it's checked at the envelope. Hold that.

The debate · Team Gold · IN HAND BY ELECTION DAY — responds

ColeLena just showed you the who check is the same for every ballot. I'll grant that. It's the when check that worries me.

ColeErin's rule rests on proof of the mailing date. The Postal Service says its postmark isn't one, and a barcode scan isn't one either. A voter's own date rests on the voter's word. And no state publishes how often its fallback is used.

ColeAnd the audits won't save you. They check the count, not the envelope.

ColeSo my first answer. A rule should rest on a fact the office records itself: the ballot is in its hands when the polls close.

The research · Is mail-ballot fraud real? The integrity record

LenaYes. And here's where it's been proven.

WaltGive me one I can picture.

LenaNorth Carolina, twenty eighteen, a congressional race. A paid operation collected people's absentee ballots, some of them unsealed and unvoted. Its workers signed as witnesses for ballots they hadn't seen marked, and mailed them in small batches from nearby post offices. The State Board threw out the result. The ballots it touched outnumbered the margin, about nine hundred votes.

WaltHow was it caught?

LenaThe paper trail. Elsewhere in the district, about one in ten people who asked for an absentee ballot never voted. In two counties, about a quarter, and more than a third.

WaltIs that the only one?

LenaNo. In Bridgeport, Connecticut, in twenty twenty-three, a court threw out a mayoral primary after video showed people who weren't allowed to handle absentee ballots putting stacks of them into drop boxes. Across the last decade, the cases that changed or voided a result are a handful, almost all local.

WaltI've heard about Paterson, New Jersey, too.

LenaA judge threw out a city council election there in twenty twenty, over how mail ballots were handled, and said in so many words he was making no finding of fraud. Fraud charges followed. Six years later, none has produced a conviction.

WaltAnd late ballots? Any fraud there?

LenaNone found. The largest public list of proven fraud cases, kept by the Heritage Foundation, holds a hundred and thirty-one absentee cases from the last decade. Not one involves a ballot that arrived after Election Day, or a false postmark. Neither side in the Supreme Court case cited one. The list calls itself a sample, so that means none found, not that it never happened.

WaltBut don't serious people call absentee ballots the big risk?

LenaThey do. In two thousand five, a bipartisan commission, co-chaired by a former president and a former secretary of state, wrote: absentee ballots remain the largest source of potential voter fraud. Its fix was to let no one but the voter, family, the post office and election officials handle a ballot. That's the rule the North Carolina and Bridgeport cases broke.

WaltThen why do results move for days after election night?

LenaBecause more votes are counted after Election Day than there used to be, mostly mail and provisional ballots. Which side gains has nothing to do with fraud. It depends on who voted by mail that year, and when a state lets counting start. It has favoured each party in different elections.

WaltDoes a grace period make that bigger?

LenaYes. In twenty sixteen, Washington, with a window then, counted about thirty-one percent of its votes after Election Day. Oregon, which wants ballots in hand, about six. Both vote entirely by mail.

WaltAnd does a long count cost trust?

LenaIn the only randomized test we found, telling people a race took six days to call cut trust in the result by about six and a half points. A forty-second explainer won most of it back. There are two caveats. It tested a slow call, not a grace period. And the race in the story had a winner from one party, so for some people, a slow count came mixed with their side losing.

WaltSo how much do people trust mail ballots?

LenaIt moves with who wins. Pew found confidence that mail ballots were counted accurately rose from fifty-nine percent after twenty twenty to seventy-five after twenty twenty-four. Voters on the side that lost in twenty twenty went from nineteen percent to seventy-two. The side that won it went from ninety-five to seventy-eight. Election researchers call that the winner-loser effect. One thing it doesn't explain: in states with lots of mail ballots, Republicans' confidence still lagged Democrats', even after accounting for which party dominates the state.

WaltSo proven mail fraud is real, it runs through people handling other people's ballots, and nothing found ties it to a late ballot. Hold that.

The debate · Team Green — her gaps first, then the ballots a firm line throws out

ErinFirst, what my rule can't promise. Proof of the mailing date is the unmeasured half: nobody knows how often a postmark is wrong, or how often a fallback is used. And a longer count costs some trust. Lena showed you that too.

ErinAnd voters do adapt. In twenty twenty-four, states that want ballots by Election Day and states with a window rejected about the same small share of mail ballots as late.

ErinBut look at the other side of the scale. The proven fraud ran through people handling other people's ballots, and a deadline doesn't touch that. When North Carolina ended its three-day window, about twenty-four hundred ballots that arrived in those three days after the twenty twenty-four general were thrown out. And military and overseas voters lose nearly half of their rejected ballots to lateness.

ErinSo prove the mailing date, check it like any other ballot, log it, seal it apart, and give the mail a few days. A vote mailed on time is a vote.

The debate · Team Gold · IN HAND BY ELECTION DAY

ColeLet me grant what's true first. No proven fraud case is tied to a late ballot. The identity check is the same under both rules. And North Carolina's change cost voters real ballots. I'll carry all three.

ColeOne more against me. A receipt rule won't finish the count on election night. How fast a state counts depends mostly on whether officials can prepare mail ballots before Election Day.

ColeNow what my rule buys. Every ballot that will count is in the office's hands, stamped by the office, when the polls close. It doesn't lean on a stamp the Postal Service says it doesn't always apply, doesn't guarantee to date, and won't let anyone fix.

ColeSecond, voters adapt to a firm line. In twenty twenty-four, the share of mail ballots rejected as late was about the same in receipt states as in grace states. Averaged state by state, receipt states do run higher, and the two count late differently. But the totals say most voters adjust.

ColeThird, trust. A grace period makes the count after Election Day bigger, and a count that moves for days is exactly what a suspicious voter expects. In that one experiment, a six-day call cost about six and a half points of trust. Even the Court's majority called results that appear to flip after Election Day a significant concern.

ColeMy weakest ground, taken by me. A firm line throws out ballots that were mailed on time, and those voters are more often young, rural, or overseas.

ColeSo pair the line with what the Bipartisan Policy Center recommends: reliable ballot tracking, and a real chance to fix a problem. And mail early, or use a drop box.

ColeIn hand by Election Day. A deadline anyone can check, and a date nobody has to guess.

The verdict

RajThis one doesn't resolve into a yes or a no, and no design wins it. Three things from me. What's settled, what survived, and the dial that's yours.

RajBoth teams accepted the same record. States may count a late mail ballot or refuse it; the Court left that to them. Every ballot, early or late, gets the same check of who sent it. Proven mail-ballot fraud is real, and it has run through people handling other people's ballots. No case found ties it to a late arrival.

RajWhat survived from green. A voter controls when they mail, not how fast the mail moves: in the one state that measured it, about five in a hundred on-time ballots arrived after the day. And a late ballot can be checked like any other, logged, and kept sealed apart, the way Pennsylvania did in twenty twenty.

RajWhat survived from gold. The postmark is the date of a machine run, not a receipt, and nobody measures how often it's wrong, or how often the fallbacks are used. A firm line needs no proof of mailing, voters largely adapt to it, and a longer count measurably costs trust.

RajWhat fell. Late ballots are fraud: no case found. Mail-ballot fraud is a myth: a congressional result was thrown out over it. The postmark proves when you mailed it: the Postal Service never promised that. Audits prove every ballot is legitimate: they check the count. And this is one party's rule: Texas and California both count late ballots.

RajSo here's the design that survives both teams. The same who check for every ballot, and every late ballot logged and kept sealed apart. The weak link is proof of when, and both sides' best rule protects the voter who mails early.

RajThat leaves one dial neither team can set for you: what your state should accept as proof that a late ballot was mailed on time. At one end, arrival by Election Day: the office's own stamp, at the price of those five in a hundred. Then a postmark alone, only as good as the stamp. Then a postmark with a fallback, like the date the voter signed, which no state measures. Then any of those, plus a log and a sealed bin. And under every setting, the days: one catches more than half the late ones, seven almost all.

RajWhatever your state chose, the advice under every rule is the same. Look up your own state's deadline with your election office. Mail at least a week early, or use a drop box or the office before your state's cutoff. If you mail in the last week, ask the counter for a hand postmark, and track your ballot. What should count as proof? That one's yours.
