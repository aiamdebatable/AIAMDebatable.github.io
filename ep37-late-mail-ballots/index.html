Mail-In Ballots: How Do We Know a Late One Is Real?

When a mail ballot shows up after Election Day, how do we know it's real, and should it count?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

A late mail ballot faces two checks: when it was mailed, and who sent it. Two AI teams argue what should prove a late ballot is real and on time, and how long to wait; you set the dial.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in October 2026, the Supreme Court had ruled in June, five to four, that a state may count a mail ballot postmarked by Election Day that arrives later. And on October 1, the Postal Service told every employee its policy is to try to postmark every return ballot.

Settled

What both sides accept

States may count a late mail ballot or refuse it; the Supreme Court left that to them. Every ballot, early or late, gets the same check of who sent it. Proven mail-ballot fraud is real, and it has run through people handling other people's ballots, but no case found ties it to a late arrival.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesProve it, then count it

You control when you mail, not how fast the mail moves: in Washington, about five in a hundred on-time ballots arrived late, more often young and rural voters. Prove the mailing date, use the signing date when the postmark is missing, give it the same identity check, log it and seal it apart. Seven days catch almost all.

Gold arguesIn hand by election day

The Postal Service says its postmark isn't proof of the mailing date, and a barcode scan isn't either. So a rule should rest on a fact the office records itself: the ballot is in hand when the polls close. Voters largely adapt to a firm line, and a longer count measurably costs trust.

What the shouting gets wrong

The real fight is over proof of when a ballot was mailed, more than how many days to wait. The postmark is the date of a machine run, not a receipt, and nobody measures how often it's wrong. What your state should accept as proof was left to you, and under every rule the advice is the same: mail early, or ask the post office counter for a hand postmark.

The verdict

What survives both teams
The same who check for every ballot, and every late ballot logged and kept sealed apart. The weak link is proof of when, and both sides' best rule protects the voter who mails early.
What didn't hold up
Late ballots are fraud: no case found. Mail-ballot fraud is a myth: a congressional result was thrown out over it. The postmark proves when you mailed it: the Postal Service never promised that. Audits prove every ballot is legitimate: they check the count. And this is one party's rule: Texas and California both count late ballots.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: What should your state accept as proof that a late ballot was mailed on time?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Arrival by Election Day
    What it buys
    The office's own stamp: every ballot that will count is in the office's hands when the polls close. A firm line needs no proof of mailing, voters largely adapt to it, and a longer count measurably costs trust.
    What it costs
    At the price of those five in a hundred: in the one state that measured it, about five in a hundred on-time ballots arrived after the day, more often from young and rural voters. When North Carolina ended its three-day window, about 2,400 ballots that arrived in those three days after the 2024 general were thrown out.
    Not measured
    Whether a deadline itself changes how many ballots get rejected: no before-and-after study isolates it, and the one trust test was of a slow call, not a grace period.

    On the record: row 5.7 of the fact-check log, row 8.5 of the fact-check log, row 5.3 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 5.6 of the fact-check log, row 6.4 of the fact-check log

  2. 2A postmark alone
    What it buys
    It counts the ballot that was mailed on time and that the mail delivered late. A voter controls when they mail, not how fast the mail moves.
    What it costs
    Only as good as the stamp. The postmark is the date of a machine run, not a receipt, and the Postal Service never promised it matches the day you mailed. It tries to postmark every ballot, but some still arrive with none, and no one downstream may add one. In this year's primaries, hand stamps were set to the wrong date at five of nine plants checked.
    Not measured
    How often an on-time ballot gets a late postmark, or none: nobody measures it.

    On the record: row 4.2 of the fact-check log, row 4.10 of the fact-check log, row 4.6 of the fact-check log, row 4.13 of the fact-check log, row 11.7 of the fact-check log

  3. 3A postmark with a fallback
    What it buys
    When the postmark is missing, the ballot can still be dated, like by the date the voter signed. California and Washington go by that date; Illinois takes the voter's date, or a barcode scan where the county tracks one; New Jersey counts an unpostmarked ballot that arrives within 48 hours of the polls closing.
    What it costs
    Which no state measures. A voter's own date rests on the voter's word, and the Postal Service says a barcode scan isn't proof of the mailing date.
    Not measured
    How often the fallbacks are used: no state publishes it.

    On the record: row 11.2 of the fact-check log, row 11.1 of the fact-check log, row 11.3 of the fact-check log, row 4.11 of the fact-check log, row 13.34 of the fact-check log

  4. 4Any of those, plus a log and a sealed bin
    What it buys
    A late ballot checked like any other, logged, and kept sealed apart, the way Pennsylvania did in 2020: about 10,000 arrived in its window, each logged with the delivery date and the postmark, and kept in a sealed container. Still in its envelope, a ballot can be pulled out if a court rules it out.
    What it costs
    It adds a record, not proof. The weak link is still proof of when, so it is only as strong as the postmark or fallback it sits on.
    Not measured
    No state we found checks a readable postmark against the Postal Service's tracking.

    On the record: row 13.24 of the fact-check log, row 13.25 of the fact-check log, row 13.26 of the fact-check log, row 13.27 of the fact-check log, row 13.35 of the fact-check log

Under every setting: Every ballot, early or late, gets the same check of who sent it. And under every setting, the days: one catches more than half the late ones, seven almost all, in the one state that measured it.
