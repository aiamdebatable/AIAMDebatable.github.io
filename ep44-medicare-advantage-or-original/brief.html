Research brief — Medicare Advantage: Is It a Better Deal Than Original Medicare?

The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video.

Episode type: DEBATE, with a design-style verdict. Two teams argue at full strength; the verdict names what survives both and hands the viewer one dial. The judge may name the dial the research earned. The judge never sets it. Why a dial and not a winner: on the enrollee's half of the question the record's answer changes with who you are (your health, whether you would otherwise have Medigap, your state, your county, whether you also have Medicaid). On the taxpayer's half it does not change much, and the brief says where it leans.

Scope, said early on air: this is the choice a person makes inside Medicare: a private Medicare Advantage plan, or Original Medicare (usually with a Medigap supplement and a drug plan), and who pays for the extras the private plans offer. It is not about "Medicare for All", not about the Affordable Care Act marketplace, and not about any one company's plan. ⚠ Every cost comparison flips with its comparison group: Original Medicare with a supplement, without one, or everyone in it (log 15, 19, 22). Say which, every time.

The pitch, in one line

Medicare Advantage is a better deal while you're healthy and your plan stays in your county, and taxpayers and everyone's Part B premium pay for the extras that make it one; the catch comes when you get sick, and in most states the door back to Original Medicare's supplements can close behind you. Against Original Medicare with no supplement, an Advantage plan cut what people spent at every level, most for people with a cancer history (log 22, 23). Against Original Medicare as people actually hold it (most with some extra coverage, log 29), the best switcher study found no clear saving (log 19, 80; it compared switchers with all who stayed, not supplement holders only). Medicare pays about 14% more for people in Advantage than they would cost in Original Medicare, about $76 billion this year (log 327), and that money helps fund the extras (log 322, 354). Advantage enrollees ask permission far more often (log 114, 127), and the sharpest denial record is for rehab and nursing care after a hospital stay (log 84, 104). Three in four could be turned down for a Medigap policy if they tried to switch back outside a protected window (log 284), and only four states guarantee one to everyone 65 and older, with narrower protections in four more (log 273, 276). That is exposure, not denials (log 286). So the answer depends on which year you're buying for: the year you're well, or the year you're not.

The viewer's moment: which year are you buying for?

(His Ruling 1, 2026-10-21: the spine.) Four people at the same kitchen table in the same October, and the viewer is one of them. (Invented and unnamed; each situation is built only from log rows.) Each gets a short answer, and the answers differ. Then the money, and who pays, is the reason they differ.

  • 1. Healthy at 65, choosing for the first time. A $0-premium plan with dental and hearing (log 47, 59) against a Medigap premium of about $150–$200 a month (log 48, 52; Plan G's $194 is KFF's 2025 average among current policyholders of all ages, not a quote for a 65-year-old, log 472). Both routes pay the Part B premium: $202.90 a month in 2026; 2027 isn't announced yet, and Medicare's trustees expect about $210 (log 461, 464, 459). At full strength for the bundle: the yearly cap, at most $9,850 in 2027 (log 34); against Original Medicare with no supplement, lower spending at every level studied (log 22, 23); and the people already in these plans are as satisfied as people in Original Medicare, and more people switch in than out (log 310, 306). Their short answer: this is the one time the door is wide open. The six-month Medigap window starts with Part B at 65 and happens once (log 449, 452, 456); inside it an insurer can't turn you down or charge more because of your health, though prices still differ by age, smoking, sex and where you live (log 450, 453, 455). Choose a plan instead, and the first-year trial right lets you leave within 12 months and buy any Medigap policy (log 263, 265, 482). After that, in most states, a supplement can be refused or priced on your health (log 457, 458, 283).
  • 2. The parent after a hospital stay (told through the adult child who gets the call). The hardest moment isn't October: it's where the parent goes next. In June 2024 Advantage insurers denied 12% of requests for a skilled-nursing stay, almost two-thirds for a long-term-care hospital and more than half for inpatient rehab (log 93, 104). Of the nursing-home denials, 82% were never appealed; of the few that were, 95% were reversed, mostly at one insurer (log 95–98). A parent already living in a nursing home was denied skilled-nursing care 40% of the time, against 11% for other enrollees (log 101). The other side, at full strength: Original Medicare uses prior authorization too (log 126–128), and fewer nursing-facility days in Advantage came with no worse readmission or mortality (log 215, 216). Their short answer, "am I trapped?": partly. From January 1 to March 31 an Advantage member can make one change, including back to Original Medicare with a drug plan (log 479, 480), but that window carries no Medigap guarantee of its own (log 481); three in four Advantage enrollees have a condition that could get them refused, outside four states (log 284, 273). If a doctor leaves the plan mid-year, the plan must give notice (30 days for specialists, 45 for primary care and behavioral health, log 488), but a switch window opens only if CMS judges the change significant, case by case (log 489, 490). If it comes to hospice, Original Medicare pays for it even for someone who stays in the plan (log 491, 492).
  • 3. The letter: the plan is ending. A plan that won't renew must mail a notice at least 90 days ahead (log 259). From December 8 to the last day of February they can pick another plan; if they pick nothing they land in Original Medicare (log 254, 258). Their short answer: two clocks. A guaranteed right to a Medigap policy runs to March 4, 2027 for a plan ending December 31, but only if they go to Original Medicare (log 267–269; the date is our arithmetic, 63 days after the plan ends). It bars pricing by health, not higher prices in general (log 266).
  • 4. The person who can't afford a supplement (his Ruling 2; top-up G1). Their short answer: check whether you qualify for help first. The fullest Medicare Savings Program (QMB) pays the Part B premium and Medicare's deductibles, coinsurance and copays, and Medicare providers can't bill you for them, on either route (log 494, 495); in 2026 the federal limit for one person is about $1,350 a month in income, with a resource test, and your state can be more generous (log 494, 498). Two smaller programs pay the Part B premium (log 496, 497). Extra Help with drugs means a $0 drug-plan premium and deductible in 2027 and small copays (log 499). The gap is real but dated: in 2018–2020 about 2 in 5 eligible people weren't enrolled in a Savings Program (log 503), and in 2020 about 3.6 million people potentially eligible for Extra Help weren't getting it (log 504, an estimate from before the 2024 expansion). For people on both Medicare and Medicaid, nearly 12.5 million in 2025 (log 500), Medigap generally can't be sold to them (log 316). The bundle at full strength here: people on both programs in plans built for them report somewhat better access and satisfaction than in Original Medicare, though not much better than in ordinary Advantage plans (log 506: satisfaction, not spending or health outcomes); eligible people in Advantage were more often enrolled in a Savings Program (61% against 53%, a snapshot, not proof the plan caused it, log 505); and for low-income people not on Medicaid, an analysis paid for by the insurers' alliance found 12% of Advantage enrollees against 24% in fee-for-service spent over a fifth of their income on care, in an Advantage group that was poorer (log 507, 15–17). ⛔ Never "pick Advantage because you can't afford Medigap": that is advice, and the help programs may change the sum. What a supplement costs as a share of a low income is unresolved and off air (log 508).

Why the answers differ: the money. The bundle can be the better deal for you because Medicare pays more for it: MedPAC puts it at about 14% more than the same people would cost in Original Medicare, about $76 billion in 2026, and the Congressional Budget Office, measuring a different way, at roughly 15% for a person who switches: a similar size, a different yardstick (log 327, 402). That money funds the rebates that pay for the extras (log 322, 354), and everyone on Part B pays about $14.61 a month more for it, in a plan or not (log 348).

The question

Is Medicare Advantage a better deal for you than Original Medicare, and who pays for the extras? Under it sits the question the record forces: better for whom, and in which year: the year you're well, or the year you need serious care and want to change your mind?

Underneath, insurers are pulling out of places. Why it fits the thesis, not just the subject: the exits are the question in miniature. The way back to Original Medicare runs through Medigap, and the guaranteed right to buy a policy without health questions is tied to exactly this event (log 266–269). Settled (not the debate)

The two routes

  • Original Medicare has no yearly limit on what you pay for hospital and doctor care "unless you have supplemental coverage", and is the only major form of insurance without one (log 31, 32). With no supplement in 2026 you pay a $1,736 hospital deductible per benefit period (it can recur within a year) and, after a $283 deductible, usually 20% of doctor bills (log 40).
  • Most people in Original Medicare have a supplement: 87% in 2023 (Medigap 43%, employer 29%, Medicaid 14%); 3.5 million (13%) had none (log 29, 30). A Medigap Plan G covers all Part A and B cost sharing except the Part B deductible, and none of drugs, dental, vision or hearing (log 39). Plan G averaged $194 a month in 2025 for existing policyholders ($164 in New Mexico to $257 in New York), rose 13% in one year and 38% over five (log 48, 49).
  • Advantage plans must cap in-network out-of-pocket costs: at most $9,850 in 2027 ($14,800 in- and out-of-network combined for PPOs) (log 34, 35). The typical plan is far lower: $5,421 on average in 2026, for individual plans (log 36). About 9% of enrollees are in plans at the maximum (log 37). Nobody publishes how many people actually reach their cap (log 38). The cap covers Part A and Part B services only; drugs have their own separate cap, so 2027's two ceilings are $9,850 + $2,400 = $12,250 in network, before premiums, out-of-network care in an HMO and extras (log 474–478; our arithmetic). Never "the most you can pay".
  • Premiums: 75% of people in individual Advantage drug plans pay no premium beyond Part B (2026; $15 a month on average, $59 among those who pay one) (log 47). Both routes pay the Part B premium: $202.90 a month in 2026; 2027 is not yet announced, and the Medicare trustees project about $209.50 (log 45, 46). A stand-alone drug plan is projected to average about $36 a month in 2027 (log 43).
  • Drug costs are capped on both routes: $2,100 in 2026, rising to $2,400 in 2027, deductible included (log 55–57).

The extras

  • Nearly every individual Advantage plan offers some vision (99%), dental (98%) and hearing (98%) coverage in 2026; two in three offer an over-the-counter allowance, down from three in four (log 59, 60). Original Medicare does not cover hearing aids or the exams to fit them (log 68).
  • Use: roughly 40–60% of Advantage enrollees use dental services in a year, and about 1 in 12 use hearing services (log 61, 65). For the newer extras (OTC cards, meals, transport) Medicare's data are too poor to measure use at all, and MedPAC did not try (log 70, 71).

Asking permission

  • Virtually all Advantage enrollees (99%) need prior authorization for some services; there were 1.7 requests per enrollee in 2024, 52.8 million in all, and 7.7% were denied in full or part (log 109, 110, 113, 114). Of denials, about 1 in 9 were appealed and 8 in 10 of those appeals won (log 111, 112).
  • Original Medicare uses prior authorization too, for a short list of services: just over 625,000 reviews in fiscal 2024, about 2 per 100 people, and 22.9% were denied (log 126–128). From 2026 a CMS model called WISeR adds it in six states (New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas, Arizona, Washington) through 2031, run by vendors paid a share of the spending they avert (log 130, 131).
  • In 2019, of a sample of denials by 15 large insurers, an estimated 13% met Medicare's coverage rules and "likely would have been approved" in Original Medicare (log 81, 87, 88). That predates a 2024 rule limiting the plans' own criteria (log 133, 134).

Getting out

  • At 65 the door is open once. The federal Medigap open enrollment period lasts six months from the first month you have Part B and are 65 or older; it happens once and can't be moved or repeated (log 449, 451, 452, 456). Inside it an insurer can't refuse you or charge more for your health; prices still vary by age, smoking, sex and area, and a pre-existing condition can be excluded for up to six months without prior coverage (log 450, 453–455).
  • Federal law guarantees a Medigap policy without health questions only in narrow windows: a 12-month "trial right" in two cases (you dropped Medigap to try your first Advantage plan; or you joined Advantage when you first got Medicare at 65), and when your plan ends or leaves your area (log 261–266). The right applies only if you go to Original Medicare (log 269). Outside those windows a Medigap insurer may refuse you or charge more (log 272, 283).
  • Only four states (Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine, New York) require continuous or yearly guaranteed issue for everyone 65 and older; four more (Delaware, Illinois, Minnesota, Rhode Island) have narrower protections (log 273, 276).
  • 74% of people in Advantage for all of 2023, about 20.9 million, had a condition that could trigger a Medigap denial (log 284). That is exposure, not denials (log 286).

Who pays

  • MedPAC, Congress's Medicare advisers, estimates Medicare will spend 14% more for Advantage enrollees than it would for the same people in Original Medicare: about $76 billion in 2026 (log 327). It says this is not an estimate of plan profit (log 321).
  • The higher payments help fund the extras (log 322). Nearly every plan bids below its benchmark and keeps a share of the difference as a rebate, typically 65% or 70% (log 326, 356); conventional plans project spending their rebate about 35% on lower cost sharing, 22% on extra benefits like dental and vision, 26% on drug coverage, 9% on the Part B premium and 8% on administration and profit (log 354).
  • Everyone on Part B pays part of it: MedPAC estimates Part B premiums are about $11 billion higher in 2026 because of higher Advantage payments, about $14.61 a month per person, in Advantage or not (log 347, 348).

Contested (the real fight)

Side A — "Take the Advantage." (Medicare Advantage is the better deal for most people; the insurers, their trade groups and the studies that favour it.) Original Medicare has no ceiling on what a bad year costs you; every Advantage plan has one, $9,850 at most in 2027 and about $5,400 on average (log 31, 34, 36). Three in four Advantage enrollees pay no extra premium (log 47), against about $194 a month for a Plan G that rose 13% last year (log 48, 49). Against Original Medicare without a supplement, people who chose Advantage at 65 spent less at every level studied, and for people with a cancer history up to about $9,900 less in their worst years, and were less likely to skip medicine for cost (log 22, 23, 25). An analysis for the insurers' alliance found Advantage enrollees spent 36% less out of pocket than everyone in fee-for-service, premiums included, and half as often spent more than a fifth of their income on care (log 15–17). The extras are real: Original Medicare pays for no hearing aids, while 92% of conventional Advantage enrollees now have hearing-aid coverage, and for enrollees with plan dental coverage, the share of dental costs they paid themselves fell from 61% to 35% (log 67, 68, 63). On care: a review of 48 studies found Advantage better on preventive visits, hospital admissions and emergency visits (log 207); a 2025 study found 12.8–17.5% lower use of care with quality that is "better in MA" (log 85, 220); people in Advantage got 9.2% fewer low-value services (log 156); fewer days in nursing facilities came with no worse readmission or mortality (log 215, 216). Prior authorization is "one of the many tools health plans use to promote safe, timely, evidence-based, affordable, and efficient care" (log 155); the inspector general did not read the underlying records and cannot tell paperwork denials from medical ones (log 138, 139); the plans say they have cut prior authorization in Advantage by more than 15% since 2025 (log 143, 144). People choose it: 55% of eligible people are in Advantage, and in 2019–20, 6.8% of Original Medicare enrollees switched into Advantage against 2.0% going the other way (log 302, 306). Satisfaction is no different (log 310). On the money: plans bid to deliver the core benefit for less than Original Medicare spends, and MedPAC says its estimated gap is not profit (log 321, 325). The industry argues MedPAC overstates the selection part of it, and Humana's own study puts selection at 4.9–5.8% rather than about 11% (log 413–420).

Side B — "Keep the original." (Original Medicare with a supplement is the better deal, and the extras cost the public more than they're worth; MedPAC, CBO, the inspector general, Senate staff and the critics' studies.) Most people in Original Medicare have a supplement (log 29), and against that the best study of people who switched to Advantage found differences that were small and showed "no consistent pattern" (log 19, 80). In 2018, Original Medicare with a supplement had the fewest people reporting cost-related problems (12%, against 19% in Advantage), and among people in fair or poor health, 39% in Advantage against 31% in Original Medicare (log 26, 28). The cap is a ceiling, not a bill you never see (log 34, 37). The costs come when you're sick. In 2019 an estimated 13% of sampled denials met Medicare's rules (log 81, 82). In June 2024 insurers denied almost two-thirds of requests for long-term-care hospitals and more than half for inpatient rehab (log 104); 82% of nursing-facility denials were never appealed, and nobody knows what happened to those patients (log 97). Senate investigators found one insurer's post-acute denial rate went from 8.7% to 22.7% in three years while its overall rate barely moved (log 121, 122). The network can be wrong: on average 55% of the mental-health providers listed in Advantage directories in ten counties saw no Advantage patients in a year (log 182, 183); for cancer, Advantage enrollees used about 12% of the oncology practices that Original Medicare patients in the same county used (log 203). And the sick leave. In their last year of life, Advantage enrollees left for Original Medicare at more than twice the rate of others (log 288, 289); after a cancer diagnosis, switching rose only in the states that guarantee Medigap (log 296); Medicare then spent 27% more on people who left than on similar people who never joined (log 295). Most can't leave easily: three in four Advantage enrollees have a condition that could get them refused a Medigap policy (log 284). The extras are used no more than people in Original Medicare use dental, vision and hearing care, and Advantage enrollees still paid 57.9% of those costs themselves (log 72, 75); about half didn't know they had the coverage (log 74). And the public pays for them: 14% more, about $76 billion in 2026 (log 327); the Congressional Budget Office independently puts a switcher's extra cost at roughly 15% (log 402); everyone's Part B premium is about $14.61 a month higher (log 348); coding alone has added an estimated $233 billion since 2007 (log 365).

The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:

  • B at A: The cheaper comparisons are against people with no supplement, 13% of Original Medicare (log 22, 30). The 36% is an insurer-commissioned, observational comparison of different populations: the Advantage group was poorer and more often had Medicaid help, and "Advantage" meant one month in any managed-care plan (log 16, 17, 79). The $0 premium is paid for: the drug part of Advantage premiums falls from $11.32 to $7 in 2027 "after application of MA rebates" (log 44), and those rebates come from the payment MedPAC says is 14% higher (log 327, 349). The stars that sell plans are not a reliable measure of quality, says MedPAC (log 250), are set per contract, not per plan (log 234), and are in litigation (log 237, 248). The pledge is self-reported, and services can be added back (log 144, 145).

The reframe

The shouting compares two products and asks which is cheaper this year. The record says two things decide it instead.

  1. The enrollee's deal and the taxpayer's deal are the same money, seen from both ends. Medicare pays plans more than the same people would cost in Original Medicare (log 327); plans spend most of that rebate on lower cost sharing, extras and drug coverage (log 354); and everyone on Part B, including people who never join a plan, pays about $14.61 a month more for it (log 348). So Advantage can be a better deal for you precisely because it costs the public more.
  2. It's a choice about your future self, and it's easier to make than to unmake. The bundle is cheapest while you're well and your plan stays; its costs show up as permission and networks when you need serious care (log 84, 104, 203); the sick leave at more than twice the rate (log 288); and in most states the supplement you'd go back to can be priced on, or refused for, your health (log 283, 284).

So the live question is not "which is cheaper". It is: what are you paying for: a lower bill now, or an open door later, and who else is paying for your choice?

Both sides quietly agree

The plans can deliver the core benefit for less than Original Medicare spends; the fight is over the rest of the money. MedPAC: plan bids are 95% of what Original Medicare would spend after adjusting for coding and selection, and their medical costs alone about 18% lower (log 325, 342). A study commissioned by UnitedHealth puts Advantage payments at about 96% of fee-for-service (log 423, 425). MedPAC says the gap is not profit (log 321), and plans keep about 8% of conventional rebates for administration and profit (log 354). The real argument is how much of the extra is favorable selection (healthier-than-scored people choosing Advantage), and whether the public should pay for it at all (log 329, 413–420).

And both use prior authorization. CMS is adding it to Original Medicare in six states, through vendors paid on savings, the incentive critics name in Advantage (log 130, 131); the insurers pledged to cut it, and the binding rule now sets deadlines and requires reasons (log 160, 166, 167). The fight is how much, and where: after a hospital stay is where the record is sharpest (log 104, 121).

The fight is narrower than it sounds (our reading of the record, not a finding): no one in the record argues that a sick person can easily switch back outside four states; the industry's case is that Advantage serves the sick well, not that leaving is easy.

Practical facts a viewer can act on (exact, sourced)

  • Open enrollment: October 15 to December 7 every year; for 2027 coverage, October 15 to December 7, 2026 (log 256).
  • If your Advantage plan is not renewing: the plan must mail you at least 90 days before it ends (log 259) and tell you about your Medigap rights if you go to Original Medicare (log 260). You can join another Advantage or drug plan from December 8 to the last day of February (2027: February 28); a choice in December starts January 1, in January February 1, in February March 1 (log 254, 255, 257). If you choose nothing, you're placed in Original Medicare (log 258).
  • Medigap guaranteed issue when your plan ends: Medicare.gov says you can apply from 60 days before your coverage ends until 63 days after; the statute starts an involuntary termination's window on the date you receive the notice. For a plan ending December 31, 2026, that is March 4, 2027 (log 267, 268; our arithmetic). It applies only if you go to Original Medicare, not to another Advantage plan (log 269). It covers Plans A, B, C, D, F, G, K and L; C and F only if you turned 65 before January 1, 2020 (log 270, 271). It bars pricing by your health, not higher prices in general (log 266).
  • The trial right: if you joined Advantage when you first got Medicare at 65, you can leave within 12 months and buy any Medigap policy; if you dropped Medigap to try your first Advantage plan, you can go back within 12 months to the same policy if that insurer still sells it (log 261–265). It does not apply to people under 65 on Medicare (log 318). Maine stretches it to three years (log 280).
  • State rules: Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine and New York require continuous or yearly guaranteed issue for everyone 65+ (Maine's yearly window guarantees at least Plan A; Massachusetts requires February–March, though all its insurers sell year-round) (log 273–275). Rhode Island's 2025 law gives Advantage enrollees a yearly window during October 15 – December 7 (log 277); Delaware's 2025 windows can't refuse you but can price by health and impose a 6-month wait (log 278). Illinois's year-round access is one insurer's practice, not law (log 279). "Birthday rules" in sixteen states let existing Medigap holders switch to equal or lesser coverage; they are no route back from Advantage (log 281).
  • The Medigap window at 65: six months, starting the first month you have Part B and are 65 or older; once only, set by your Part B sign-up (log 449, 451, 452, 456). Inside it no refusal and no pricing by health; price can still vary by age, smoking, sex and area (log 450, 453, 455).
  • January 1 to March 31 (the Advantage open enrollment period): someone already in an Advantage plan can make one change, to another plan or back to Original Medicare with a drug plan; it can't be used to join Advantage from Original Medicare (log 479, 480). It gives no Medigap guarantee of its own (log 481).
  • If your doctor leaves your plan mid-year: the plan must give written notice, at least 30 days ahead for specialists you see regularly and 45 days for primary care and behavioral health (log 488). A special window to switch opens only if CMS judges the network change significant, case by case (log 489, 490).
  • Hospice: once hospice starts, Original Medicare covers the terminal illness even if you stay in your Advantage plan (log 491, 492).
  • Outside a window: a Medigap insurer may refuse you or charge more (log 283). In one industry survey, a third of 2024 applications were underwritten and 15% of those were declined (log 287).
  • Costs on both routes: Part B $202.90 a month in 2026; 2027 not yet announced: SSA says the amount reaches people from late November, and the trustees estimate $209.50 (log 45, 459, 464). Part B deductible $283 in 2026, about $292 estimated for 2027; Plan G doesn't pay it (log 462, 465, 470). Drug costs capped at $2,400 in 2027 on both routes (log 55, 468). The Advantage in-network cap is at most $9,850 in 2027, for Part A and B services only; plans may set it lower (log 34, 33, 474–477).
  • If you have Medicaid too: Medigap generally can't be sold to you; the Medigap advice above doesn't apply (log 316).
  • Help with costs: the Medicare Savings Programs (QMB pays the Part B premium and Medicare's cost sharing; SLMB and QI pay the Part B premium) and Extra Help with drugs; 2026 federal limits, applied and sometimes raised by your state, with a resource test (log 494–499).
  • Networks: the rules require at least one provider of each listed type within a time and distance limit for 90% of enrollees (85% in rural counties), never a particular hospital or cancer center (log 194–196); dialysis clinics have no distance test (log 198). The directory rule is a proposal for 2028, not this enrollment (log 170, 177).
  • Star ratings are set per contract, not per plan, and the 2027 ratings are being challenged in court (log 234, 237).
