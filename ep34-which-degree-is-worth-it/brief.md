## The pitch, in one line

**A four-year degree costs a family about $60,000 at a state school and about $105,000 at a private one,
after grants; what it pays back depends on the field you pick and on whether you finish; and the biggest
line on a state-school bill is housing and food, not tuition.** Is it worth what it really costs, for you,
and how much risk are you willing to take?

## The viewer's moment

Two people, this fall. (Invented and unnamed; never a real or identifiable person.)

- **A family looking at an aid offer.** After grants, a typical in-state public university still asks
  about **$15,000 a year**, a typical private nonprofit about **$26,500**: four years, roughly
  **$61,000 or $106,000** (log 17.2, 17.3). The student can borrow **$31,000** in federal loans in total
  (log 17.9). The rest comes from savings, work, a parent's PLUS loan or a private lender, and about
  two-thirds of the public-university bill is housing and food, not tuition (log 22.24).
- **A student who left after two years**, one of about three in ten starters who leave with no
  credential (log 11.2), repaying loans for a degree they don't have: 45% of borrowers who left with no
  credential defaulted within 12 years, against 8% of bachelor's finishers (log 12.9), and only 28% of
  non-finishers say college was worth it (log 22.26).

And the field question, through one example: a student who wants to teach. A needed job, about **$47,000**
four years out (the national median for teacher-education graduates, log 8.1), and a degree that costs
about **1.3 years of that pay** at a public school, **1.9** at a private one (log 17.15).

The episode follows the family's bill through the settings of the risk dial and asks, at each, what it
costs, what it pays, and the odds of finishing. (The teacher's repayment and "who should carry the risk"
are the companion episode's story.)

## Why now

The 2027–28 FAFSA opened on **2026-09-23**, the earliest launch in the program's history for the second
year running
([ED, 2026-09-23](https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-department-of-education-announces-earliest-fafsa-launch-program-history-second-consecutive-year)).
Since last cycle (December 2025), a first-year applicant can see a **"lower earnings"** notice for any
school on their list whose graduates earn less, four years out, than typical high-school graduates in
that state (nationally, if the school serves mostly out-of-state students). It is **per school**, it is
built from **federal-aid completers of 2017–18 and 2018–19** with earnings measured in 2022 and 2023,
and it is information only: it changes no aid
([FSA announcement, updated 2026-03-23](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/knowledge-center/library/electronic-announcements/2025-12-03/new-lower-earnings-indicator-fafsar-form);
[ED blog](https://www.ed.gov/about/homeroom-blog/introducing-new-earnings-indicator-fafsar-form)).
ED's September launch release lists it among the features introduced last cycle; **no ED text we read
says the 2027–28 Submission Summary displays it**, or which data year it uses. ED says fewer than 3% of
undergraduates attend such a school, and that **about 25% of students shown the flag dropped that school**
and picked another
([ED, 2026-03-26](https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-department-of-education-celebrates-more-10-million-fafsar-forms-complete-and-additional-transparency-measures);
ED's own claim, unaudited).

Next year a **similar** bar, not the same one, gets teeth. Under a final rule published 2026-07-01 and
effective **2027-07-01**, an **undergraduate program** fails if its graduates' median earnings, in the
fourth tax year after finishing, are **less than** those of **working adults aged 25–34 with only a
high-school diploma** in the school's state (or nationally). A program that fails in **two of any three**
years loses federal **Direct Loans**; ED's analysis puts the first sanction year in the award year
starting **July 2028**
([Federal Register 2026-13286](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-01/pdf/2026-13286.pdf)).
ED projects, in the final rule's Table 5.12, that **1.1% of bachelor's programs** (0.6% of their
students) fail, against **6.0%** of associate programs and **24.1% of undergraduate certificates**
(27.6% of certificate students); **5.2%** of all programs. At the institution level, a school with half
its aid recipients or aid in failing programs can lose all federal aid for those programs unless it
bars loans in them, and ED's Table 5.11 has **43% of for-profit institutions** failing that test (both
tables typed by hand from ED's images; re-read the images before airing a table figure). ED says it
will publish the program-level data in **2027**
([FSA, GENERAL-26-49](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/knowledge-center/library/electronic-announcements/2026-08-11/guidance-fvt/ge-data-reporting-stats-early-implementation-and-next-steps-publication-updated-aug-28-2026)).

The flag and the rule are **cousins**: one per school against "typical high school graduates", one per
program against a defined Census group aged 25–34. Never say they are the same bar, and never say 25–34
about the flag.

And the public mood has moved: a record-low **31%** of Americans call a college education "very
important", down from 70% in 2013
([Gallup, 2026-10-01](https://news.gallup.com/poll/715133/record-low-say-college-education-important.aspx);
an opinion poll, n=1,200, ±4 points).

**So Washington has written down a version of the episode's answer, and its bar is "beat a high-school
diploma".** It does not subtract the price you pay. It does not count the students who don't finish. At
the bachelor's level it fails about one program in ninety. That gap is the episode.

- **Description (hot):** the 2027–28 FAFSA is open; its "lower earnings" flag; one line on the 2027
  rule. Keywords: `2027-28 FAFSA`, `lower earnings`. Optionally Gallup's 31%, labelled as a poll.
- **Script (structural):** "The form you're filling in this fall can warn you when a college's graduates
  earn less than a typical high-school graduate. From 2027, a program whose graduates fall below a
  similar bar two years in three loses federal loans. That's the government's test. It isn't yours."
- **Never** the title or thumbnail.

### TOTAL COST — what a four-year degree really costs (ruling 4; top-up D2) [BOTH]

⛔ **The coordinator's federal-only debt table (a coordinator working table, unpublished, "bachelor's federal
debt ~$23k whatever the field") is SUPERSEDED.** Federal undergraduate debt is capped by law, so it hides
what families pay or borrow on top. Never cite it; never build a conclusion on it (log 17.20, 20.1).

- **What four years cost after grants** (ED's College Scorecard institution file, our arithmetic; net
  price = full cost of attendance, including tuition, books and living costs, minus all grants, for
  first-time full-time students who get federal aid, in-state at publics, academic year 2023–24;
  "typical" = enrollment-weighted median across bachelor's-granting schools) (log 17.1–17.3, 17.5):

  | sector | net price a year (middle half) | four years | sticker cost a year |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Public, in-state | **$15,364** ($12,397–$18,749) | **$61,456** ($49.6k–$75.0k) | $26,718 |
  | Private nonprofit | **$26,505** ($20,011–$35,639) | **$106,020** ($80.0k–$142.6k) | $54,630 |
  | Private for-profit | $17,833 | $71,332 | $23,744 |

  By family income (per year → four years): public in-state **$10,126 → $40,504** under $30k income,
  up to **$22,742 → $90,968** above $110k; private nonprofit **$18,557 → $74,228** up to **$33,731 →
  $134,924** (log 17.5). Out-of-state public (~$30,700 a year) is an approximation: off air as a figure
  (log 17.4). Only about half finish in four years (49.1%, 2017 entry, same school), so many pay a
  fifth year (log 17.6). Spoken: "about $15,000 a year after grants at a state school, about $26,000 at
  a private one: roughly $60,000 or $105,000 for four years."
- **⚠ RECONCILE the two net-price numbers. They are not in conflict; they measure different people in
  different years.**
  - **Scorecard $15,364** (log 17.2): the **median** across schools of the net price paid by
    **first-time full-time students who receive federal aid**, in-state, **2023–24** actual data. It
    leaves out students with no federal aid (often better-off families, who get fewer grants and pay
    more), and its living-cost allowance is each school's own.
  - **College Board $21,340** (log 9.4): an **average** full net cost for **all** first-time full-time
    in-state students at public four-years, **2025–26**, a **projection** (grant data end in 2022–23),
    with housing, food, books, transport and other costs built in.
  - **Use:** the **Scorecard** figures for **every comparison** (by sector, by family income, by field,
    and every cost-÷-pay ratio), because they come from the same ED files as the field pay figures and
    the same aided population. The **College Board** figures for the **one-year "what college costs
    now" beat** and for **what the bill is made of** (housing and food $13,900 of $21,340, log 22.24).
    Say which one is on screen; **never put them on one chart, and never mix them in one sum or ratio**
    (e.g. never "$21,340 × 4 ÷ teacher pay"). NCES's $15,200 (2021–22, aid recipients, log 9.11) is the
    same kind of measure as Scorecard's and older: do not air it beside either.
- **What federal loans can cover:** a dependent undergraduate can borrow at most **$31,000** in federal
  student loans in total, unchanged by the 2025 law (log 17.9). On a typical public degree **about
  $30,000** must come from somewhere else; at a typical private nonprofit **about $75,000** (our
  arithmetic, see the log's derivations). That gap is why federal debt understates the burden.
- **How families cover the gap** (Sallie Mae/Ipsos 2025; ⚠ Sallie Mae is the largest private student
  lender, so attribute; survey of 1,000 parents and 1,000 students): families spent **$30,837** in
  2024–25; **48%** from income and savings, 27% grants and scholarships, **23% borrowed** (12% student,
  11% parent) (log 17.10). **48% of families borrowed**: 27% federal student loans, **11% Parent PLUS**,
  **12% private student loans**, 11% parent credit cards; **17% withdrew retirement savings** (log
  17.11). 35% of borrowing families say loan access made them consider pricier schools (self-report,
  log 17.12). Private (nonfederal) loans: about **$13.9 billion** a year, ~14% of education lending
  (College Board estimate, log 17.13). College Board: 47% of 2023–24 graduates borrowed, averaging
  $27,420 (public) and $34,420 (private nonprofit), **Parent PLUS excluded** (log 17.8).
- **Beyond the cap:** Parent PLUS is now $20,000 a year and $65,000 total per student (from
  2026-07-01, log 4.1, 4.2); private loans have no federal limit and no federal forgiveness (log 19.11).
- **Total cost vs pay, by field** (four-year net cost at the schools that grant the field ÷ ED's median
  pay four years out; our arithmetic) (log 17.15–17.19):

  | field | pay 4 yrs out | public: 4-yr cost · ÷ pay | private nonprofit: 4-yr cost · ÷ pay |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Teacher ed (specific levels) | $47,382 | $59,868 · **1.26** | $89,888 · **1.90** |
  | Psychology | $50,706 | $59,868 · 1.18 | $111,384 · 2.20 |
  | Social work | $51,790 | $59,556 · 1.15 | $89,888 · 1.74 |
  | Ethnic/cultural/gender/group studies | $51,710 | $57,004 · 1.10 | $110,316 · 2.13 |
  | Drama/theatre | $39,775 | $61,456 · **1.55** | $123,568 · **3.11** |
  | Fine/studio arts | $41,367 | $59,556 · 1.44 | $117,864 · 2.85 |
  | Music | $42,892 | $61,128 · 1.43 | $117,112 · 2.73 |
  | Criminal justice | $55,378 | $54,228 · 0.98 | $92,824 · 1.68 |
  | Business administration | $68,257 | $57,104 · 0.84 | $98,616 · 1.44 |
  | Registered nursing | $88,910 | $57,148 · **0.64** | $89,888 · 1.01 |
  | Computer science | $107,009 | $59,868 · **0.56** | $109,160 · 1.02 |

  **Reading:** the price is roughly the same whatever the major (~$55–64k public, ~$89–124k private);
  **pay is what moves**, and the school type multiplies it. Drama at a private nonprofit is about three
  years of pay; teacher ed at a public about 1.3; computer science at a public about half a year.
  Earnings are federal-aid recipients who work and are not enrolled, four years out, not a lifetime
  return; net price is held flat across four years (no tuition growth).

### HOW GRADUATES GET OUT (ruling 5; top-up D1) [BOTH]

- **PSLF:** what is left of a **Direct Loan** is cancelled, tax-free, after **120 qualifying payments**
  while working full time in public service; public-school teaching and public child/family social
  work are named (log 19.4, 19.10). RAP payments count (log 19.5). The early program approved almost no
  one (TEPSLF approved 1%, 661 of ~54,000, log 19.6); **now about 1.29 million borrowers have had $96.4
  billion discharged** through August 2026 (log 19.7; re-read the sheet rows before airing). From
  2026-07-01 ED can disqualify employers with a "substantial illegal purpose" (log 19.8; lawsuit not
  read).
- **Teacher Loan Forgiveness:** after **5 consecutive years in a low-income school**, up to **$5,000**,
  or **$17,500** for secondary math/science or special education (log 19.1); about **13,800** borrowers
  got it in FY2025, down from ~45,000 in FY2018 (log 19.2). The same years cannot count for both TLF and
  PSLF (log 19.3).
- **RAP / income-driven forgiveness:** RAP forgives after **360 payments (30 years)**; old IDR plans
  forgave after 20 or 25; legacy borrowers must move by 2028-07-01 (log 19.9). ⚠ **IDR/RAP forgiveness
  granted from 2026 is federally taxable** (plain reading of the statute, log 19.10).
- **Bankruptcy:** federal and private student loans survive bankruptcy unless repaying is an "undue
  hardship" (log 19.11). Since November 2022 a DOJ/ED attestation lets government lawyers agree to
  discharge; in the first 10 months 632 cases were filed, and in **99% of the cases a court had ruled
  on** the government recommended and the court granted full or partial discharge (of decided cases,
  not of filers; log 19.12–19.13).
- **Parent PLUS:** a parent borrowing on or after 2026-07-01 gets only a Tiered Standard plan, **no
  PSLF**; older Parent PLUS keeps a PSLF route only through consolidation before 2026-07-01 plus an IDR
  plan by 2028-07-01, lost on any new Direct Loan; PSLF turns on the **parent's** job (log 19.14, 4.10).
- **Worked case: a teacher on $47,000 who borrowed about $80,000** (hypothetical; federal + parent/
  private). As a dependent student they could borrow at most **$31,000** themselves; the other
  ~**$49,000** is Parent PLUS (their parent's debt) or private loans. Their own Direct Loans have an exit:
  **PSLF** after ten years in a public school, tax-free; on RAP at $47,000 their payment would be about
  **$157 a month** before the $50-per-dependent credit (our arithmetic on RAP's 4% bracket, flat pay; not
  an ED example) (log 19.4, 19.5, 4.6). TLF needs five years in a low-income school and those years
  can't also count for PSLF. **The parent's PLUS loan has an exit only through the parent's own
  public-service job, and only on the narrow legacy path; the private loans have no federal exit
  except bankruptcy's undue-hardship test.** One line: **the federal part has an exit; the parent and
  private parts mostly don't.**

### GRADUATES VS JOBS — one yardstick for every field (ruling 6; top-up D4) [BOTH]

- **The rule** (applied to all 73 NY Fed majors, ACS 2024, ages 22–27, released February 2026): a field
  **falls short** if its recent graduates are **more often underemployed than the all-majors 39.4%** AND
  have a **lower early-career median than the all-majors $58,000** (log 21.8, 21.12). It is relative, so
  about half fall short by construction: read it as "worse than the typical graduate on both counts".
- **34 of 73 majors fall short on both, 17 on one, 22 pass** (our arithmetic, APPROXIMATE, log 21.12).
- **The worst on underemployment: criminal justice (65.8%), the performing arts (63.9%), then fine arts
  (58.9%)** (log 21.10). Criminal justice is also worst on Strada's five-year measure (68%, log 21.23).
- **The fields Lucas named sit mid-pack among those that fall short:** ethnic studies 49.6%
  underemployed, $52k; sociology 52.0%, $49.9k; psychology 48.3%, $45k; English 48.5%, $48k; history
  50.1%, $47.5k; philosophy 47.1%, $52k (log 21.9), **beside business management (52.6%, $56k),
  communications, biology and political science**. Gender studies has no NY Fed row of its own (log
  21.32); ED pools it with ethnic studies at **$51,710**, the same as social work and psychology (log
  21.17). Ethnic studies' mid-career median ($90k) is above the all-majors $87k (log 21.15).
- **Sizes:** gender + ethnic studies together are about **2,500 degrees a year** (~0.13% of
  bachelor's); criminal justice graduates **~18×** as many, business (excl. accounting) **~131×**
  (IPEDS via Urban, 2022–23, APPROXIMATE, log 21.4, 21.6). The big numbers of graduates in jobs that
  don't need the degree come from the big majors: business, psychology, communications, criminal justice.
- **The problem is underemployment, not unemployment.** Recent-grad unemployment by major runs only
  0.7–7.9%; computer science (7.0%) and computer engineering (7.8%) are among the highest (log 21.14).
  73% of those who start underemployed still are ten years on (Strada, log 21.24).
- **"Necessary" degrees are not all well paid.** Teaching and social work land jobs (16–30%
  underemployed) at below-average pay ($43–45k early) (log 21.11); BLS projects about 227,900 K–12
  teacher openings a year (log 21.27). Fields that pass: nursing, accounting, computer science,
  engineering (log 21.11).

### NON-MONEY VALUE AND THE "EXPERIENCE" (ruling 2; top-up D3) [PERSONAL]

- **Associations, mostly, not causes.** Graduates report being happy more often (5 points over
  high-school graduates; about 2 points at the same income), have more rewarding jobs, vote and
  volunteer more (log 22.2–22.3); the BA / no-BA gap in expected years of life between 25 and 75 rose
  from 1.9 to 3.1 years, 1990–2018 (log 22.11). Case and Deaton "take no position" on whether a degree
  causes health (log 22.12). **For college itself the causal record is thin:** one mortality study
  (Vietnam-draft men, ~28% lower cumulative mortality, log 22.8) and one voting study (+21–30 points,
  contested; the critiques were not read, log 22.15, 22.18). Reviewers: whether health gains beyond
  high school are causal "is not known" (2006); the effect "exists in some contexts but not in others"
  (2018) (log 22.5, 22.9). Nothing shows the field studied changes the health benefit (log 22.6).
  Script line: "Graduates live longer, vote more and say they're happier. How much of that the degree
  causes is still argued; the best studies say some of it, for some people."
- **What the "experience" costs.** At a public in-state university, **housing and food are $13,900 of a
  $21,340 net cost, about two-thirds**; net tuition is $2,300 (College Board 2025–26, log 22.24); at a
  private nonprofit about 43%. ⚠ A student who skipped college would still pay rent and food: the true
  price of "the experience" is campus living over living at home, which **no source measures**. On the
  school side, instruction is about a third of core spending at public four-years (about a quarter of
  total spending, our arithmetic; the rest includes research and hospitals, not only amenities) (log
  22.22); the average school spent **51 cents on amenities per academic dollar** in 2007 (log 22.20);
  students reward amenities, but student demand explains only 16% of how schools split spending (log
  22.21).
- **Regret (Fed SHED 2023, the latest asking):** 68% of bachelor's holders say the benefits beat the
  costs, 43% of associate holders, **28% of those who didn't finish** (log 22.26); 44% of degree holders
  still paying loans vs 68% who paid off or never borrowed (log 22.27). **35% would choose a different
  field** (engineering 27%, humanities/arts 43%, social/behavioral sciences 44%) (log 22.29); the common
  regret is too **little** education (45%), not too much (10%) (log 22.28). **In every field more people
  say "worth it" than "not worth it"** (log 22.30): the fair answer to "they complain".
- **Why students choose low-paying fields: mostly taste.** Enjoyment, grades and interest beat expected
  pay in every study read (log 22.34, 22.36); beliefs about pay are often wrong, worst among low-income
  students aimed at low-paying programs (over 100% too high in Chile, log 22.37). Real numbers move
  choices only a little (4.6% in Chile; ~7.5% would switch at Duke with perfect forecasts, log 22.37,
  22.38). So missing information is part of the answer; "they wanted to study it" is most of it.
- **The people who need low-pay degrees:** high-school teachers median $72,040, ~63,500 openings a year;
  K–elementary $62,680–$63,970, ~99,400 openings; social workers $61,780, ~69,100 openings (BLS, all
  ages, May 2025; log 22.40–22.42); teachers earn about 27% less per week than comparable graduates (EPI,
  a labor-aligned think tank; log 22.43). ⚠ BLS medians are all ages; ED's $47k is four years out.

### The earlier settled record

**On average, a bachelor's pays** [PERSONAL]
- **Raw, a bachelor's holder earns about 60% more than a high-school graduate.** BLS 2025: median
  weekly earnings **$1,578 vs $966**, unemployment 2.8% vs 4.3% (age 25+, full-time, bachelor's only)
  ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/emp/tables/unemployment-earnings-education.htm)). NCES 2024, full-time
  workers aged 25–34: **$74,480 vs $46,380**
  ([NCES Digest 502.30](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d25/tables/dt25_502.30.asp)). Not the SF Fed's
  75%, which includes graduate degrees.
- **The best causal studies still find real gains, for students at the margin of getting in.** Florida:
  about **22%** higher earnings 8–14 years out for marginal admits, close to zero for women and better-off
  students ([Zimmerman, JOLE 2014](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/676661)). Georgia: about
  **17%** higher estimated household income at 30, marginally insignificant, about 27% for students from
  low-income high schools ([NBER w27177](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27177)). In these studies the
  alternative was **mostly a two-year college, not "no college"**, and rigorous degree-vs-no-degree
  studies are few ([Lovenheim and Smith, NBER w29933](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29933)). Ability bias
  does not erase the return to schooling ([Card, Econometrica 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00237)).
- **Prestige is mostly who gets in.** A more selective college adds little for the average student,
  more for disadvantaged students ([Dale and Krueger, NBER w7322](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7322),
  [w17159](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17159)); the selectivity premium fades within a few years
  ([Mountjoy and Hickman, NBER w29276](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29276)).
- **There is no US causal estimate for the typical enrollee.** None was found (log row 7.11).

**Field is the biggest money dial** [PERSONAL]

### WHY IT'S ALLOWED (ruling 1; top-up D1) [SYSTEM]

- **The lender is Washington, and the law tells it to lend to every eligible student** at an approved
  school: Direct Loans are funded with "such sums as may be necessary" (20 USC 1087a; log 18.1–18.2).
  Undergraduate loan size depends on **class year and dependency**, never on field, price or likely pay
  (log 18.3–18.4). The only credit screen is PLUS's "adverse credit history" check (log 18.6,
  APPROXIMATE as "the only"). Schools are approved by accreditors and states, not on outcomes (log 18.8,
  APPROXIMATE). There is **no federal cap on what a college charges**; price is disclosure-only in the
  2027 rule (log 18.35, APPROXIMATE as an absence).
- **The caps leaked through parents and grad school:** until 2026-07-01, Parent PLUS and Grad PLUS were
  limited only by the school's cost of attendance (log 18.5); now Parent PLUS is $20k/$65k and Grad
  PLUS is ended (log 18.7).
- **Every attempt to tie loans to results was narrow, struck down or repealed.** 2011 rule vacated in
  court in 2012 (log 18.9); 2014 rule rescinded (published 2019, effective 2020) after one year of rates,
  with **no program ever losing aid** though 2,050 were failing or in the zone (log 18.10–18.12); 2023
  rule's loss of aid covered only gainful-employment programs, mostly for-profits and certificates
  (log 18.13); 90/10 applies to for-profits only (log 18.15); cohort-default sanctions hit a handful of
  schools a year, most winning appeal (12 sanctioned for FY2017, 10 won, log 18.16–18.17, APPROXIMATE),
  and were gamed by pushing borrowers into forbearance (GAO, log 18.18). The 2025 law put an
  **earnings-only** test in statute for all programs and dropped the debt test (log 18.14).
- **Who pays when loans aren't repaid: the borrower and the taxpayer, never the school.** CBO: about **5
  cents per dollar lent** on the official basis, about **17 cents** on a fair-value basis, for 2027
  (log 18.30); losses sit in subsidized undergraduate loans and income-driven repayment, while **Parent
  PLUS makes money for the government** (log 18.31). GAO: 25 years of Direct Loans first booked as $114
  billion income were by FY2021 estimated to cost $197 billion (log 18.32, pre-RAP). The 2025 law moved
  the estimate back by $50 billion, mainly through RAP (log 18.33). A "share never repaid" is UNRESOLVED
  (log 18.34).
- **The 2025 law and the dropped House risk-sharing.** The House-passed bill made every school repay part
  of its students' unpaid loans from 2028–29 (100% where completers' value-added earnings were negative)
  (log 18.19); the Senate swapped it for the earnings test and the enacted law has no such section (log
  18.20). CBO's score "more than $6 billion" reaches us only through NPR (log 18.21, APPROXIMATE).
- **Does cutting off aid change behaviour?** In the 1990s, sanctioned for-profits lost ~40% of
  enrolment, community colleges took many of those students, and total enrolment fell just 3% (NBER
  w22967, 2016 version, log 18.38).
- **The steelman both ways** is under "Two framings" (DEBATE), log 18.22–18.29.

### WHERE THE DEBT THAT NEVER GETS PAID BACK SITS (top-up D2) [SYSTEM]

- **Bachelor's federal student debt is not where it concentrates:** 57 of 21,801 bachelor's programs
  (**0.3%**) have median federal debt above their graduates' median pay, against **534 of 6,764
  master's programs (7.9%)** (graduates who borrowed, federal only, our arithmetic, log 20.5). On a
  GE-style yearly test, 5.4% vs **34.8%** (APPROXIMATE, not ED's calculation, log 20.6). Master's over
  1× pay cluster in private schools (nonprofit 13.5%, for-profit 14.1%, public 2.3%) and in counselling,
  therapy and arts fields (log 20.9); master's drama debt is about **2×** pay (log 20.7).
- **Parent PLUS is the invisible part:** $115 billion outstanding for 3.5 million students (log 20.11);
  ED publishes no Parent PLUS default rate and schools face no consequence (Brookings, APPROXIMATE, log
  20.12); drama parents borrow a median ~**$62,000** (26% of graduates' parents) (log 20.4); at **7.1% of
  bachelor's programs** the parent's PLUS median alone tops the graduate's pay (log 20.10).
- **Graduate students take ~48% of new federal loans** while being ~16–17% of students (log 20.14);
  **49% of outstanding federal debt is held by the 11% who owe $80,000+** (log 20.15).
- **Non-completers are the largest group:** 45% default vs 8% of bachelor's finishers (log 12.9); they
  pay one or more years of the costs above with no degree.
- **CBO forgiveness shares (⚠ DATED, February 2020, before SAVE and before RAP):** of loans on
  income-driven plans, ~**21%** of undergraduate and ~**56%** of graduate dollars were projected to be
  forgiven, Parent PLUS excluded (log 20.18). Say "in 2020, CBO projected"; no post-RAP update exists.
- **FREOPP (attribute; a model including completion risk):** about 23% of bachelor's programs and 43% of
  master's have negative ROI; fine arts usually negative; psychology, education and English low but not
  negative (log 20.20–20.21). FREOPP's older "psychology usually negative ROI" line is CORRECTED by its
  own update and does not air (log 20.22).

**The government's tests** [SYSTEM]
- The 2027 test counts **only graduates**, does **not** subtract price or debt (the debt-to-earnings
  test was dropped; price is collected for disclosure only), and the undergraduate bar does **not** vary
  by field. A tie passes; appeals are for calculation errors only; a failing program is out for two
  years ([FR 2026-13286](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-01/pdf/2026-13286.pdf)).
- **Price-aware tests already exist in states:** Texas counts a "credential of value" only if earnings
  beat both the cost of education (including forgone earnings) and high-school earnings, and ties
  community-college funding to it ([THECB](https://www.highered.texas.gov/?p=39574)); Colorado finds 71%
  of programs (93% of bachelor's programs) meet its threshold, with no penalty
  ([CDHE 2026](https://highered.colorado.gov/publications/Reports/Legislative/ROI/202607_ROI.pdf)).
- **England nets out price:** the average graduate is about **£100,000 (15%)** better off after tax and
  loan repayments, but about 20% of women and 30% of men can expect a negative return
  ([IFS 2026](https://ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/2026-07/IFS%20Report%20-%20New%20estimates%20of%20the%20impact%20of%20undergraduate%20degrees%20on%20lifetime%20earnings_changed.pdf)).

  2025 law [BOTH].
- **GREEN — yes: make colleges carry part of the risk, and stop lending for programs that don't pay.**
  What the record gives it: Washington lends to every eligible student with no test of field, price or
  likely pay (log 18.1–18.4); the school sets the price and bears none of the loss when loans go unpaid

  taxpayers lose about 5 cents per dollar officially, 17 on a fair-value basis (log 18.30); cutting off

- **GOLD — no: that cuts access and hits needed low-pay fields; the real killers are price and not
  finishing.** What the record gives it: risk-sharing's own designers warn of "reduced access to
  education for low-income students" and pass-through tuition increases (log 18.24); the House formula
  skipped defaulted loans and needed data ED doesn't have (log 18.25–18.26, APPROXIMATE); needed
  low-pay fields (teaching, social work, counselling) are exactly what a pay test hits (ED's commenters,
  log 18.27; teachers earn ~27% less than comparable graduates, log 22.43); taxpayer losses sit mostly in
  income-driven relief Congress chose to give, and Parent PLUS makes money (log 18.31); the bachelor's
  debt problem is small at the program level (0.3% of programs over 1× pay, log 20.5) and concentrates

  the bill (housing and food) isn't tuition (log 22.24).
- **The teacher case is the heart of this framing (Lucas, Q3).** A teacher on $47,000 whose degree cost

- **Where the record leans** (to be earned in the debate, not asserted): on "who carries the risk now",

## Contested (the real fight)

**GREEN — "Price the degree against the field."** The question isn't whether college pays on average; it
does (log 6.1, 6.2). It is whether **this** degree, at **this** price, pays for you, and the record says
the price barely moves with the major while the pay moves a lot. A public in-state degree costs about
$60,000 after grants whatever you study; four years out, computer science graduates earn a median
$107,000 and drama graduates $40,000 (log 17.2, 8.1). So the same bill is half a year of pay in one field
and one and a half in another, and at a private nonprofit drama passes three years (log 17.17–17.18).
Students guess wrong about what fields pay, and the poorest students aimed at the lowest-paying programs
guess worst (log 22.36–22.37). One yardstick for every field names the ones whose recent graduates are
both more often underemployed and lower paid than the typical graduate: criminal justice, the performing
arts and fine arts lead it (log 21.10). Pick the field with your eyes open, then the school by what you'll
actually pay, and borrow no more than that field's pay can carry.

**GOLD — "Cut the bill you control."** A field median is a promise to graduates, and three in ten
starters never become one (log 11.2). They are the people the record hurts most: 45% of borrowers who left
with no credential defaulted within 12 years, against 8% of finishers (log 12.9), and only 28% of them
say college was worth it (log 22.26). The odds aren't luck: 67% of full-time starters finish against 34%
part-time, 71–75% at public and private nonprofit four-years and 36% at for-profits (log 11.5, 11.6). And
the biggest line on a public student's bill isn't tuition at all: housing and food are about two-thirds of
the full net cost, net tuition about $2,300 (log 22.24). So the first question is where you're likely to
finish; the second is how little of the bill you can avoid while you do: a cheaper start, a cheaper place
to live, no more borrowing than you must.

**The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:**

- *Gold at green:* **every field number is a graduate number** (log 8.6, 2.11); **non-money value is
  real**, if mostly association (log 22.2, 22.11); **people mostly choose by taste and say it was worth it
  in every field** (log 22.30, 22.34); **field pay is noisy early** (computer science 7.0% recent-grad
  unemployment, log 21.14).
- *Green at gold:* **the cheap routes have their own dropout problem** (1 in 6 transfer to a bachelor's,
  log 11.10; apprentices cancel more than they finish, log 13.2); **the living-at-home saving is not
  measured** by any source; **for students on the margin, the four-year beat the two-year** (log 7.7).

**Where the record leans, said plainly.** On "does a degree pay on average", it is not 50/50: it does.
Between the designs, the record leans **gold on the size of the risk** (not finishing is the most common
and the most expensive bad outcome) and **green on the money dial** (field moves pay far more than price
moves cost). Gold's cheap routes rest on thinner, older or single-state records; green's field figures
cover only graduates.

## Verdict framing

"The design that survives both teams' objections, and the dial you still have to set." Candidates, each
to be **earned in the debate** and dropped if it is not:

- **Survives:** **price the whole bill after grants, housing included, against what the field pays four
  years out, and count your odds of finishing as part of the price.** Green's cost-against-pay rule
  survives because pay is what moves; gold's finishing check survives because non-completion is the worst
  outcome on the record; gold's "cut the bill" survives as a question to ask of every line, not a rule
  that the cheapest route wins.
- **Fell:** "College isn't worth it anymore" (the average still pays). "Any degree pays" (field spread;
  three in ten leave with nothing). "The sticker is the price" and "tuition is the bill" (two-thirds of a
  public student's net cost is housing and food). "Federal debt tells you what it costs" (it is capped at
  $31,000; total cost is the measure). "The FAFSA flag or the 2027 rule tells you whether it's worth it"
  (graduates only, no price).

## Contested (pre-pitch, kept for the record)

**GREEN — "Pick the field, at the price you'll actually pay."** The degree-in-general question is
settled: a bachelor's pays about 60% more, raw, and the best studies still find real gains for students
on the edge of getting in. What is not settled for any one family is **which** degree, and the record
says the field moves the money far more than the school: computer science graduates earn a median
$107,000 four years out, drama graduates $40,000, and inside a single school the best field earns about
2.5 times the worst (2019 data; about 2.2 times on the June 2026 file, our arithmetic). Prestige mostly reflects who got in (Dale and Krueger; Mountjoy and Hickman), so
paying more for a famous name buys little for the average student. The price that matters is the net
one, and it varies by a factor of two-and-a-half by family income at the same kind of school. So: pick
the field on the record, pick the school on net price, and cap borrowing against what that field pays.
Green adds the strongest causal point in the record: in Florida and Georgia, students who **just** got
into the four-year school, rather than the two-year they would otherwise have attended, earned more.
"Start cheap" was the alternative those studies measured, and it lost.

**GOLD — "Bet on finishing: cheapest credible path first."** A field median is a promise to graduates,
and three in ten starters never become one. They leave with debt and a paycheck about a tenth above a
high-school diploma; 41% of those borrowers were behind on payments in 2024 against 11% of bachelor's
holders; in the one primary default study, 45% defaulted within 12 years against 8% of finishers, and
the Fed finds them worse off on their own reports than people who never went. Finishing is not luck: it
runs 67% for full-time starters and 34% for part-time, 71–75% at public and private nonprofit
four-years and 36% at for-profits. So the first dial is the odds of finishing, and the second is how
little you can spend while you find out: grants have covered community-college tuition on average since
2009–10, and in one state, students who finished an associate degree before transferring came out about
$50,000 ahead over 20 years. Gold adds that the government's own test shares the blind spot gold is
pointing at: it counts only graduates.

**The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:**

- *Gold at green:* **every field number is a graduate number.** The Scorecard medians, the FAFSA flag and
  the 2027 test all describe completers; none tells you your odds of becoming one. **The middle halves
  overlap**, so a field median is not a forecast for one student. **Scorecard covers federal-aid
  recipients only.** **Field pay is noisy at the start of a career** (computer engineering's recent-grad
  unemployment is somewhere between 4% and 11% on EIG's own calculation, and one analyst calls
  computer-science majors "in rough shape"). **Nursing's figure likely includes working nurses finishing a BSN**, which would
  lift it (our inference, not checked in the file; say it as a caveat or not at all).
- *Green at gold:* **the cheap path has its own dropout problem.** About 1 in 6 community-college starters
  finishes a bachelor's, while 8 in 10 want one. **Apprentices cancel more often than they finish**
  (48.2% vs 46.5%, FY2024), and DOL's count leaves out those who quit during probation. **Certificates
  are where the federal test bites**: 24.1% of undergraduate-certificate programs fail, against 1.1% of
  bachelor's. **The apprenticeship gain is small and narrow**: a few thousand dollars in one quarter, in
  IT, healthcare and manufacturing programs, not the trades. **And the causal studies cut against
  "start cheap"** for the students they measure: the four-year beat the two-year.

**Where the record leans, said plainly.** On "is a degree worth it" in general, the record is not 50/50:
it pays, raw and in the best causal studies. Between the two designs, the record leans **gold on the
size of the risk** (not finishing is the most common and the most expensive outcome the record shows)
and **green on the route** (the cheap routes gold reaches for have finishing rates as weak or weaker,
and the causal studies favour the four-year over the two-year for marginal students). Equal length on
"documented failures" would misrepresent this: gold's alternatives rest on single-state, single-program
or decade-old studies, and green's field figures cover only graduates.

3. **Finishing** — the hidden price tag: 61% overall; 36% to 75% by sector; 34% part-time; 30% vs 92%

   an associate degree ($4.6–7.2k a year over leaving without one), an apprenticeship (positive, narrow,
   about half don't finish), a job.

## The causal verdict (what the record lets us say)

**Safe wording:** "Raw, a bachelor's holder earns about 60% more than a high-school graduate. The best
studies, of students on the edge of getting in, still find real gains, roughly a fifth more pay. That is
smaller than the raw gap, and it is not the same for everyone."

- It **supports** "it pays" for students at the margin of admission, whose alternative was mostly a
  community college (Florida, Georgia; Lovenheim and Smith).
- It does **not** give a US estimate for the typical enrollee. England's IFS finds about half of the raw
  gap is background and prior attainment (observable controls).
- **Never:** "a degree raises your pay 75%"; "studies prove college causes the premium for everyone";
  "Andrews-Li-Lovenheim shows…" as causal.

## The reframe

The shouting asks "is college worth it?" The record says three things that question misses.

1. **The average is settled; your case isn't.** A bachelor's pays on average. The variance sits in four
   dials (field, price, finishing, the alternative), and the average hides all four.
2. **The price is three numbers.** Sticker ($11,950), net tuition (about $2,300) and the full net cost of
   a year (about $21,340). Families anchor on the first; the third is the one that gets borrowed.
3. **The government's bar answers a different question.** "Beat a high-school diploma" catches the worst
   programs, mostly certificates and for-profits; it fails about one bachelor's program in ninety, and
   it ignores the price you pay and the students who don't finish. It is a floor for the system, not an
   answer for a family.

---

## Both sides quietly agree

**Every official number a family will see this fall describes people who finished.** The FAFSA flag is
built from "undergraduate completers"; the 2027 test from "the students who completed the program";
the Scorecard field medians from graduates. Green's field rule and gold's finishing rule both start
from the same buried fact: **the dropout is the person nobody measures**, and both designs exist to keep
the student from becoming one, one by choosing a field worth finishing, the other by choosing a route
they will finish.

And both agree **the sticker price is not the price.** Neither design is built on it.

## Verdict framing (pre-pitch, kept for the record)

"The design that survives both teams' objections, and the dial you still have to set." Candidates, each
