Is College Worth It? Which Degree, at What Price, Pays Off for You

Is a four-year degree worth what it really costs, for you?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in October 2026, families were opening fall aid offers, and a record-low 31% of Americans called a college education very important, down from 70% in 2013.

Settled

What both sides accept

On average, a degree still pays. The price is the whole bill after grants, housing included, and federal student loans cover only part of it. The pay moves with the field, and about three in ten who start leave with nothing.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesPrice it by field

The price barely changes with the major, and the pay changes a lot. The same public-university bill is about half a year's pay in computer science and a year and a half in drama. Add up four years after grants, set it against what your field pays four years out, and borrow only what that pay can carry.

▶ Hear Green at 3:11 on YouTube
Gold arguesCut the bill

About three in ten who start college leave with nothing, and 45% of borrowers who left without a credential defaulted, against 8% of graduates. Housing and food are about two-thirds of a public-university bill. Cut the bill you control, and count your odds of finishing as part of the price.

▶ Hear Gold at 9:01 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

"College isn't worth it anymore" fell, because the average still pays. "Any degree pays" fell too, given the spread between fields and the three in ten who leave. The plan that survived both teams prices the whole bill against what your field pays four years out and counts your odds of finishing as part of the price, and how much risk to take is your call.

The verdict

What survives both teams
Price the whole bill, after grants, against what your field pays four years out, and count your odds of finishing as part of the price. Green's yardstick, and gold's finishing check.
What didn't hold up
College isn't worth it anymore: the average still pays. Any degree pays: not with that field spread, and three in ten leaving. Tuition is the bill: two-thirds is housing and food. And the cheapest route always wins: the cheap routes leak too.

Read the verdict as it aired

Cole This one doesn't resolve into a yes or a no, and no design wins it. Three things from me. What's settled, what survived, and the dial that's yours.

Cole Both teams accepted the same record. On average, a degree still pays. The price is the whole bill after grants, housing included, and federal student loans cover only part of it. The pay moves with the field. And about three in ten who start leave with nothing.

Cole What survived from green. Price against the field, because pay moves far more than price: the same public bill is half a year's pay in computer science, and a year and a half in drama. And one yardstick for every field, so the data names the fields that fall short, not anyone's politics.

Cole What survived from gold. Finishing as part of the price: not finishing is the most common bad outcome on the record, and the most expensive. And cutting the bill as a question to ask of every line, starting with housing and food.

Cole What fell. College isn't worth it anymore: the average still pays. Any degree pays: not with that field spread, and three in ten leaving. Tuition is the bill: two-thirds is housing and food. And the cheapest route always wins: the cheap routes leak too.

Cole So here's the design that survives both teams. Price the whole bill, after grants, against what your field pays four years out, and count your odds of finishing as part of the price. Green's yardstick, and gold's finishing check.

Cole That leaves one dial neither team can set for you: how much risk you'll take. Low: a state school, in a field whose pay covers the bill, like nursing or computer science, with no borrowing past the federal limit; even there, pay is a graduate's median. Middle: a field that costs a year or more of pay, like teaching, psychology or social work, with the gap covered by savings or work, and early pay below average.

Cole High: a private college in a low-paying field, where drama passes three years of pay, or borrowing past the federal limit through a parent's loan or a private lender. And at every setting, the hidden one: not finishing, where nearly half of borrowers who left defaulted. How much risk will you take for a degree? That one's yours.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: How much risk will you take for a degree?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Low
    What it buys
    A state school, in a field whose pay covers the bill, like nursing or computer science, with no borrowing past the federal limit. There, four years after grants cost about half to two-thirds of a year's pay four years out.
    What it costs
    Even there, pay is a graduate's median: it says nothing about your odds of becoming one. And the federal limit is $31,000 for the whole degree, so on a typical public degree about $30,000 still has to come from income or savings.
    Not measured
    How much a family saves on the biggest line, housing and food, by living at home instead of on campus: no source measures it.

    On the record: row 17.18 of the fact-check log, row 8.1 of the fact-check log, row 17.9 of the fact-check log, row 17.2 of the fact-check log, row 22.24 of the fact-check log

  2. 2Middle
    What it buys
    A field that costs a year or more of pay, like teaching, psychology or social work, with the gap covered by savings or work. At a public university that's about 1.3 years of pay in teaching and 1.2 in psychology, and teaching and social work do land their graduates in jobs that need the degree.
    What it costs
    Early pay below average. Teaching and social work pay less than the typical graduate's $58,000 early on, and about half of recent psychology graduates are in jobs that don't need the degree.
    Not measured
    How many borrowers from lower-paying fields struggle to repay: the government's field data hides almost every bachelor's program, so nobody can say.

    On the record: row 17.15 of the fact-check log, row 17.16 of the fact-check log, row 17.19 of the fact-check log, row 21.11 of the fact-check log, row 21.9 of the fact-check log

  3. 3High
    What it buys
    The field you want at the college you want, even when its pay won't cover the bill. People mostly pick fields because they want to study them, and in every field more graduates say it was worth it than say it wasn't.
    What it costs
    A private college in a low-paying field, where drama passes three years of pay (about 3.1 at a private nonprofit), or borrowing past the federal limit through a parent's loan or a private lender. That borrowing sits on top of the federal debt you usually hear about.
    Not measured
    How often parents default on the loans they take out for a child's degree: the government publishes no default rate for them.

    On the record: row 17.17 of the fact-check log, row 17.11 of the fact-check log, row 22.30 of the fact-check log, row 22.34 of the fact-check log, row 20.12 of the fact-check log

At every setting: The hidden one: not finishing, where nearly half of borrowers who left defaulted (45%, against 8% of graduates). About three in ten who start leave with nothing, and full-time starters finish about twice as often as part-timers.
