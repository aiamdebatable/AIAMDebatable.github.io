The research · The whole bill: what four years really cost

ErinNot the sticker. The bill a family actually pays. We'll use the College Scorecard, the Education Department's own data.

RajSo what does a family actually pay?

ErinAfter grants, the typical student at an in-state public university pays about fifteen thousand dollars a year, everything in: tuition, books, housing and food. At a private nonprofit, about twenty-six thousand. Over four years, roughly sixty thousand, or a hundred and five.

RajDoes it depend on what my family makes?

ErinA lot. At a public university, families earning under thirty thousand dollars pay about ten thousand a year after grants. Above a hundred and ten thousand in income, it's about twenty-three thousand.

RajAnd loans cover the rest?

ErinFederal student loans don't. A dependent undergraduate can borrow thirty-one thousand dollars from the government in total, for the whole degree. On a typical public degree, that leaves about thirty thousand to find somewhere else. At a private nonprofit, about seventy-five.

RajSo where does that come from?

ErinSallie Mae, the biggest private student lender, surveys families every year. In its latest, about half of what families paid came from income and savings. About half of families borrowed: through a parent's federal loan, a private lender, a credit card. And about one in six pulled money out of retirement savings.

RajWhy does that matter?

ErinBecause the debt you hear about is federal student debt, and it's capped. The parent's loan and the private loan sit on top of it, and the savings never show up as debt at all. So the number to judge a degree by is the whole bill.

RajOne more. Does everyone finish in four years?

ErinAbout half finish in four years at the school where they started. Many pay for a fifth.

RajSo the price is the whole bill, for as long as it takes. Hold that.

The research · What it pays back, and why the field matters

ErinThe mood first, then the record.

RajThe mood?

ErinGallup found a record-low thirty-one percent of Americans call a college education very important. In twenty thirteen it was seventy. That's an opinion poll, not a paycheck.

RajAnd the record?

ErinOn average, a degree still pays. A bachelor's holder earns about sixty percent more a week than a high-school graduate, and is out of work less often. That's the raw gap. The best studies, of students on the edge of getting in, still find real gains, roughly a fifth more pay. Smaller than the raw gap, and not the same for everyone.

RajSo it pays. End of story?

ErinNot for one family, because the field moves the pay. Four years after graduating, the Education Department's median is about a hundred and seven thousand dollars in computer science. Eighty-nine thousand in nursing. Forty-seven thousand in teacher education. Forty thousand in drama.

RajAnd the price changes with the major too?

ErinBarely. At a public university, four years after grants come to close to sixty thousand dollars, whatever the major. So set that bill against one year's pay, four years out. Computer science: about half a year. Teaching: about a year and a quarter. Drama: about a year and a half. At a private nonprofit, drama passes three years.

RajWhich fields come up short?

ErinUse one yardstick for every field. The New York Fed tracks recent graduates by major. Call a field short if its graduates are more often in jobs that don't need a degree than the typical graduate, which is about four in ten, and earn less early on than the typical graduate's fifty-eight thousand.

RajAnd who's at the bottom?

ErinOn jobs that don't need the degree, criminal justice is worst: about two in three of its recent graduates. Then the performing arts. Then fine arts.

RajAnd the fields that get argued about? Ethnic and gender studies, sociology, English?

ErinThey land in the middle of the pack. Ethnic studies, sociology, psychology, English and history: about half their recent graduates are in jobs that don't need the degree, earning around fifty thousand early on. That's right beside business management, communications and biology. And ethnic and gender studies, which the Education Department counts as one field, pay about the same four years out as psychology and social work.

RajSo a graduate can't find a job?

ErinMostly they find one. Unemployment for recent graduates is under eight percent in every major, and computer science is near the top. The problem is the kind of job. And it sticks: of graduates who start in a job that doesn't need their degree, nearly three in four are still in one ten years later.

RajAnd the jobs the country needs? Teaching?

ErinTeaching and social work do land their graduates in jobs that need the degree. At below-average pay. Needed and well paid aren't the same list.

RajSo it pays on average, the price is flat, and the field moves the pay. Hold that.

The research · The student who left after two years

ErinEvery pay figure you just heard describes graduates. Now meet a student who left after two years.

RajHow common is that?

ErinCommon. Follow everyone who started college one recent fall. Six years on, about six in ten had finished a degree or certificate. One in ten was still going. And about three in ten had left with nothing.

RajIs it luck?

ErinNo. Where you start matters. About seven in ten finish when they start at a public or private nonprofit four-year school. At a for-profit, about one in three. And full-time starters finish about twice as often as part-timers.

RajSo what happens to the student who left?

ErinThey paid for two years and hold no degree. People with some college and no degree earn about a tenth more a week than a high-school graduate.

RajAnd the loans?

ErinStill due. In the government's long study of students who started in two thousand three, forty-five percent of borrowers who left with no credential defaulted. Of bachelor's graduates, eight percent. And in a Federal Reserve survey, only twenty-eight percent of people who didn't finish say college was worth what it cost.

RajDoesn't the government warn you about this?

ErinAbout something else. Since last year, the federal aid form can flag a college whose graduates earn less, four years out, than typical high-school graduates in its state. And under a federal rule that takes effect in July twenty twenty-seven, a program whose graduates earn less than young working adults with only a high-school diploma, two years out of three, loses federal student loans.

RajSo that's my test?

ErinIt's the government's test. Both count only graduates. Neither one subtracts the price you pay. The student who left after two years isn't in either number.

RajThen why does anyone lend for a program like that?

ErinWashington lends to every eligible student without asking what the program costs or what it pays. Whether colleges should carry that risk is a debate of its own, and it gets its own episode.

RajSo every official number describes the people who finished. Hold that.

The research · The experience, what money can't count, and the cheaper routes

ErinNow the question families ask out loud.

RajOkay. Why should I go into debt for the experience of college?

ErinStart with what the experience costs. Take the full cost of a year at a public university, after grants. College Board estimates housing and food are about two-thirds of it. Tuition, after grants, is about twenty-three hundred dollars.

RajTwo-thirds? For a room and meals?

ErinCareful with that. You'd pay rent and eat if you skipped college too. The real price of the experience is campus living over living at home, and no source we found measures it. But the biggest line on a state-school bill isn't tuition. At a private nonprofit, housing and food are about two-fifths.

RajAnd what does a degree buy that isn't money?

ErinGraduates live longer, vote more and say they're happier. How much of that the degree causes is still argued. The best studies say some of it, for some people.

RajDo graduates think it was worth it?

ErinMost do. More than two in three bachelor's holders say the benefits beat the costs. About a third would pick a different field: fewer in engineering, more in the arts, the humanities and the social sciences. But in every field, more say it was worth it than say it wasn't. And the common regret isn't too much school. It's too little.

RajSo why do people pick fields that pay less?

ErinMostly because they want to study them. In every study we read, enjoyment and interest beat expected pay. And students guess wrong about pay. In Chile, low-income students applying to low-earning programs guessed graduates earned more than twice what they do. Showing them the real numbers moved choices only a little.

RajThen the cheaper routes. Start at a community college?

ErinOn average, grants cover community-college tuition. But of students who start there, only about one in six earns a bachelor's, while about eight in ten say they want one.

RajAn associate degree? A trade?

ErinAn associate degree pays: several thousand dollars a year more than leaving without one. Apprenticeships leak too: about half of apprentices don't finish. And no study we found compares a trade head to head with a bachelor's, after cost and finishing.

RajSo start cheap?

ErinNot as a rule. For students on the edge of getting into a four-year school, the best studies found the four-year beat the two-year they'd otherwise have started at.

RajSo two-thirds of the bill is housing and food, the cheap routes leak too, and most graduates say it was worth it. Hold that.

The verdict

ColeThis one doesn't resolve into a yes or a no, and no design wins it. Three things from me. What's settled, what survived, and the dial that's yours.

ColeBoth teams accepted the same record. On average, a degree still pays. The price is the whole bill after grants, housing included, and federal student loans cover only part of it. The pay moves with the field. And about three in ten who start leave with nothing.

ColeWhat survived from green. Price against the field, because pay moves far more than price: the same public bill is half a year's pay in computer science, and a year and a half in drama. And one yardstick for every field, so the data names the fields that fall short, not anyone's politics.

ColeWhat survived from gold. Finishing as part of the price: not finishing is the most common bad outcome on the record, and the most expensive. And cutting the bill as a question to ask of every line, starting with housing and food.

ColeWhat fell. College isn't worth it anymore: the average still pays. Any degree pays: not with that field spread, and three in ten leaving. Tuition is the bill: two-thirds is housing and food. And the cheapest route always wins: the cheap routes leak too.

ColeSo here's the design that survives both teams. Price the whole bill, after grants, against what your field pays four years out, and count your odds of finishing as part of the price. Green's yardstick, and gold's finishing check.

ColeThat leaves one dial neither team can set for you: how much risk you'll take. Low: a state school, in a field whose pay covers the bill, like nursing or computer science, with no borrowing past the federal limit; even there, pay is a graduate's median. Middle: a field that costs a year or more of pay, like teaching, psychology or social work, with the gap covered by savings or work, and early pay below average.

ColeHigh: a private college in a low-paying field, where drama passes three years of pay, or borrowing past the federal limit through a parent's loan or a private lender. And at every setting, the hidden one: not finishing, where nearly half of borrowers who left defaulted. How much risk will you take for a degree? That one's yours.
