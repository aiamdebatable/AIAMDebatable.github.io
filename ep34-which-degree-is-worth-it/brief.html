The pitch, in one line

A four-year degree costs a family about $60,000 at a state school and about $105,000 at a private one, after grants; what it pays back depends on the field you pick and on whether you finish; and the biggest line on a state-school bill is housing and food, not tuition. Is it worth what it really costs, for you, and how much risk are you willing to take?

The viewer's moment

Two people, this fall. (Invented and unnamed; never a real or identifiable person.)

  • A family looking at an aid offer. After grants, a typical in-state public university still asks about $15,000 a year, a typical private nonprofit about $26,500: four years, roughly $61,000 or $106,000 (log 17.2, 17.3). The student can borrow $31,000 in federal loans in total (log 17.9). The rest comes from savings, work, a parent's PLUS loan or a private lender, and about two-thirds of the public-university bill is housing and food, not tuition (log 22.24).
  • A student who left after two years, one of about three in ten starters who leave with no credential (log 11.2), repaying loans for a degree they don't have: 45% of borrowers who left with no credential defaulted within 12 years, against 8% of bachelor's finishers (log 12.9), and only 28% of non-finishers say college was worth it (log 22.26).

And the field question, through one example: a student who wants to teach. A needed job, about $47,000 four years out (the national median for teacher-education graduates, log 8.1), and a degree that costs about 1.3 years of that pay at a public school, 1.9 at a private one (log 17.15).

The episode follows the family's bill through the settings of the risk dial and asks, at each, what it costs, what it pays, and the odds of finishing. (The teacher's repayment and "who should carry the risk" are the companion episode's story.)

The question

Is a four-year degree worth what it really costs, for you? The dial the viewer sets is RISK (ruling 3): how much total cost and debt you'll take on, for what pay, at what odds of finishing.

The combined question at the pitch was "…and who should carry the risk when it doesn't pay?"; that half is now the companion episode (ep36).

The earlier question, kept for the record: "Which degree, at what price, is worth it for you?" Its four dials (field, price, finishing, the alternative) still hold, and the field and price dials now read through total cost.

Why now

The 2027–28 FAFSA opened on 2026-09-23, the earliest launch in the program's history for the second year running (ED, 2026-09-23). Since last cycle (December 2025), a first-year applicant can see a "lower earnings" notice for any school on their list whose graduates earn less, four years out, than typical high-school graduates in that state (nationally, if the school serves mostly out-of-state students). It is per school, it is built from federal-aid completers of 2017–18 and 2018–19 with earnings measured in 2022 and 2023, and it is information only: it changes no aid (FSA announcement, updated 2026-03-23; ED blog). ED's September launch release lists it among the features introduced last cycle; no ED text we read says the 2027–28 Submission Summary displays it, or which data year it uses. ED says fewer than 3% of undergraduates attend such a school, and that about 25% of students shown the flag dropped that school and picked another (ED, 2026-03-26; ED's own claim, unaudited).

Next year a similar bar, not the same one, gets teeth. Under a final rule published 2026-07-01 and effective 2027-07-01, an undergraduate program fails if its graduates' median earnings, in the fourth tax year after finishing, are less than those of working adults aged 25–34 with only a high-school diploma in the school's state (or nationally). A program that fails in two of any three years loses federal Direct Loans; ED's analysis puts the first sanction year in the award year starting July 2028 (Federal Register 2026-13286). ED projects, in the final rule's Table 5.12, that 1.1% of bachelor's programs (0.6% of their students) fail, against 6.0% of associate programs and 24.1% of undergraduate certificates (27.6% of certificate students); 5.2% of all programs. At the institution level, a school with half its aid recipients or aid in failing programs can lose all federal aid for those programs unless it bars loans in them, and ED's Table 5.11 has 43% of for-profit institutions failing that test (both tables typed by hand from ED's images; re-read the images before airing a table figure). ED says it will publish the program-level data in 2027 (FSA, GENERAL-26-49).

The flag and the rule are cousins: one per school against "typical high school graduates", one per program against a defined Census group aged 25–34. Never say they are the same bar, and never say 25–34 about the flag.

And the public mood has moved: a record-low 31% of Americans call a college education "very important", down from 70% in 2013 (Gallup, 2026-10-01; an opinion poll, n=1,200, ±4 points).

So Washington has written down a version of the episode's answer, and its bar is "beat a high-school diploma". It does not subtract the price you pay. It does not count the students who don't finish. At the bachelor's level it fails about one program in ninety. That gap is the episode.

HOW GRADUATES GET OUT (ruling 5; top-up D1) [BOTH]

  • PSLF: what is left of a Direct Loan is cancelled, tax-free, after 120 qualifying payments while working full time in public service; public-school teaching and public child/family social work are named (log 19.4, 19.10). RAP payments count (log 19.5). The early program approved almost no one (TEPSLF approved 1%, 661 of ~54,000, log 19.6); now about 1.29 million borrowers have had $96.4 billion discharged through August 2026 (log 19.7; re-read the sheet rows before airing). From 2026-07-01 ED can disqualify employers with a "substantial illegal purpose" (log 19.8; lawsuit not read).
  • Teacher Loan Forgiveness: after 5 consecutive years in a low-income school, up to $5,000, or $17,500 for secondary math/science or special education (log 19.1); about 13,800 borrowers got it in FY2025, down from ~45,000 in FY2018 (log 19.2). The same years cannot count for both TLF and PSLF (log 19.3).
  • RAP / income-driven forgiveness: RAP forgives after 360 payments (30 years); old IDR plans forgave after 20 or 25; legacy borrowers must move by 2028-07-01 (log 19.9). ⚠ IDR/RAP forgiveness granted from 2026 is federally taxable (plain reading of the statute, log 19.10).
  • Bankruptcy: federal and private student loans survive bankruptcy unless repaying is an "undue hardship" (log 19.11). Since November 2022 a DOJ/ED attestation lets government lawyers agree to discharge; in the first 10 months 632 cases were filed, and in 99% of the cases a court had ruled on the government recommended and the court granted full or partial discharge (of decided cases, not of filers; log 19.12–19.13).
  • Parent PLUS: a parent borrowing on or after 2026-07-01 gets only a Tiered Standard plan, no PSLF; older Parent PLUS keeps a PSLF route only through consolidation before 2026-07-01 plus an IDR plan by 2028-07-01, lost on any new Direct Loan; PSLF turns on the parent's job (log 19.14, 4.10).
  • Worked case: a teacher on $47,000 who borrowed about $80,000 (hypothetical; federal + parent/ private). As a dependent student they could borrow at most $31,000 themselves; the other ~$49,000 is Parent PLUS (their parent's debt) or private loans. Their own Direct Loans have an exit: PSLF after ten years in a public school, tax-free; on RAP at $47,000 their payment would be about $157 a month before the $50-per-dependent credit (our arithmetic on RAP's 4% bracket, flat pay; not an ED example) (log 19.4, 19.5, 4.6). TLF needs five years in a low-income school and those years can't also count for PSLF. The parent's PLUS loan has an exit only through the parent's own public-service job, and only on the narrow legacy path; the private loans have no federal exit except bankruptcy's undue-hardship test. One line: the federal part has an exit; the parent and private parts mostly don't.

GRADUATES VS JOBS — one yardstick for every field (ruling 6; top-up D4) [BOTH]

  • The rule (applied to all 73 NY Fed majors, ACS 2024, ages 22–27, released February 2026): a field falls short if its recent graduates are more often underemployed than the all-majors 39.4% AND have a lower early-career median than the all-majors $58,000 (log 21.8, 21.12). It is relative, so about half fall short by construction: read it as "worse than the typical graduate on both counts".
  • 34 of 73 majors fall short on both, 17 on one, 22 pass (our arithmetic, APPROXIMATE, log 21.12).
  • The worst on underemployment: criminal justice (65.8%), the performing arts (63.9%), then fine arts (58.9%) (log 21.10). Criminal justice is also worst on Strada's five-year measure (68%, log 21.23).
  • The fields Lucas named sit mid-pack among those that fall short: ethnic studies 49.6% underemployed, $52k; sociology 52.0%, $49.9k; psychology 48.3%, $45k; English 48.5%, $48k; history 50.1%, $47.5k; philosophy 47.1%, $52k (log 21.9), beside business management (52.6%, $56k), communications, biology and political science. Gender studies has no NY Fed row of its own (log 21.32); ED pools it with ethnic studies at $51,710, the same as social work and psychology (log 21.17). Ethnic studies' mid-career median ($90k) is above the all-majors $87k (log 21.15).
  • Sizes: gender + ethnic studies together are about 2,500 degrees a year (~0.13% of bachelor's); criminal justice graduates ~18× as many, business (excl. accounting) ~131× (IPEDS via Urban, 2022–23, APPROXIMATE, log 21.4, 21.6). The big numbers of graduates in jobs that don't need the degree come from the big majors: business, psychology, communications, criminal justice.
  • The problem is underemployment, not unemployment. Recent-grad unemployment by major runs only 0.7–7.9%; computer science (7.0%) and computer engineering (7.8%) are among the highest (log 21.14). 73% of those who start underemployed still are ten years on (Strada, log 21.24).
  • "Necessary" degrees are not all well paid. Teaching and social work land jobs (16–30% underemployed) at below-average pay ($43–45k early) (log 21.11); BLS projects about 227,900 K–12 teacher openings a year (log 21.27). Fields that pass: nursing, accounting, computer science, engineering (log 21.11).

NON-MONEY VALUE AND THE "EXPERIENCE" (ruling 2; top-up D3) [PERSONAL]

  • Associations, mostly, not causes. Graduates report being happy more often (5 points over high-school graduates; about 2 points at the same income), have more rewarding jobs, vote and volunteer more (log 22.2–22.3); the BA / no-BA gap in expected years of life between 25 and 75 rose from 1.9 to 3.1 years, 1990–2018 (log 22.11). Case and Deaton "take no position" on whether a degree causes health (log 22.12). For college itself the causal record is thin: one mortality study (Vietnam-draft men, ~28% lower cumulative mortality, log 22.8) and one voting study (+21–30 points, contested; the critiques were not read, log 22.15, 22.18). Reviewers: whether health gains beyond high school are causal "is not known" (2006); the effect "exists in some contexts but not in others" (2018) (log 22.5, 22.9). Nothing shows the field studied changes the health benefit (log 22.6). Script line: "Graduates live longer, vote more and say they're happier. How much of that the degree causes is still argued; the best studies say some of it, for some people."
  • What the "experience" costs. At a public in-state university, housing and food are $13,900 of a $21,340 net cost, about two-thirds; net tuition is $2,300 (College Board 2025–26, log 22.24); at a private nonprofit about 43%. ⚠ A student who skipped college would still pay rent and food: the true price of "the experience" is campus living over living at home, which no source measures. On the school side, instruction is about a third of core spending at public four-years (about a quarter of total spending, our arithmetic; the rest includes research and hospitals, not only amenities) (log 22.22); the average school spent 51 cents on amenities per academic dollar in 2007 (log 22.20); students reward amenities, but student demand explains only 16% of how schools split spending (log 22.21).
  • Regret (Fed SHED 2023, the latest asking): 68% of bachelor's holders say the benefits beat the costs, 43% of associate holders, 28% of those who didn't finish (log 22.26); 44% of degree holders still paying loans vs 68% who paid off or never borrowed (log 22.27). 35% would choose a different field (engineering 27%, humanities/arts 43%, social/behavioral sciences 44%) (log 22.29); the common regret is too little education (45%), not too much (10%) (log 22.28). In every field more people say "worth it" than "not worth it" (log 22.30): the fair answer to "they complain".
  • Why students choose low-paying fields: mostly taste. Enjoyment, grades and interest beat expected pay in every study read (log 22.34, 22.36); beliefs about pay are often wrong, worst among low-income students aimed at low-paying programs (over 100% too high in Chile, log 22.37). Real numbers move choices only a little (4.6% in Chile; ~7.5% would switch at Duke with perfect forecasts, log 22.37, 22.38). So missing information is part of the answer; "they wanted to study it" is most of it.
  • The people who need low-pay degrees: high-school teachers median $72,040, ~63,500 openings a year; K–elementary $62,680–$63,970, ~99,400 openings; social workers $61,780, ~69,100 openings (BLS, all ages, May 2025; log 22.40–22.42); teachers earn about 27% less per week than comparable graduates (EPI, a labor-aligned think tank; log 22.43). ⚠ BLS medians are all ages; ED's $47k is four years out.

The earlier settled record

On average, a bachelor's pays [PERSONAL]

  • Raw, a bachelor's holder earns about 60% more than a high-school graduate. BLS 2025: median weekly earnings $1,578 vs $966, unemployment 2.8% vs 4.3% (age 25+, full-time, bachelor's only) (BLS). NCES 2024, full-time workers aged 25–34: $74,480 vs $46,380 (NCES Digest 502.30). Not the SF Fed's 75%, which includes graduate degrees.
  • The best causal studies still find real gains, for students at the margin of getting in. Florida: about 22% higher earnings 8–14 years out for marginal admits, close to zero for women and better-off students (Zimmerman, JOLE 2014). Georgia: about 17% higher estimated household income at 30, marginally insignificant, about 27% for students from low-income high schools (NBER w27177). In these studies the alternative was mostly a two-year college, not "no college", and rigorous degree-vs-no-degree studies are few (Lovenheim and Smith, NBER w29933). Ability bias does not erase the return to schooling (Card, Econometrica 2001).
  • Prestige is mostly who gets in. A more selective college adds little for the average student, more for disadvantaged students (Dale and Krueger, NBER w7322, w17159); the selectivity premium fades within a few years (Mountjoy and Hickman, NBER w29276).
  • There is no US causal estimate for the typical enrollee. None was found (log row 7.11).

The price you pay is not the sticker, and not the tuition either [PERSONAL] (read with TOTAL COST above: these are College Board's and NCES's measures)

Private nonprofit: $45,000 sticker, about $16,910 net tuition, about $37,380 net cost. Community college: $4,150 sticker, and on average grants have covered tuition since 2009–10. College Board estimates for first-time full-time students (Trends in College Pricing and Student Aid 2025).

Private nonprofit: about $20,800 even at $30,001–48,000, up to $29,800 (NCES). Different definition and year from College Board's figure: never on one chart.
  • Debt: 47% of 2023–24 bachelor's graduates (public and private nonprofit, same school start to finish) borrowed, averaging $29,560; 53% left with none (College Board, above).
  • Borrowing this year: undergraduate federal loans 6.52%, PLUS 9.07% for 2026–27 (FR 2026-18493).

The 2025 law changed what families can borrow [BOTH] (FR 2026-08556)

  • Parent PLUS: from 2026-07-01, $20,000 a year per dependent student (all parents combined) and $65,000 in total, counting earlier borrowing. Students already enrolled with a loan before 2026-07-01 keep the old limits for their expected time to credential.
  • Grad PLUS ends for new loans, with no replacement; new graduate caps $20,500 a year ($100,000 total), professional $50,000 ($200,000).
  • RAP (the Repayment Assistance Plan) replaces the income-driven plans for new loans from 2026-07-01: payments by brackets of total income ($120 a year up to $10,000, then 1% … up to 10% above $100,000), minus $50 per dependent per month, $10 minimum, forgiveness after 360 payments; brackets not indexed to inflation. New Parent PLUS borrowers get only a Tiered Standard plan that does not count for Public Service Loan Forgiveness.

Finishing is the hidden price tag [PERSONAL]

  • 61.1% of students who started in fall 2019 finished any credential within six years; 29.8% had left with nothing; 9.0% were still enrolled (National Student Clearinghouse; data appendix).
  • By where you start: public four-year 71%, private nonprofit 75%, for-profit four-year 36%, public two-year 44%; full-time starters 67%, part-time 34%; starters aged 21+ about 36% (our sums from the appendix). By neighbourhood income: 50% vs 76%.
  • By selectivity (same school, full-time bachelor's seekers, 2017 entry): about 30% at open-admission four-years vs about 92% at schools admitting under a quarter (NCES Digest 326.10). Mostly who enrolls, not what the school does; never say a selective school makes you finish.
  • Community college to bachelor's: about 1 in 6. Of fall 2018 starters, 31.6% transferred within six years and 48.7% of those finished (about 15% of the cohort, our arithmetic) (NSC Tracking Transfer), while nearly 80% say they want a bachelor's (CCRC).

Starting and not finishing is the worst outcome on the record [BOTH]

  • 43.1 million Americans have some college and no credential (NSC).
  • They earn $1,062 a week vs $966 for a high-school diploma, about a tenth more, against $1,578 for a bachelor's (BLS 2025, link above).
  • Of borrowers who left a degree program without even an associate degree, 41% were behind on payments in 2024, vs 11% of bachelor's holders (Fed SHED 2024).
  • 45% of borrowers who left with no credential defaulted within 12 years, vs 8% of bachelor's finishers; certificate finishers 44% (NCES, 2003–04 starting cohort) (NCES 2018-410).
  • The Fed's authors find non-completing borrowers report lower well-being than people who never went at all (descriptive, not causal) (FEDS Note 2023).

The alternatives are positive but thin [PERSONAL]

  • Associate degrees pay: about $4,640–$7,160 a year over leaving without an award, across eight states (CCRC review). Longer career-technical programs do well, especially health (California, 14–45%, NBER w21137); short-term certificates show little or no effect in most fields (Dadgar and Trimble).
  • Apprenticeship: in one federal study of newer programs (IT, healthcare, advanced manufacturing, funded by two DOL grant initiatives), apprentices earned a few thousand dollars more in one quarter, about two years later, than similar community-college and job-service participants (Mathematica / Urban for DOL). Not the construction trades, not per year, not lifetime.
  • About half of apprentices don't finish either: DOL's FY2024 nationwide figures show 46.5% completing vs 48.2% cancelling (DOL dashboard; transcribed from a browser render; re-read before airing), and DOL's definition drops people who leave during probation (DOL circular 2026-03).
  • Electricians and plumbers earn medians of about $63,000–64,000, typically with a high-school diploma plus an apprenticeship (BLS OOH; transcribed from a browser render; re-read before airing). Medians of everyone in the trade, not an outcome study.
  • Employers' "skills-based hiring" moved fewer than 1 in 700 hires (Burning Glass Institute and HBS, 2024).
  • No study compares an apprenticeship or trade head to head with a bachelor's, net of cost, time and completion. Every alternatives study is one state, one grant program, or over a decade old.

WHY IT'S ALLOWED (ruling 1; top-up D1) [SYSTEM]

  • The lender is Washington, and the law tells it to lend to every eligible student at an approved school: Direct Loans are funded with "such sums as may be necessary" (20 USC 1087a; log 18.1–18.2). Undergraduate loan size depends on class year and dependency, never on field, price or likely pay (log 18.3–18.4). The only credit screen is PLUS's "adverse credit history" check (log 18.6, APPROXIMATE as "the only"). Schools are approved by accreditors and states, not on outcomes (log 18.8, APPROXIMATE). There is no federal cap on what a college charges; price is disclosure-only in the 2027 rule (log 18.35, APPROXIMATE as an absence).
  • The caps leaked through parents and grad school: until 2026-07-01, Parent PLUS and Grad PLUS were limited only by the school's cost of attendance (log 18.5); now Parent PLUS is $20k/$65k and Grad PLUS is ended (log 18.7).
  • Every attempt to tie loans to results was narrow, struck down or repealed. 2011 rule vacated in court in 2012 (log 18.9); 2014 rule rescinded (published 2019, effective 2020) after one year of rates, with no program ever losing aid though 2,050 were failing or in the zone (log 18.10–18.12); 2023 rule's loss of aid covered only gainful-employment programs, mostly for-profits and certificates (log 18.13); 90/10 applies to for-profits only (log 18.15); cohort-default sanctions hit a handful of schools a year, most winning appeal (12 sanctioned for FY2017, 10 won, log 18.16–18.17, APPROXIMATE), and were gamed by pushing borrowers into forbearance (GAO, log 18.18). The 2025 law put an earnings-only test in statute for all programs and dropped the debt test (log 18.14).
  • Who pays when loans aren't repaid: the borrower and the taxpayer, never the school. CBO: about 5 cents per dollar lent on the official basis, about 17 cents on a fair-value basis, for 2027 (log 18.30); losses sit in subsidized undergraduate loans and income-driven repayment, while Parent PLUS makes money for the government (log 18.31). GAO: 25 years of Direct Loans first booked as $114 billion income were by FY2021 estimated to cost $197 billion (log 18.32, pre-RAP). The 2025 law moved the estimate back by $50 billion, mainly through RAP (log 18.33). A "share never repaid" is UNRESOLVED (log 18.34).
  • The 2025 law and the dropped House risk-sharing. The House-passed bill made every school repay part of its students' unpaid loans from 2028–29 (100% where completers' value-added earnings were negative) (log 18.19); the Senate swapped it for the earnings test and the enacted law has no such section (log 18.20). CBO's score "more than $6 billion" reaches us only through NPR (log 18.21, APPROXIMATE).
  • Does cutting off aid change behaviour? In the 1990s, sanctioned for-profits lost ~40% of enrolment, community colleges took many of those students, and total enrolment fell just 3% (NBER w22967, 2016 version, log 18.38).
  • The steelman both ways is under "Two framings" (DEBATE), log 18.22–18.29.

WHERE THE DEBT THAT NEVER GETS PAID BACK SITS (top-up D2) [SYSTEM]

  • Bachelor's federal student debt is not where it concentrates: 57 of 21,801 bachelor's programs (0.3%) have median federal debt above their graduates' median pay, against 534 of 6,764 master's programs (7.9%) (graduates who borrowed, federal only, our arithmetic, log 20.5). On a GE-style yearly test, 5.4% vs 34.8% (APPROXIMATE, not ED's calculation, log 20.6). Master's over 1× pay cluster in private schools (nonprofit 13.5%, for-profit 14.1%, public 2.3%) and in counselling, therapy and arts fields (log 20.9); master's drama debt is about 2× pay (log 20.7).
  • Parent PLUS is the invisible part: $115 billion outstanding for 3.5 million students (log 20.11); ED publishes no Parent PLUS default rate and schools face no consequence (Brookings, APPROXIMATE, log 20.12); drama parents borrow a median ~$62,000 (26% of graduates' parents) (log 20.4); at 7.1% of bachelor's programs the parent's PLUS median alone tops the graduate's pay (log 20.10).
  • Graduate students take ~48% of new federal loans while being ~16–17% of students (log 20.14); 49% of outstanding federal debt is held by the 11% who owe $80,000+ (log 20.15).
  • Non-completers are the largest group: 45% default vs 8% of bachelor's finishers (log 12.9); they pay one or more years of the costs above with no degree.
  • CBO forgiveness shares (⚠ DATED, February 2020, before SAVE and before RAP): of loans on income-driven plans, ~21% of undergraduate and ~56% of graduate dollars were projected to be forgiven, Parent PLUS excluded (log 20.18). Say "in 2020, CBO projected"; no post-RAP update exists.
  • FREOPP (attribute; a model including completion risk): about 23% of bachelor's programs and 43% of master's have negative ROI; fine arts usually negative; psychology, education and English low but not negative (log 20.20–20.21). FREOPP's older "psychology usually negative ROI" line is CORRECTED by its own update and does not air (log 20.22).

The government's tests [SYSTEM]

  • The 2027 test counts only graduates, does not subtract price or debt (the debt-to-earnings test was dropped; price is collected for disclosure only), and the undergraduate bar does not vary by field. A tie passes; appeals are for calculation errors only; a failing program is out for two years (FR 2026-13286).
  • Price-aware tests already exist in states: Texas counts a "credential of value" only if earnings beat both the cost of education (including forgone earnings) and high-school earnings, and ties community-college funding to it (THECB); Colorado finds 71% of programs (93% of bachelor's programs) meet its threshold, with no penalty (CDHE 2026).
  • England nets out price: the average graduate is about £100,000 (15%) better off after tax and loan repayments, but about 20% of women and 30% of men can expect a negative return (IFS 2026).

  • GREEN — yes: make colleges carry part of the risk, and stop lending for programs that don't pay. Green asks why the school was never at risk; gold asks whether a rule that punishes the school would have closed the program the country needs.
  • Where the record leans (to be earned in the debate, not asserted): on "who carries the risk now", it is settled: the borrower, the parent and the taxpayer, never the school. On "would risk-sharing help", the record is thin: the only behavioural evidence is 1990s default sanctions on for-profits (log 18.38) and the 2014 rule's closures (log 15.2, APPROXIMATE); nobody has measured risk-sharing on degree-granting schools.

Contested (the real fight)

GREEN — "Price the degree against the field." The question isn't whether college pays on average; it does (log 6.1, 6.2). It is whether this degree, at this price, pays for you, and the record says the price barely moves with the major while the pay moves a lot. A public in-state degree costs about $60,000 after grants whatever you study; four years out, computer science graduates earn a median $107,000 and drama graduates $40,000 (log 17.2, 8.1). Students guess wrong about what fields pay, and the poorest students aimed at the lowest-paying programs guess worst (log 22.36–22.37). One yardstick for every field names the ones whose recent graduates are both more often underemployed and lower paid than the typical graduate: criminal justice, the performing arts and fine arts lead it (log 21.10). Pick the field with your eyes open, then the school by what you'll actually pay, and borrow no more than that field's pay can carry.

GOLD — "Cut the bill you control." A field median is a promise to graduates, and three in ten starters never become one (log 11.2). They are the people the record hurts most: 45% of borrowers who left with no credential defaulted within 12 years, against 8% of finishers (log 12.9), and only 28% of them say college was worth it (log 22.26). And the biggest line on a public student's bill isn't tuition at all: housing and food are about two-thirds of the full net cost, net tuition about $2,300 (log 22.24). So the first question is where you're likely to finish; the second is how little of the bill you can avoid while you do: a cheaper start, a cheaper place to live, no more borrowing than you must.

  • It does not give a US estimate for the typical enrollee. England's IFS finds about half of the raw gap is background and prior attainment (observable controls).
  • Never: "a degree raises your pay 75%"; "studies prove college causes the premium for everyone"; "Andrews-Li-Lovenheim shows…" as causal.

The reframe

The shouting asks "is college worth it?" The record says three things that question misses.

  1. The average is settled; your case isn't. A bachelor's pays on average. The variance sits in four dials (field, price, finishing, the alternative), and the average hides all four.
  2. The price is three numbers. Sticker ($11,950), net tuition (about $2,300) and the full net cost of a year (about $21,340). Families anchor on the first; the third is the one that gets borrowed.
  3. The government's bar answers a different question. "Beat a high-school diploma" catches the worst programs, mostly certificates and for-profits; it fails about one bachelor's program in ninety, and it ignores the price you pay and the students who don't finish. It is a floor for the system, not an answer for a family.

Both sides quietly agree

Every official number a family will see this fall describes people who finished. The FAFSA flag is built from "undergraduate completers"; the 2027 test from "the students who completed the program"; the Scorecard field medians from graduates. Green's field rule and gold's finishing rule both start from the same buried fact: the dropout is the person nobody measures, and both designs exist to keep the student from becoming one, one by choosing a field worth finishing, the other by choosing a route they will finish.

And both agree the sticker price is not the price. Neither design is built on it.

Verdict framing (pre-pitch, kept for the record)

"The design that survives both teams' objections, and the dial you still have to set." Candidates, each to be earned in the debate and dropped if it is not:

  • Survives: choose the field on the record and the school on net price, then check the odds that a student like you finishes there, and treat a low-finish route as expensive even when it is cheap. Green's field-and-net-price rule survives because it moves the money most; gold's finishing check survives because non-completion is the worst outcome on the record. Gold's "cheapest first" does not survive as a rule, because the cheap routes have weak finishing records of their own; it survives as a question to ask of every route.
  • Fell: "College isn't worth it anymore" (the average still pays, and the causal studies agree for marginal students). "Any degree pays" (field spread; three in ten leave with nothing). "The FAFSA flag tells you whether a school is worth it" (per school, graduates only, no price). "Sticker is the price." "Trades are the safe bet" (about half of apprentices don't finish; no head-to-head study). "The 2027 rule will close bad bachelor's programs" (about 1 in 90 fail).
  • The one dial the viewer sets — the judge names it, the judge does not set it. Two honest candidates; Lucas picks one at the pitch:
  • A. HOW MUCH YOU'LL PAY FOR A GIVEN FIELD (net price and borrowing against what the field pays). Settings, each a real institution with a documented weakness: (1) community college first — grants cover tuition on average; weakness: about 1 in 6 reach a bachelor's. (2) in-state public four-year — about $21,340 a year all-in net; weakness: 71% finish. (3) private nonprofit — about $37,380 net; weakness: price, though 75% finish. (4) borrow to the federal limits — Parent PLUS now stops at $20,000 a year and $65,000 per student; weakness: 9.07% interest and, for new parent borrowers, only a standard repayment plan.
  • B. HOW MUCH FINISHING RISK YOU'LL ACCEPT FOR A HIGHER-PAYING FIELD (e.g. a selective program in a high-paying field vs a surer route in a lower-paying one). Weakness of this framing: completion rates by school and selectivity are mostly about who enrolls, so the setting is the viewer's honest read of their own odds, which no table gives.

The research earned the price tag on each setting. It did not earn a right answer.

Script traps

  • 75% vs 60%. The SF Fed's 75% counts graduate degrees. The bachelor's-only raw premium is about 60% (BLS 2025 $1,578 vs $966; NCES 2024 $74,480 vs $46,380). Label it raw.
  • Zimmerman's 22% is men and free-lunch students; close to zero for women and better-off students; local to the admission margin.
  • Georgia is about 17%, marginally insignificant, household income estimated from credit data. Not "17–20%".
  • Andrews-Li-Lovenheim is not causal. The 80–130% business-major paper is Andrews, Imberman and Lovenheim, and it is a business major vs mostly STEM, not a degree vs none.
  • The causal studies' alternative was mostly community college, not "no college".
  • The SF Fed letter never says the premium "is not an artifact of who goes to college".
  • Blog figures: don't air "pharmacy over $100k, dance under $29k". ED's current file: pharmacy $116,539, dance $39,980.
  • NY Fed by-major figures are 2024 data, released February 2026. Never put them beside the 2026 Q2 5.6% / 42% as the same moment. NY Fed underemployment includes graduate-degree holders.
  • Two cut "facts": "more than 60% of defaulters left with no degree" (not on the cited page) and "health certificates earn $50,000 less than construction" (not in the cited source; the best study points the other way). Neither airs.
  • Flag ≠ rule bar. The flag is per school, federal-aid completers of 2017–19, against "typical high school graduates"; the rule is per program, against working adults 25–34 with only a high-school diploma. Never "the same bar"; never "25–34" about the flag; never say the 2027–28 form displays the flag.
  • A tie passes the 2027 test; graduate programs face the lowest of three medians.
  • Pell: a failing program loses only Direct Loans; the institution-level test can cost all federal aid for those programs. Never "loses Pell after three years".
  • Failure rates: final rule only (bachelor's 1.1% / 0.6% of students; associate 6.0%; certificates 24.1% / 27.6%; all 5.2%). Not 18–29% or 31% (proposal era), not "about 6%". 3,302 and 6,520 are paperwork counts. Table figures were typed by hand from ED's images: re-read the images before airing.
  • Cosmetology's 93% is undergraduate-certificate programs.
  • RAP is bracketed on total income with a $120-a-year floor, not a smooth "1–10%".
  • Parent PLUS consolidation before 2026-07-01 is the PSLF path, needs an IDR plan by 2028-07-01, and is lost on any new Direct Loan.
  • Internships: an odds ratio (48.5% lower odds); raw 54% vs 41%; observational.
  • Two-year completion is about 44% (43.9%), not 44.1%. The 71.2% transfer-finish rate is a different cohort from the 31.6% who transfer.
  • Apprenticeship gain is per quarter, about two years in, in DOL grant programs (IT, healthcare, manufacturing); not per year, not lifetime, not the trades. Completion 46.5% vs 48.2% cancel (FY2024), not "46% (FY2014)".
  • Income quintiles in the completion data are by neighbourhood, not family income.
  • Selectivity completion (30% vs 92%) is same-school and mostly who enrolls.
  • Scorecard: federal-aid recipients only; never compare the 5-year column with the 4-year one (older cohort).
  • Net price: College Board ($21,340, all first-time full-time, 2025–26 estimate) and NCES ($15,200 / $9,700–$24,200, aid recipients, 2021–22) are different measures; never on one chart. College Board's 2023–24 onward figures are projections.

  • Gallup's 31% is opinion, not evidence that degrees don't pay.
