# ep52 — transcript · "Medicaid Work Requirements: Should Coverage Depend on a Job?"

## Cold open
**Narrator:** This is about a condition on health coverage, what it's for, and whether it's doing that. Supporters ask a fair question: if you can work, why shouldn't Medicaid ask something of you?
**Narrator:** From January, every state that expanded Medicaid must ask, unless it wins a delay: eighty hours a month of work, school or volunteering, enough household income, or an exemption.
**Narrator:** Nebraska went first. Its draft minutes count more than five hundred people who lost coverage under the rule in July and August: forty-three found not meeting it, and four hundred and ninety who didn't answer the state's request.
**Narrator:** So who were the four hundred and ninety? Nobody has measured that yet, and by the end, it's yours to weigh.
**Narrator:** One rule for this town. What the record has settled, we build solid. What people still argue about, we build in glass.

## The clash
**Walt:** Maybe there's an envelope from the state on your counter right now, with a date on it in bold. Or maybe you clock in every morning and wonder why anyone who could, shouldn't.
**Walt:** So is this a reasonable ask of anyone able to earn a paycheck, or a trap set for those already earning one?

## The research · The letter: what it asks, and who it reaches
**Lena:** Start with one household, made up but built from the record. A woman in Nebraska, forty, working shifts at a diner, covered through the state's Medicaid expansion. Last month her youngest turned fourteen. Until then, caring for a child thirteen or under excused her. Now the rule reaches her.
**Walt:** What does she have to show?
**Lena:** Eighty hours in a month of work, school, volunteering or a work program, a little under twenty hours a week. Or five hundred and eighty dollars of income that month, counted for her whole household, so a partner's pay counts too. Hours can't be shared.
**Walt:** And how often?
**Lena:** Nebraska asks for one qualifying month a year now. From twenty twenty-seven, renewals come every six months, and she'll need one qualifying month in each six.
**Walt:** Who checks, and how?
**Lena:** The state checks its own records first. They show her pay, not her hours. Volunteering or school she can simply declare on the state's form. Paid work the records can't confirm needs documents, like pay stubs.
**Walt:** And if a letter comes and she misses it?
**Lena:** She gets thirty days to answer. If she doesn't, she can lose her coverage. And someone cut off under this rule can't get a subsidised marketplace plan instead: the law bars the tax credits.
**Walt:** Does this reach everyone on Medicaid?
**Lena:** No. Only adults nineteen to sixty-four who got Medicaid through the expansion or a few named waivers: forty-four places, counting DC. If you're on SSI or Medicare, this requirement doesn't touch you.
**Walt:** And if you're sick?
**Lena:** If you're sick or disabled and covered through the expansion, you're excluded. But the state has to see it, in your medical records or your paperwork. More on that proof later.
**Walt:** And the people it's really aimed at? The ones who aren't working?
**Lena:** They're real too. Nebraska found forty-three who didn't meet the rule in its first two months of renewals. Nationally, in any single month of twenty twenty-two, roughly half of the adults it would reach didn't work eighty hours. Some were in school, sick or caregiving; others couldn't find work or gave other reasons.

## The research · What is it for? The purpose, the 6%, and the budget
**Lena:** Ask its supporters, in their own documents. The federal rule says work and community engagement help people escape isolation and dependency and build confidence. It calls the program a short-term hand up, not a lifetime handout, and puts Medicaid in line with food stamps and cash welfare, which have work rules of their own.
**Walt:** And the people who wrote the bill?
**Lena:** The House report on the bill files it under personal accountability. Most people get insurance through a job, it argues, and Medicare is earned by working. So asking able adults to work, study or serve will promote the dignity of work. That's the House bill's report, not the law's text.
**Walt:** People also hear that six percent of Medicaid is fraud. Is that right?
**Lena:** Six percent was improper, and improper isn't fraud. In fiscal twenty twenty-five, about thirty-seven billion federal dollars, about six cents of every federal Medicaid dollar, were paid improperly. Most was missing paperwork, which the agency says generally isn't a sign of fraud. About one cent went to people or services found ineligible.
**Walt:** Thirty-seven billion is still real money. Does the work rule go after it?
**Lena:** Not by the agency's own math: it counts the rule's savings as people leaving the rolls, times what each one costs. The law's error fix is a separate section. From twenty thirty, it tightens an old penalty on states whose error rates top three percent: about seven and a half billion over ten years.
**Walt:** Then the big one people ask: is this paying for the tax cuts?
**Lena:** Here's the arithmetic. The same law also cut taxes. By the budget office's standard count, over ten years it lowers revenue by about four and a half trillion dollars and cuts spending by about one trillion, most of it Medicaid.
**Walt:** So the Medicaid cuts pay for them?
**Lena:** Partly offset them. The cuts cover about a quarter of the revenue the law gives up, and it still adds about three point four trillion dollars to deficits.
**Walt:** Is that link written down anywhere?
**Lena:** In the budget resolution behind the law. Its rule let the room for tax cuts grow or shrink by however far the spending cuts beat their target or fell short of it. That's a rule on paper, not anyone's stated motive.
**Walt:** And who comes out ahead?
**Lena:** In the budget office's analysis of the law, averaged over twenty twenty-six to twenty thirty-four, the poorest tenth of households loses about twelve hundred dollars a year, mainly from Medicaid and food stamps. The richest tenth gains about thirteen thousand six hundred, mainly from lower taxes.

## The research · Is it doing that? Nebraska, Arkansas, and the work
**Lena:** The state's draft minutes count seven thousand two hundred and eighty renewals in July and August that needed a decision. Fifty-seven percent were exempt. Thirty-four percent qualified, nearly all by working eighty hours or reaching five hundred and eighty dollars of income. Two percent got a hardship pass. Seven percent lost coverage.
**Walt:** And who were the seven percent?
**Lena:** More than five hundred people. About eleven in every twelve lost coverage because they didn't respond to the state's request. Forty-three were found not meeting the rule.
**Walt:** So were the four hundred and ninety working?
**Lena:** Nobody has measured it. The state says it's too early to determine trends in the data, and it hasn't published how many cleared on records alone, or how appeals went. Some may have qualified and missed the mail. Some may have had other coverage or no longer qualified. Nobody knows the split.
**Walt:** Has anyone run this long enough to see what happens?
**Lena:** Arkansas, in twenty eighteen. It closed more than eighteen thousand cases for not meeting or not reporting the rule. Among people who actually had to report, eighty-eight percent didn't report eighty hours.
**Walt:** Did people even know about it?
**Lena:** A third of enrolled adults in their thirties and forties hadn't heard of the rule at all. And for most of that year, the only way to report was a website.
**Walt:** And did the people who stayed on work more?
**Lena:** No sign of it. In a survey study of Arkansas adults thirty to forty-nine, coverage fell about thirteen points in twenty eighteen and the share uninsured rose about seven, with no detectable change in work. Georgia's version found no employment gain either. The ranges are wide: no sign of more work, not proof of none.
**Walt:** Food stamps have had work rules for years. What do those show?
**Lena:** Studies built on states' own records find no employment gain from them. Virginia's rules out a rise of more than three and a half points. Two survey-based studies find a small gain, or one for the least-educated adults.
**Walt:** So has any work rule ever raised work?
**Lena:** One kind did. In the nineteen-nineties, welfare-to-work trials for single parents, with job search, training or earnings support attached, raised employment modestly, about four points on average, against the old cash program. Family income usually didn't rise.
