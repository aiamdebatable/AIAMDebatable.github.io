Medicaid Work Requirements: Should Coverage Depend on a Job?

Should getting Medicaid depend on working?

Last changed 2026-11-08 · See every change

The verdictYour dialNumbers you heardWhat the fact-check caughtUp next

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

Medicaid work rules are here. In the first state, most who lost coverage didn't answer a letter. What is the rule for, and is it doing that?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

Since October, Montana can deny or drop expansion coverage for adults who don't show 80 hours a month of work, school or volunteering, or the household income that counts instead, and aren't exempt, joining Nebraska, which started in May. Every other state that expanded Medicaid has to start by January 1, 2027, unless the federal government grants it a delay.

Settled

What both sides accept

Both teams accepted the same record. The rule alone hasn't been shown to raise work, and nobody has shown Nebraska's 490 were working. In any single month of 2022, roughly half the adults it reaches didn't work 80 hours. Both want the records to do the checking. They split on the letter: Gold would keep it as the backstop, Green would stop a missed letter from costing anyone their coverage.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesA test of the mailbox, not the job

In Nebraska's first two months of renewals, 490 people lost coverage for not answering the state's request and 43 for not meeting the rule: about eleven of every twelve losses. The records see earnings, not hours, so for the volunteer, the gig driver or anyone they miss, the test is a form on time, and the agency's own model expects some people who meet the rules to lose coverage anyway. No study found a rule like this made people work more, and anyone cut off can't get marketplace help instead.

▶ Hear Green at 3:32 on YouTube
Gold arguesThe condition is the point

Most people get insurance through work and Medicare is earned by work, so asking an adult with no child under 14, who isn't frail, for a little under 20 hours a week of work, school or service is fair. Most people pass: in Nebraska, more than nine in ten renewals were approved, as exempt or qualified, and Georgia's paperwork share fell to 2.3% once its program was running. The deal is popular, with 62% support in one national poll, so keep the condition and fix the letter: check the records first, every time.

▶ Hear Gold at 6:35 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

This sounds like a fight over whether people should have to work for coverage. But in Nebraska, most renewals the rule reached were excused or already qualified, and the check removed 43 for not meeting it and 490 for not answering. The records can see a paycheck, not hours, so for anyone they miss, the test is a letter and thirty days. So is this checking your job, or your mailbox? And if it's the mailbox, is that a flaw to fix, or the point?

Still argued · your call

Your dial: What should keeping Medicaid ask of an adult who can work?

The judge left one dial: what keeping Medicaid should ask of an adult who can work. These are the five settings the judge named along it, from asking nothing extra to checking at the front door, and nobody was declared the winner. The judge said settings two and three are where the teams nearly meet. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Nothing extra
    What it buys
    Medicaid with no work condition, the way states ran expansion before this rule. Nobody loses coverage over a work-rule request they missed: in Nebraska's first two months, that was 490 of the 533 who lost coverage under the rule. And it gives up no measured gain in work: no study found a rule like this, on its own, made people work more.
    What it costs
    But on a one-month test, about half didn't work eighty hours, the gap supporters point to: in any single month of 2022, between about 45% and 55%, depending on who's counted. Some were in school, sick or caregiving; others couldn't find work or gave other reasons. And on whether Medicaid should carry a duty at all, evidence can't decide, and majorities say it should.

    On the record: row D8 of the fact-check log, row D7 of the fact-check log, row B9 of the fact-check log, row E1 of the fact-check log, row C20 of the fact-check log

  2. 2Let the data check it
    What it buys
    Keep a condition, but confirm it from records and ask only what they can't show, as the rule already requires first. If her pay shows up in the records, she clears without lifting a finger. The agency expects 56% of about 20 million people to clear that way, and in 2018 Arkansas excused about two-thirds by data match. Both teams want the records to do the checking.
    What it costs
    But records see earnings, not hours, and Arkansas still closed eighteen thousand cases after excusing two-thirds by data match. The new federal data match lists no hours source at all, so for the volunteer, the gig driver, anyone the records miss, the test is a form, on time.
    Not measured
    How many people clear on records alone: no state running it now has published its share, and whether a records-first design can shrink the paperwork losses is still open.

    On the record: row A20 of the fact-check log, row B6 of the fact-check log, row E34 of the fact-check log, row A34 of the fact-check log, row E18 of the fact-check log

  3. 3Ask once, keep it light
    What it buys
    The federal floor: one qualifying month per review, household income counts, thirty days to answer, the way Nebraska runs it. That month needs eighty hours of work, school, volunteering or a work program, or $580 of household income, so a partner's pay counts too. Volunteering or school can simply be declared on Nebraska's form. In its first two months, more than nine in ten renewals were approved, as exempt or qualified, and 43 were found not meeting the rule.
    What it costs
    But its first two months lost four hundred and ninety people who didn't answer, with no plans to add staff. From 2027, renewals come every six months, with one qualifying month needed in each six.
    Not measured
    Whether Nebraska's 490 qualified: some may have qualified and missed the request, some may have had other coverage or no longer qualified, and nobody knows the split.

    On the record: row E1 of the fact-check log, row W9 of the fact-check log, row T4-26 of the fact-check log, row E12 of the fact-check log, row T4-36 of the fact-check log

  4. 4Ask, and pay for the help
    What it buys
    The work rule bundled with job search and training, like the nineteen-nineties welfare-to-work trials. That is the one kind of work rule the record shows lifting employment: modestly, about four points on average across a few of those trials, against the old cash program.
    What it costs
    But only three of eleven of those programs raised employment by more than three points over five years, and family income usually didn't rise. Those trials were for single parents on cash welfare in the 1990s, not today's Medicaid adults.
    Not measured
    Whether a requirement with help attached would lift employment today: the evidence is 1990s cash welfare.

    On the record: row C1 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row C2 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row C16 of the fact-check log, row C14 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected)

  5. 5Check at the front door
    What it buys
    Prove it when you apply, then stop re-checking: once you're in, no work-rule request at each renewal to miss. Indiana will ask applicants for three months running, the most the law allows.
    What it costs
    But Nebraska's first applicants show the same pattern: more than eight in ten of their work-rule denials were for not answering, 466 of 557 from May to August.

    On the record: row T4-23 of the fact-check log, row A30 of the fact-check log, row E6 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pick: Whichever setting keeps a condition, a cut leaves no marketplace help: someone cut off under this rule can't get a subsidised marketplace plan instead, because the law bars the tax credits.

What our fact-check caught

37 of the 294 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidSix percent of Medicaid is fraud.

    The record saysAbout 6% of federal Medicaid payments in fiscal 2025, about $37 billion, were paid improperly, and improper isn't fraud. Most was missing paperwork, which the agency says generally isn't a sign of fraud. About one cent of every federal dollar went to people or services found ineligible.

  2. Draft saidThe Medicaid cuts pay for the tax cuts.

    The record saysThey partly offset them. Over ten years the law lowers revenue by about $4.5 trillion and cuts spending by about $1.1 trillion, so the cuts cover about a quarter and the law adds about $3.4 trillion to deficits. A budget rule tied the room for tax cuts to the size of the spending cuts: a rule on paper, not anyone's stated motive.

  3. Draft saidIn Nebraska, 7% of people renewing lost coverage for not meeting the work rule.

    The record says7% lost coverage under the rule, but most for not answering the state's request: 490 of 533. Only 43 were found not meeting it. Nobody has measured whether the 490 were working.

  4. Draft saidYou have to earn $580 a month yourself to count.

    The record saysThe $580 is your household's income, so a partner's pay or Social Security can count. Hours can't be shared, but if the income clears the month, that's enough by itself.

  5. Draft saidA court put the Medicaid work requirement on hold.

    The record saysOn October 29, a federal court struck one extra test from the rule, so a sick adult now only has to fit one of the frailty groups. The requirement itself and its January start are untouched. As of early November, the government hadn't said whether it will appeal.
