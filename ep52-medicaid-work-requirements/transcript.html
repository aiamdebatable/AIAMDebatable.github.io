ep52 — transcript · "Medicaid Work Requirements: Should Coverage Depend on a Job?"

Cold open

NarratorThis is about a condition on health coverage, what it's for, and whether it's doing that. Supporters ask a fair question: if you can work, why shouldn't Medicaid ask something of you?

NarratorFrom January, every state that expanded Medicaid must ask, unless it wins a delay: eighty hours a month of work, school or volunteering, enough household income, or an exemption.

NarratorNebraska went first. Its draft minutes count more than five hundred people who lost coverage under the rule in July and August: forty-three found not meeting it, and four hundred and ninety who didn't answer the state's request.

NarratorSo who were the four hundred and ninety? Nobody has measured that yet, and by the end, it's yours to weigh.

NarratorOne rule for this town. What the record has settled, we build solid. What people still argue about, we build in glass.

The clash

WaltMaybe there's an envelope from the state on your counter right now, with a date on it in bold. Or maybe you clock in every morning and wonder why anyone who could, shouldn't.

WaltSo is this a reasonable ask of anyone able to earn a paycheck, or a trap set for those already earning one?

The research · The letter: what it asks, and who it reaches

LenaStart with one household, made up but built from the record. A woman in Nebraska, forty, working shifts at a diner, covered through the state's Medicaid expansion. Last month her youngest turned fourteen. Until then, caring for a child thirteen or under excused her. Now the rule reaches her.

WaltWhat does she have to show?

LenaEighty hours in a month of work, school, volunteering or a work program, a little under twenty hours a week. Or five hundred and eighty dollars of income that month, counted for her whole household, so a partner's pay counts too. Hours can't be shared.

WaltAnd how often?

LenaNebraska asks for one qualifying month a year now. From twenty twenty-seven, renewals come every six months, and she'll need one qualifying month in each six.

WaltWho checks, and how?

LenaThe state checks its own records first. They show her pay, not her hours. Volunteering or school she can simply declare on the state's form. Paid work the records can't confirm needs documents, like pay stubs.

WaltAnd if a letter comes and she misses it?

LenaShe gets thirty days to answer. If she doesn't, she can lose her coverage. And someone cut off under this rule can't get a subsidised marketplace plan instead: the law bars the tax credits.

WaltDoes this reach everyone on Medicaid?

LenaNo. Only adults nineteen to sixty-four who got Medicaid through the expansion or a few named waivers: forty-four places, counting DC. If you're on SSI or Medicare, this requirement doesn't touch you.

WaltAnd if you're sick?

LenaIf you're sick or disabled and covered through the expansion, you're excluded. But the state has to see it, in your medical records or your paperwork. More on that proof later.

WaltAnd the people it's really aimed at? The ones who aren't working?

LenaThey're real too. Nebraska found forty-three who didn't meet the rule in its first two months of renewals. Nationally, in any single month of twenty twenty-two, roughly half of the adults it would reach didn't work eighty hours. Some were in school, sick or caregiving; others couldn't find work or gave other reasons.

The ringmaster

WaltErin, she's working shifts. Why should she be worried?

The debate · Team Green · A TEST OF THE MAILBOX, NOT THE JOB — opens

ErinBecause for her, this isn't a test of the job. If the state's records clear her, fine. If they miss her, it's a letter and thirty days, and in Nebraska, that letter is where eleven of every twelve people who lost coverage lost it.

ErinAnd the agency that wrote the rule expects this. Its own model assumes some people who are working, studying or excused will still lose coverage over paperwork. That's not my guess. It's built into their forecast.

The ringmaster

WaltLena, step back. What's this rule meant to accomplish?

The research · What is it for? The purpose, the 6%, and the budget

LenaAsk its supporters, in their own documents. The federal rule says work and community engagement help people escape isolation and dependency and build confidence. It calls the program a short-term hand up, not a lifetime handout, and puts Medicaid in line with food stamps and cash welfare, which have work rules of their own.

WaltAnd the people who wrote the bill?

LenaThe House report on the bill files it under personal accountability. Most people get insurance through a job, it argues, and Medicare is earned by working. So asking able adults to work, study or serve will promote the dignity of work. That's the House bill's report, not the law's text.

WaltPeople also hear that six percent of Medicaid is fraud. Is that right?

LenaSix percent was improper, and improper isn't fraud. In fiscal twenty twenty-five, about thirty-seven billion federal dollars, about six cents of every federal Medicaid dollar, were paid improperly. Most was missing paperwork, which the agency says generally isn't a sign of fraud. About one cent went to people or services found ineligible.

WaltThirty-seven billion is still real money. Does the work rule go after it?

LenaNot by the agency's own math: it counts the rule's savings as people leaving the rolls, times what each one costs. The law's error fix is a separate section. From twenty thirty, it tightens an old penalty on states whose error rates top three percent: about seven and a half billion over ten years.

WaltThen the big one people ask: is this paying for the tax cuts?

LenaHere's the arithmetic. The same law also cut taxes. By the budget office's standard count, over ten years it lowers revenue by about four and a half trillion dollars and cuts spending by about one trillion, most of it Medicaid.

WaltSo the Medicaid cuts pay for them?

LenaPartly offset them. The cuts cover about a quarter of the revenue the law gives up, and it still adds about three point four trillion dollars to deficits.

WaltIs that link written down anywhere?

LenaIn the budget resolution behind the law. Its rule let the room for tax cuts grow or shrink by however far the spending cuts beat their target or fell short of it. That's a rule on paper, not anyone's stated motive.

WaltAnd who comes out ahead?

LenaIn the budget office's analysis of the law, averaged over twenty twenty-six to twenty thirty-four, the poorest tenth of households loses about twelve hundred dollars a year, mainly from Medicaid and food stamps. The richest tenth gains about thirteen thousand six hundred, mainly from lower taxes.

The ringmaster

WaltThat's the math, Cole. What does your side call it?

The debate · Team Gold · THE CONDITION IS THE POINT — responds

ColeAs spending restraint, which is how the people who wrote the budget described it. Their report says the deficit comes from excessive spending, not insufficient revenue. You can disagree with that, but both readings are arithmetic. And a condition on Medicaid was never mainly about the money.

ColeIt's about the deal. Most people get their insurance through work, and Medicare is earned by work. Asking an adult with no child under fourteen, who isn't frail, for a little under twenty hours a week of work, school or service is fair. That's the dignity of work the bill's report talks about.

ColeAnd the improper money matters. Six billion dollars in a year, paid for people or services found ineligible, is money that could have gone to someone who qualifies. Yes, most of the thirty-seven billion is missing paperwork. But a check at every renewal makes a state document who qualifies, and that's the point.

The ringmaster

WaltLena, so is it doing what it's for? Start with Nebraska's renewals.

The research · Is it doing that? Nebraska, Arkansas, and the work

LenaThe state's draft minutes count seven thousand two hundred and eighty renewals in July and August that needed a decision. Fifty-seven percent were exempt. Thirty-four percent qualified, nearly all by working eighty hours or reaching five hundred and eighty dollars of income. Two percent got a hardship pass. Seven percent lost coverage.

WaltAnd who were the seven percent?

LenaMore than five hundred people. About eleven in every twelve lost coverage because they didn't respond to the state's request. Forty-three were found not meeting the rule.

WaltSo were the four hundred and ninety working?

LenaNobody has measured it. The state says it's too early to determine trends in the data, and it hasn't published how many cleared on records alone, or how appeals went. Some may have qualified and missed the mail. Some may have had other coverage or no longer qualified. Nobody knows the split.

WaltHas anyone run this long enough to see what happens?

LenaArkansas, in twenty eighteen. It closed more than eighteen thousand cases for not meeting or not reporting the rule. Among people who actually had to report, eighty-eight percent didn't report eighty hours.

WaltDid people even know about it?

LenaA third of enrolled adults in their thirties and forties hadn't heard of the rule at all. And for most of that year, the only way to report was a website.

WaltAnd did the people who stayed on work more?

LenaNo sign of it. In a survey study of Arkansas adults thirty to forty-nine, coverage fell about thirteen points in twenty eighteen and the share uninsured rose about seven, with no detectable change in work. Georgia's version found no employment gain either. The ranges are wide: no sign of more work, not proof of none.

WaltFood stamps have had work rules for years. What do those show?

LenaStudies built on states' own records find no employment gain from them. Virginia's rules out a rise of more than three and a half points. Two survey-based studies find a small gain, or one for the least-educated adults.

WaltSo has any work rule ever raised work?

LenaOne kind did. In the nineteen-nineties, welfare-to-work trials for single parents, with job search, training or earnings support attached, raised employment modestly, about four points on average, against the old cash program. Family income usually didn't rise.

The ringmaster

WaltErin, nothing here shows more work. Does that settle it for you?

ErinIt tells you what the check can't do. Arkansas closed eighteen thousand cases, and no study found more employment. Yet a survey there found nearly ninety-seven percent of adults in their thirties and forties already met the rule or should have been exempt. That's everyone surveyed, not just the people cut.

ErinSo I'm not saying nobody should be asked. I'm saying this check, as built, mostly measures who opens the mail.

WaltThe records can see a paycheck, not hours. So for anyone they miss, the test is a letter and thirty days.

The research · What the check costs, the proof, and your questions

LenaThe agency's own estimate first. People sending proof at renewal will spend about thirty-five million hours a year on it, about four hours each, valued at about four hundred and fifty-five million dollars. Building the state computer systems runs about one and a half billion over ten years, and that's systems only.

WaltCan't the records do more of it?

LenaThat's the design. A state has to try its records first, and the agency expects fifty-six percent of about twenty million people to clear that way. In twenty eighteen, Arkansas excused about two-thirds by data match. But no state running it now has published how many clear automatically.

WaltDoes the paperwork cost come down once it's running?

LenaIn Georgia's version, about two-thirds of everything spent through early twenty twenty-five went to administration, mostly start-up. By then, the running share was two point three percent. Georgia also enrolled far fewer than it planned: under thirty-five hundred full-year enrollees in its first fifteen months, against more than twenty-five thousand projected.

WaltYou said you'd come back to the sick. How do they prove it?

LenaThe state checks a year of medical claims first. If nothing shows, it depends on your state. Nebraska takes your own word, with no doctor's note. Montana asks current members for a form signed with their provider.

WaltAnd if you're waiting on Social Security?

LenaOn our reading of the rule, a pending claim isn't an exemption by itself, and no award is needed either: the state decides. A first Social Security decision takes about six months on average, about as long as one renewal cycle.

WaltDidn't a court just change some of this?

LenaOn October twenty-ninth, a federal court struck one extra test from the rule, so now fitting one of the frailty groups is enough. The requirement itself and its January start are untouched, and as of early November the government hadn't said whether it will appeal.

WaltPeople ask about big employers, too. Isn't Walmart keeping its workers on Medicaid?

LenaIn six states that gave federal auditors employer data, Walmart was the top employer of working Medicaid adults in three, but at most about one worker in twenty. The same report found they're more likely than other workers to be at small firms: about four in ten work where there are fewer than twenty-five staff.

WaltAnd if you work more and earn too much? Is there a cliff?

LenaA step, not a cliff. In twenty twenty-six, one adult's Medicaid line is about twenty-two thousand dollars a year. Just over it, the basic marketplace plan costs about three and a half percent of income, roughly sixty-five dollars a month in one example. Before the extra help expired after twenty twenty-five, it cost nothing.

WaltAnd people who do lose coverage, where do they end up?

LenaThe record is mixed. In the big renewal wave that began in twenty twenty-three, one survey found seven in ten people who'd been dropped went uninsured at least for a while, and about a quarter still were when asked. A Federal Reserve staff study found no significant national rise in the uninsured.

ErinNot yet. Start with what hurts my side. Nobody has shown Nebraska's four hundred and ninety were working, and in twenty twenty-two, roughly half the adults this reaches didn't put in eighty hours in a given month. That gap is real, and I won't wave it away.

ErinBut the rule's own text says its records see earnings, not hours, and the new federal data match lists no hours source at all. So for the volunteer, the gig driver, anyone the records miss, the test is a form, on time.

ErinAnd the cost lands on people with no net under them. Thirty-five million hours a year of their time, by the agency's own count. No plans for new staff in Nebraska for twice-a-year checks. And if you're cut, no marketplace help to catch you.

ErinThink of the woman at the diner. On Nebraska's numbers so far, if she loses coverage, it's far likelier to be over an envelope than over her shifts. Let the records do the checking, and stop ending coverage over a missed request.

The debate · Team Gold · THE CONDITION IS THE POINT

ColeOn work. The rule alone hasn't been shown to raise it: not in Arkansas, not in Georgia, not in the food-stamp records. The gains we know of came in the nineteen-nineties, with help attached, and family incomes mostly didn't rise. I won't tell you this letter gets anyone a job.

ColeAnd four hundred and ninety lost coverage over paperwork. That's a design problem, and the rule designs against it: records first, thirty days to answer, coverage kept meanwhile, a ninety-day way back, and no lock-out. A sick adult now only has to fit one of the frailty groups.

ColeHere's the case, then. Think of the woman at the diner. If her pay shows up in the records, she clears without lifting a finger. Most people pass: in Nebraska, more than nine in ten renewals were approved, as exempt or qualified. The condition isn't a trap. Most people already meet it.

ColeAnd most Americans back the deal. Sixty-two percent supported it in one national poll, and sixty-eight percent backed a version with exceptions for caregivers and disability. Different questions, so not a trend. But both are clear majorities.

ColeSo keep the condition, and fix the letter. Check the records first, every time. Make the forms simple and the reminders loud. Georgia's administration share fell to two point three percent once it was running. The cost is up front. The condition is the point.

ErinBut the support moves with the argument. In KFF's polling, support fell to about a third after people heard the paperwork argument, and rose past three in four after the supporters' case. And Georgia's cheap paperwork came with a program that enrolled a fraction of what it planned.

ErinWhere Cole and I split is smaller than it sounds. We both want the records to do the checking. He'd keep the letter as the backstop. I'd stop a missed letter from costing anyone their coverage.

The verdict

RajNo winner here. What's settled: the rule alone hasn't been shown to raise work. In Nebraska, almost everyone who lost coverage didn't answer, rather than being found idle. The records see earnings, not hours. And in any single month of twenty twenty-two, roughly half the adults it reaches didn't work eighty hours.

RajStill open: whether Nebraska's four hundred and ninety qualified, and whether a records-first design can shrink the paperwork losses. On the evidence about work, the record leans toward Green. On whether Medicaid should carry a duty at all, evidence can't decide, and majorities say it should.

RajGreen's best ground: the check mostly caught people who didn't answer, the agency's own model expects people who meet the rules to lose coverage anyway, and a cut leaves no marketplace help. Gold's best ground: most people pass or are excused, Georgia's paperwork share fell once it ran, and the improper money is real.

RajWhich leaves the dial: what should keeping Medicaid ask of an adult who can work? Nothing extra: Medicaid with no work condition, the way states ran expansion before this rule. But on a one-month test, about half didn't work eighty hours, the gap supporters point to.

RajLet the data check it: keep a condition, but confirm it from records and ask only what they can't show, as the rule already requires first. But records see earnings, not hours, and Arkansas still closed eighteen thousand cases after excusing two-thirds by data match.

RajAsk once, keep it light: the federal floor, one qualifying month per review, household income counts, thirty days to answer, the way Nebraska runs it. But its first two months lost four hundred and ninety people who didn't answer, with no plans to add staff. Settings two and three are where the teams nearly meet.

RajAsk, and pay for the help: the work rule bundled with job search and training, like the nineteen-nineties welfare-to-work trials. But only three of eleven of those programs raised employment by more than three points over five years, and family income usually didn't rise.

RajCheck at the front door: prove it when you apply, as Indiana will ask applicants for three months running, then stop re-checking. But Nebraska's first applicants show the same pattern: more than eight in ten of their work-rule denials were for not answering. Where it belongs is your call.
