## The pitch, in one line

**"Starting January 1, 2027, keeping Medicaid will mean showing 80 hours of work, school or volunteering
(or the household income that counts instead, T4-26) in the month or months the state checks. In the first state to
enforce the new federal rule, 43 people lost coverage because they were found not to meet it, and 490
because they didn't answer the state's request. What is this rule for, and is it doing that?"** (His
ruling 2026-11-02: purpose first; the earlier ending, "So is it checking your job, or your mailbox?", is now
the mid-episode turn.) (E1; the state's draft minutes, E17; the check is 1 or more months per review, the
state choosing: A30, E10, E12; Arkansas enforced an earlier version in 2018: B1; the request is a notice
with 30 days to answer: A32.) In a single month of 2022, roughly half of working-age adults on Medicaid
worked 80 hours (D8: KFF 53%, June, expansion states only; AEI 44%, December, all states; approximate),
which is the gap supporters point to; no study of a Medicaid work rule has found
people working more because of it (B9, B16, B23), and the food-stamp studies built on state records rule
out more than a small gain (C6, C9); and the federal rule's own text says the data it checks see earnings, not hours (E34).

## The question

**Should getting Medicaid depend on working?** The debate it stages, as the record shapes it: **is the
requirement a test of work or a test of paperwork, and if it's paperwork, is that a fixable flaw or the
whole point?** (Working side labels below.)

**The law and the date.** A 2025 federal law (Public Law 119-21, section 71119, which adds section
1902(xx) to the Social Security Act) makes Medicaid for adults 19 to 64 in the expansion group conditional
on 80 hours a month of work, school, volunteering or a work programme, or on monthly earnings of at least
$580 (80 hours at the $7.25 federal minimum wage), unless they are exempt (A30, A31). An applicant shows 1
to 3 months before applying; an enrollee shows at least 1 month per review, the state choosing how many
(A30). (U.S. Government Publishing Office, "Public Law 119-21",
https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ21/html/PLAW-119publ21.htm.) The federal health agency's
interim final rule says every state must start **no later than January 1, 2027** (Centers for Medicare &
Medicaid Services, "Medicaid Program; Community Engagement Requirement for Certain Individuals," Federal
Register 91 FR 33348, 2026-06-03,
https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/03/2026-11094/medicaid-program-community-engagement-requirement-for-certain-individuals).
✎ A state that shows a good-faith effort can be exempted **6 months at a time, never past December 31,
2028** (A23, E45): the end of 2028 is a ceiling, not a promise, and the agency's own planning assumption
is that about 10 states ask and about 2 are approved (A22).

**Two states moved first.** Nebraska began on May 1, 2026 (the same rule); ✎ the federal agency approved
Nebraska's state plan amendment on September 4, four months after enforcement began (E11). Montana began
reviewing applications and renewals on July 1, 2026, removed no one through September, and says that
**starting in October 2026** people who do not meet the requirement "may be denied coverage or
disenrolled" (E19) (Montana Department of Public Health and Human Services, "Changes to Medicaid:
Frequently Asked Questions," https://dphhs.mt.gov/medicaidchanges/faqs). ✎ Montana has published no count
of anyone denied or removed (E23). Iowa starts December 1, 2026 (E28); Arkansas has run a "soft launch"
since July 1 with no penalties before January 1, 2027 (E25).

**The first numbers frame the episode's real fight.** ✎ The peg hunt could only read these through
Georgetown and KFF; hand pass E found and read **the state's own document**: Nebraska's Department of
Health and Human Services, Medicaid Advisory Committee, **draft** minutes of September 17, 2026
(https://dhhs.ne.gov/Documents/DRAFT%20MAC%20Meeting%20Minutes%209.17.26.pdf; due for approval on November
12, E17). Of **7,280** expansion-adult renewals in July and August that needed a work-rule decision,
**533 lost coverage: 490 for not responding to the request for information, 43 found not meeting the
rule** (E1). Of the 2,449 who qualified through an activity, 2,368, about 97%, did it **by working 80 hours
or earning $580** (E2). ✎ Not "97% passed the income test": that wording is Georgetown's and the peg hunt

https://ccf.georgetown.edu/2026/09/30/nebraska-releases-first-data-on-medicaid-work-reporting-requirement-as-expected-coverage-losses-start-to-mount-due-to-red-tape/;

https://www.kff.org/medicaid/early-medicaid-work-requirement-insights-from-nebraska/ (KFF's body text

## Topic shape (the guardrail verdict)

**Lopsided on whether the requirement, by itself, makes people work more; genuinely two-sided on whether
the paperwork losses are a fixable flaw or the mechanism, and on the value question nobody's data can
settle.**

- **Lopsided: "does it raise work?"** The SNAP studies that use the states' own records find no
  employment gain (C9's corrected wording), and Virginia's rules out gains above 3.5 points (C6). The
  survey studies of Medicaid find none either, with wide error bars: Arkansas's employment estimate is −1.1 points with a 95% interval of −8.7 to +6.5
  (B9); Georgia's is −1.6 (−8.7 to +5.4) (B23); an independent study of Arkansas on federal survey data finds
  −0.7 (B16). The supporters' real evidence is 1990s welfare-to-work programmes, where a mandate came
  **with services or earnings incentives**, against the old cash programme, and raised employment
  modestly, about 4 points on average, without usually raising family income (C1–C3, C14, C16), plus a
  gain for one disadvantaged subgroup in one survey-based SNAP study (C10). **The verdict says plainly:
  the record found no sign that the requirement alone makes people work more.** It never says "proved it
  doesn't work": the Medicaid studies' error bars are wide (B9, B23).
- **Settled as a count: who lost coverage in the first state.** 490 of 533 for not answering, 43 found
  not meeting it (E1). Not in dispute.
- **Genuinely two-sided (1): who are the people who didn't answer?** Nobody has measured it in Nebraska
  (E8, E15, E18). Some evidence says many qualified (Arkansas's survey: nearly 97% of ALL surveyed adults
  30–49 already met the rule or should have been exempt, B10; a share of everyone surveyed, not a
  measurement of the people removed; the federal agency's own model expects people who
  meet the rules to lose coverage over paperwork, A17). Some says not all did (the agency's own list of
  reasons includes improper enrollment and people with other coverage, A17; in Arkansas, a quarter of a
  small sample of leavers were uninsured, the rest re-enrolled or covered elsewhere, B13).
- **Genuinely two-sided (2): can design make the paperwork loss small?** The rule requires a data check
  first (A28), the agency expects 56% to clear on data (A20), and Arkansas cleared about two-thirds by
  data in 2018 (B6). But the data see earnings, not hours (E34), Arkansas still closed 18,164 cases (B1),
  and no live state has published how many clear automatically (E18).
- **Not a question of evidence: should a public health benefit carry a duty?** Reciprocity, "a hand
  up", escaping "isolation and dependency" and "the dignity of work" (C30, C31, T2-14, T2-17) against
  coverage as health care. Public support moves both ways with the argument heard
  (C21, C22, C25). The episode says this is a value judgement and hands it over.

**Rejected framing:** "Does it make people work?" as the spine. The record is lopsided there, so Gold
would be the straw side. It becomes one beat.

## The spine (tested against the record)

**Candidate (main session): "Is it testing work, or paperwork?"** Verdict: **it holds as the journey, with
one refinement the record forces: it tests earnings and the mailbox, and nobody yet knows whether the
people who didn't answer were working.** Proposed on-air form: **"Is it checking your job, or your
mailbox?"**

What supports it:
- **Nebraska:** 490 of 533 losses were non-response, 43 non-compliance (E1). The state says it is "too
  early to determine trends in the data" (E8, its wording, not "too early to tell").
- **The rule's own words:** quarterly wage data carry earnings, not hours; not all work can be verified
  from financial data (E34). The federal hub match lists no hours source (E35). Nebraska requires
  "primary source documents" for work the data can't confirm (E10, E36).
- **Arkansas 2018:** among those who actually had to report, 88% did not report 80 hours in a month
  (B5's KFF figure, February 2019); a third of enrollees aged 30–49 had heard nothing about the rule (B10); for
  most of 2018 the only way to report was a website (B11).
- **The agency's own model** assumes 7% of people who are working, studying or exempt lose coverage for
  administrative or procedural reasons (A17, APPROXIMATE: its reasons also include improper enrolment
  and other coverage).

The supporters' answer, built from the record (equal weight):
- **Data first is the rule.** States must try existing data before asking anyone (A28); the agency
  expects 56% of about 20 million to clear on data (A20); Arkansas excused about two-thirds by data match
  in 2018 (B6); Nebraska, Montana and Arkansas all check data first (E10, E20, E27); household income of $580
  counts without any hours log (A31, T4-26).
- **The paperwork cost is a start-up cost.** Georgia's administration share fell from 100% in its first
  years to 2.3% in the first half of fiscal 2025 (B20).
- **Mandates with services did raise work** in the 1990s (C1–C3, C14), modestly.
- **Majorities support the deal**: 62% (KFF, February 2025), 68% for a version with caregiver and
  disability exceptions (June 2025), 81% of registered voters in a supporters' poll with "able-bodied"
  wording (C20, C22, C28).
- **The numbers on who works are shared, not partisan:** the federal agency's own 12% noncompliance rate
  is built from KFF's survey data (D12).
- **Non-response is not proof of wrongful loss:** the agency's reasons list includes improper enrolment
  and other coverage (A17; an expectation in its model, with no count, T2-15); no Nebraska appeal counts
  exist (E15).

**Why not a different spine.** "Who's really on Medicaid?" (the work gap) is real (D8) but settles fast:
roughly half worked 80 hours in a month, and the rest split among school, illness, caregiving and a
residual (D1). It is a beat. "What does it save?" puts two models that can't be blended at the centre
(A10, A18). The mailbox spine is the one both teams have to answer, and it is where the viewer lives.

## The viewer's moment, and the cold open

**The viewer's moment (from the record only): the renewal letter in the mailbox.** From 2027, expansion
adults renew every 6 months and the work rule is checked at each renewal (A29, E12). A notice of
noncompliance gives **30 days**, counted as received **5 days after its date** (A32). The person's
paycheck shows earnings, not hours (E34); if household income reaches **$580** in a month, that month counts (A31, T4-26);
if not, hours need paperwork (E10, E36).

**The cold open's raw material** (≤ ~100 words on air, few rounded numbers; the writer owns the words; the
house lines vary):
1. **Premise first:** this is about whether health coverage should come with a condition: 80 hours a
   month of work, school or volunteering. From January 1, 2027, in every state that expanded Medicaid, it
   will (A23 caveat: unless the state wins a delay).
2. **The object, Nebraska's first two months:** about 7,300 renewals checked; 533 people lost coverage;
   43 because the state found they didn't meet the rule; 490 because they didn't answer (E1).
3. **The turn:** "So is it checking your job, or your mailbox?"
4. **The journey line:** what the requirement does, who it reaches, whether anyone works more, what it
   costs, and the question no number answers: should it ask at all. The viewer is the jury.

⚠ "The state's draft minutes" until the final is approved (E17). Never "the state proved most were
eligible" (E8, E15).

**The household (after the cold open; invented and unnamed, built only from figures in the log).** An adult
in Montana, 40, working restaurant shifts, renewing her coverage:
- **The hours.** She needs 80 hours, or $580 of household income, in a month (A31, T4-26), a little under 20 hours a week
  (W2, our arithmetic). Montana asks for proof covering **three months** of her review period, not
  necessarily in a row (E20), and counts earnings divided by the minimum wage as hours, topped up with
  volunteering or school (E21).
- **The hours that move.** Of adults who worked 80 hours in June 2022, about one in six fell short in at
  least one of the next six months (D3, KFF; CORRECTED from "only 44% kept it"). Volatility only bites
  where a state checks several months (D4, D10): Montana does; Nebraska asks for one month in twelve now
  and one in six from 2027 (E10, E12).
- **The 14th birthday.** Caring for a child 13 or under exempts a parent (A25). The day her youngest turns
  14, that exemption ends (A25, D2).
- **The letter.** It arrives with 30 days to answer (A32). In Nebraska, 490 people didn't (E1).
- **The fallback that isn't there.** If she is cut off for not complying, she can't get subsidised
  marketplace coverage instead: the law bars the premium tax credits (A33).

⚠ The household is illustrative: no row says a Montana restaurant worker has any of these odds. Keep each
figure tied to its own source and state (Brookings's volatility is about low-income service workers in
general, not Medicaid enrollees, D10).

## The beats — Make it Matter, one door at a time

Proposed order (the writer places each beat before the debater who uses it):

1. **The letter (what the rule asks).** 80 hours or $580; one month per review at the floor; 30 days;
   every 6 months; who's exempt (caregivers of a child 13 or under, the medically frail, people in a SNAP
   household who are themselves subject to SNAP's work rule, veterans with a total disability rating,
   pregnant and postpartum people, among others) (A24–A26, A30–A32, D12). Settled, built solid. "Hold
   that: what happens when the letter goes out?"
2. **"I'm sick, or I'm waiting on disability. Does this reach me?"** (The most-liked question in the
   council's harvest; built from top-up T1. Every clause is a row.)
   - **Who's outside it altogether.** It reaches only adults 19 to 64 who got Medicaid through the
     expansion (T1-1). Anyone on Medicare is deemed to meet it; so is anyone under 19 (T1-4, T1-5). An SSI
     recipient isn't an expansion adult, and in most states SSI brings Medicaid automatically (T1-2,
     T1-3). Pregnant people are outside the group (T1-2), and postpartum people are a listed exclusion
     (D12). Plain line: **"If you're on SSI or
     Medicare, this rule doesn't touch you."**
   - **Inside the expansion and sick: the medically frail route.** The state must exclude anyone it finds
     in one of five groups: blind or disabled by Social Security's definition; a substance use disorder;
     a disabling mental disorder; a disability that significantly limits daily living; or a serious or
     complex medical condition (T1-7). Since October 29, belonging to a group is enough: the court struck
     the extra "significantly impairs your ability to comply" test (T1-8, A1). Congress's own daily-living
     limit stays (T1-7).
   - **The disability claim.** A pending claim isn't an exemption by itself, and an award isn't needed
     either: the rule borrows Social Security's definition, not its decision, and the state decides from
     its own records and the person's statement or paperwork (T1-6, APPROXIMATE: our reading of the rule).
     ⛔ Never "a pending claim exempts you", never "you have to wait for Social Security".
   - **How it's proven depends on your state.** The state checks a year of medical claims first. Through
     2027 it **may** take a sworn statement, each time; that's a state option, not a right. From 2028, a
     sworn statement counts only once per stretch of enrolment (T1-10, A3). Nebraska: a claims match
     means no action; with no match, a self-declaration with no doctor's note (T1-11). Montana: current
     members without a match send a form signed with their provider; new applicants may self-declare
     (T1-12).
   - **Social Security's clock.** About **six months** for a first decision (185 days, September 2026),
     and well over a year more on appeal (201 days at reconsideration, 287 at a hearing, rising) (T1-15,
     W7: our arithmetic). That's about as long as one renewal cycle for an expansion adult (A29, W7). Fair
     framing: "the state's clock and Social Security's run at about the same speed". ⛔ Not "people will
     be cut off while waiting": no data on that (T1 trap 7).
   - **Mental illness and addiction.** A mental disorder counts if it's "disabling"; the rule's examples
     include schizophrenia, moderate or severe bipolar disorder, major depression and panic disorder, and
     it can be temporary (T1-18). A substance use disorder counts unless the person has been in stable
     recovery for 5+ years; being in a treatment programme is a separate exclusion (T1-19).
   - **Caring for a disabled adult.** Yes, it exempts the caregiver: the person cared for can be any age,
     an older parent included, and needn't be on any benefit (T1-20). Living together, or a relative
     elsewhere, gives regular help; a non-relative living elsewhere needs 80 hours of help a month
     (T1-21). In Montana an adult's caregiver must self-declare: its data match covers child caregivers
     only (T1-22).
   - **If it goes wrong.** Coverage continues through the 30 days, the state must check every other basis
     of eligibility, and coverage can't end before the end of the following month (T1-23). Papers sent
     within 90 days of losing coverage must be reconsidered, but they're treated as a new application,
     judged on the month before (T1-24). And there's **no lock-out**: you can reapply at once (T1-25).
   - The ringmaster's line for the beat: "Sick people are excluded. The fight is over who has to prove
     it, and how." (Both teams use it: Green's point 7, Gold's point 5.)
3. **The first state's mailbox (Nebraska).** 7,280 renewals: 57% exempt, 34% qualified, 2% hardship, 7%
   lost coverage (E4, use counts); 490 of the 533 didn't answer (E1); 97% of qualifiers did it by work
   or earnings (E2). The ringmaster asks the viewer's question: "So were the 490 working?" Answer: nobody
   has measured it (E8, E15, E18). Hold that.
4. **Who's on the rolls.** In any one month of 2022 roughly half worked 80 hours (D8: 53% in KFF's
   expansion-state count, 44% in AEI's all-state count); 9% in school, 15% illness or disability, 2%
   caregiving, 21% other reasons including not finding work (D1). The federal agency built its own
   forecast on KFF's survey (D12). The fear against the record: 56% of the public think most adult
   enrollees don't work (C26).
5. **Did anyone work more? (Arkansas, Georgia, SNAP).** Arkansas 2018: 18,164 closures (B1); coverage
   down about 13 points and uninsured up about 7 for adults 30–49 (B9), no sign of more work (B9, B16).
   Georgia: no employment gain (B23). SNAP records rule out more than small gains (C6). The 1990s
   programmes with services: modest gains, income usually flat (C14, C16).
6. **What the check costs.** The agency's own estimate: 35.2 million hours a year of enrollees' time at
   renewal, valued at about $455 million (A20); systems $1.52 billion over ten years (A21); Georgia's
   start-up spending ran at two-thirds administration, then fell to 2.3% (B18, B20). Data see earnings,
   not hours (E34).
7. **After the letter (where people go).** Mixed, said as mixed: in the 2023–24 renewal wave 70% of those
   disenrolled became uninsured at least for a while, and about a quarter still were when surveyed (D13,
   D14); a Federal Reserve staff study found no significant national rise in uninsurance (D15); emergency
   rooms in California saw a modest rise in uninsured visits (D16). Hospital losses are projections (D17).

## Settled (not the debate)

**The rule (A, E)**
- Adults 19–64 in the expansion group must show 80 hours a month of work, school, volunteering or a work
  programme, or $580 of monthly household income (80 × $7.25; MAGI-based, so a spouse's pay or Social Security can count, T4-26), unless exempt; seasonal workers alone may average
  income over 6 months (A30, A31). Applicants show 1–3 months before applying; enrollees at least 1 month
  per review (A30).
- States must start by January 1, 2027; good-faith exemptions run at most 6 months at a time and never
  past December 31, 2028 (A23, E45). The agency assumes about 10 states ask, about 2 approved (A22).
- Data first: a state must try existing data before asking the person (A28); for frailty, a year of
  medical claims, pended and denied ones included (T1-10). Through 2027 a state **may** accept a sworn
  statement of frailty, each time (a state option, not a right); from 2028 only once per period of
  enrolment, with documentation generally required where no reliable data exist (A28, T1-10, A3).
- Who is outside the rule: everyone not in the expansion (adult) group, which itself leaves out people on
  Medicare and SSI recipients; anyone under 19 or on Medicare is deemed to meet it (T1-1, T1-2, T1-4,
  T1-5). In most states an SSI award brings Medicaid automatically (T1-3).
- The five frailty groups are found by the state (T1-7); since October 29 fitting a group is enough (T1-8).

## Contested (the real fight)

**Proposed side labels (data-driven, not pro/con; a pitch question):**
- **Team Green — "A test of the mailbox, not the job."** The first state removed 490 people for not
  answering and 43 for not working; the data can't see hours; no study using the states' records found
  more work. What the requirement reliably does is cut coverage.
- **Team Gold — "The condition is the point."** (Ruled 2026-11-02; the draft was "Keep the deal, fix the
  mailbox", which the council read as accepting Green's frame.) Asking able adults for 80 hours of work, school or
  service is fair and popular, and about half didn't meet it in a given month. Most people pass on data
  or an exemption; the paperwork losses are a design flaw to fix, not a reason to drop the condition.
- **Why these labels:** both name the same object (the letter), so the clash is about one thing the
  viewer can picture. Neither label asks its team to argue against a settled fact: Gold isn't asked to
  claim the rule raises employment (lopsided against it), and Green isn't asked to claim every
  non-responder was eligible (unmeasured).

### Team Green — "A test of the mailbox, not the job" (steelmanned)

1. **What the check found was the mailbox.** Nebraska: 490 of 533 lost for non-response, 43 for
   non-compliance (E1); 97% of qualifiers did it by work or earnings (E2). Arkansas 2018: among those who
   had to report, 88% did not report (B5); a third of enrollees aged 30–49 had heard nothing about it (B10);
   reporting was website-only for most of 2018, and only 11.3% of Arkansans preferred a computer website
   (B11). New Hampshire: about 17,000 of 25,000 non-exempt people had reported nothing before the court
   stopped it (B26).
2. **Most of the people it removes may have qualified.** Arkansas's survey: nearly 97% of ALL surveyed
   adults 30–49 already met the rule or should have been exempt (B10, survey-based). That's a share of
   everyone surveyed; that most of those REMOVED qualified is Green's inference from it, not a measurement. The federal agency's own model
   expects people who meet the rules to lose coverage over paperwork (A17).
3. **No sign of more work.** Arkansas −1.1 (B9) and −0.7 in a government-data re-analysis (B16); Georgia
   −1.6 (B23); Virginia's SNAP study, on state records, rules out gains above 3.5 points (C6), and a
   five-state study finds −0.4 (C8, not peer-reviewed); suspending SNAP's rule didn't lower work (C11); no break at the age cut-off in nine
   states' records (C12). KFF, relaying earlier CBO work, says a Medicaid work requirement would not
   meaningfully raise employment among enrollees (A11, KFF's relay, not the enacted-law score).
4. **The data can't see hours.** The rule's own words (E34); the federal match lists no hours source
   (E35); hours that bounce month to month (D3, D10, D11).
5. **The savings are the coverage loss.** The agency projects 2.3 million fewer enrollees in the first
   year and 3.1–3.3 million later (A18); CBO adds 5.3 million uninsured in 2034 before interactions
   (A12); KFF, reading CBO's numbers, says the savings "will largely stem from coverage losses" (A11,
   KFF's characterisation). Coverage really fell in Arkansas (B9, B16).
6. **The cost lands on people and on staff.** 35.2 million hours a year of enrollees' time (A20); no new
   staff in Nebraska for twice-yearly checks (E12); Colorado's state auditor found at least one problem in 72
   of 80 sampled system letters (E40, 2023; "problem" includes clarity). And no safety net
   underneath: no marketplace subsidy for someone cut off (A33).
7. **The sick still have to prove it, and the proof depends on the state.** Frailty is excluded only when
   the state finds it (T1-7); a sworn statement is a state option through 2027 and limited after (T1-10);
   Montana wants a provider-signed form from current members (T1-12), and its frailty page still printed
   the struck test on November 2 (T1-13, APPROXIMATE). A first Social Security decision takes about six
   months and an appeal well over a year more (T1-15, W7), so a disability claim is no shortcut. A
   caregiver of a disabled adult in Montana has to self-declare (T1-22).

**Green's limits, stated:** Nebraska's non-responders' eligibility is unmeasured (E8, E15). Arkansas's
website-only route (B11) is not how Nebraska and Montana run it: both check data first, then take forms
and documents (E10, E20). The uninsured estimate's lower bound is
near zero (B9). A Federal Reserve staff study finds no significant national rise in uninsurance from the
2023–24 renewal wave (D15). And the one-month work gap is real (D8). On the sick: no award is needed to be
excluded (T1-6, our reading), Nebraska takes a self-declaration with no doctor's note (T1-11), the court
already removed the extra frailty test (T1-8), and nobody has measured anyone cut off while waiting on
Social Security (T1 trap 7). On integrity: improper payments and duplicate enrolment are real at the
official audits' scale (T2-1, T2-10, T2-11); "mostly paperwork" is not "none".

### Team Gold — "The condition is the point" (steelmanned; label ruled 2026-11-02, was "Keep the deal, fix the mailbox")

_Rebuilt after top-up T2: Gold opens on what supporters say the rule is FOR, in their own documents, and
then carries the integrity numbers measured honestly both ways. Each purpose is attributed to the document
that states it._

1. **What it's for, in the supporters' own words.**
   - **The rule:** work and community engagement so people "can escape isolation and dependency, build
     confidence" and reach self-sufficiency; Medicaid "in line with other public benefit programs, like
     SNAP" and TANF; "a short-term hand up, not a lifetime handout"; prioritising the most vulnerable
     (T2-14, C31).
   - **The House committee report:** the provision sits under "Increasing Personal Accountability".
     Health coverage and work are already linked (most people get insurance through a job; Medicare is
     earned by work; veterans' care follows service), so asking able adults to work, study or serve will
     "promote the dignity of work" (T2-17; the House bill's report, legislative history, not the enacted
     text).
   - **The supporters' groups:** fairness to enrollees who work and to the most vulnerable (Paragon,
     advocacy, C30); moving "able-bodied adults" to work and preserving "resources for the truly needy"
     (FGA, advocacy, T2-23). FGA's sharpest form: over 700,000 people wait for Medicaid home care. The
     count is real (KFF: over 710,000 in 2024), but that expansion adults put them there is FGA's argument,
     not shown by the count (T2-24, T2-25, CORRECTED).
   - Sayable as Gold's opening line: "Supporters say it's about the dignity of work and a hand up, and
     about keeping Medicaid for the people who need it most" (T2-14, T2-17, T2-23).
2. **The deal is fair, and most people already meet it.** 80 hours of work, school or service, or $580
   of household income (T4-26), about 20 hours a week (A31, W2), with wide exemptions: the agency's model excludes about 24%
   outright (D12), and the sick, Medicare and SSI recipients are outside or excluded (beat 2: T1-1 to
   T1-8). In Nebraska only 43 of 7,280 were found not meeting it (E1); 57% were exempt and 34% qualified
   (E4).
3. **Keeping it for the people it's for: the integrity case, at its real size.**
   - **Improper payments are real, and not small in dollars.** Fiscal 2025: 6.12% of Medicaid payments,
     $37.39 billion (T2-1). About a sixth of that, $6.24 billion, was paid for people found ineligible for
     the program or the service (T2-3, W8); HHS's figure the year before was $4.9 billion (T2-5). The
     expansion group's eligibility error rate is 5.54%, a little above the 4.93% for all income-based
     groups, with overlapping intervals (T2-4, W8).
   - **Past audits found expansion adults who didn't qualify**: the inspector general's 2014–15 audits in
     four states put about $1.4 billion federal on more than 700,000 ineligible or potentially ineligible
     expansion adults (T2-7, APPROXIMATE; California's share T2-8).
   - **Duplicates are real**: the agency's own estimate is 1.2 million people a month in two or more
     states' Medicaid in 2024 (T2-10, APPROXIMATE: no method given).
   - **The work rule's own contribution, as the rule states it:** a state must document and verify each
     adult's status with auditable data (T2-14), checked at every renewal (A29), and the agency expects improperly
     enrolled people not to send the papers (T2-15: an expectation in its model, with no count).
   - **Measured honestly the other way (Gold must carry these):** 77% of improper payments are missing
     paperwork, "generally not indicative of fraud", and the rate is a dollar rate, not a share of people
     (T2-1, T2-4). The 2014–15 audits blamed state errors, not enrollees, and found more for traditional
     enrollees; Louisiana's 2016 audit found 115 of 120 eligible (T2-7, T2-9). The law's fix for
     duplicates is a different section, address checks from 2027 (T2-13), and the House report argues
     ineligible enrolment through other provisions, the work rule through accountability (T2-18,
     CORRECTED). Paragon's "nearly half ineligible" is a model, with its own range from a quarter to over
     half, and no government audit finds near half (T2-19, T2-20, CORRECTED); Paragon's own remedies don't
     include the work rule (T2-22).
   - Sayable: "In the latest official count, about 6% of Medicaid payments were improper, mostly for
     missing paperwork;
     about a sixth went to people found ineligible. Supporters say a regular check helps keep it for the
     people it's for, though the law's main fixes for ineligible and duplicate enrolment are elsewhere."
   - **The size of it, a viewer can picture (T3-17):** about 6 cents of every federal Medicaid dollar
     ($37.4 billion of about $611 billion); missing eligibility paperwork is about 2.7 cents of it, and
     people or services found ineligible about 1 cent (our arithmetic). Federal dollars only.
   - **⛔ The work rule is not aimed at that money (T3-15, T3-16).** The agency computes the rule's savings
     as people leaving the rolls times the cost per enrollee, and assumes about half of procedural losses
     are people who do meet the rules. The House report gives the rule a "dignity of work" reason and
     puts ineligible enrolment in other sections. The law's own error-rate fix is section 71106, which
     tightens an existing 3% penalty on states' federal match from fiscal 2030, scored at $7.55 billion
     over ten years (T3-13, CORRECTED: not a new penalty; T3-14). With the duplicate-enrolment section
     (71103, $17.4 billion), the anti-error sections come to about $25 billion over ten years (T3-14).
   - **No national figure for improper payments recovered was found** (T3-18, UNRESOLVED): most of the
     $37 billion is an estimate projected from a sample, not a bill anyone can collect. Fraud prosecutors
     (the state fraud control units) recovered about $2 billion in fiscal 2025, a different measure:
     provider fraud and patient abuse, not recovered improper payments (T3-19).
   - His question at the pitch ("6% could still be a significant amount of money that could be used for
     better purposes") is Gold's strongest form here: the money is real. Whether it "could be used
     better" is opinion; keep it in a seat's mouth, never the narrator's (T3 trap).
4. **The gap is real.** In December 2022, 44% of non-disabled working-age Medicaid recipients without
   children worked 80 hours, against 72% of similar adults not on Medicaid; 51% against 84% at ages 25–54
   (C32, D7). The "most work" figures count any work in a year (D5, C32's author on the gap). Some 21%
   didn't work for reasons including not finding work, retirement or other (D1). A records-based
   advocacy estimate puts zero reported earnings at 60% (D9, with heavy caveats).
5. **The paperwork is a design problem, and the rule designs against it.** Data first (A28); 56%
   expected to clear on data (A20); Arkansas cleared about two-thirds by data match (B6); earnings count,
   so a payroll record can stand in for an hours log (A31, E21); attestation accepted for frailty in
   Nebraska, with no doctor's note (E37, T1-11); a year of medical claims checked before anyone is asked
   (T1-10); no Social Security award needed to be excluded as frail (T1-6, our reading); 30 days to cure,
   coverage continuing meanwhile, a 90-day route back and no lock-out (A32, T1-23 to T1-25); one month
   per review at the floor (A30). The court has already removed the extra frailty hurdle (A1, T1-8).
   Vendors pledged discounted systems (E38).
6. **The cost is up front.** Georgia's administration share fell to 2.3% (B20); state IT prices so far
   total at least $45.6 million across five states (E39, APPROXIMATE: brief only, off air); the agency estimates systems at
   $1.52 billion over ten years against $350.3 billion in federal savings (A18, A21).
7. **Work with help does raise work.** The 1990s mandates raised employment by about 4 points on average
   (C1–C3, ASPE's averaged figure); every job-search-first programme raised earnings by at least $400 a
   year (C14); one SNAP study finds a large effect for low-income, low-education adults (C10).
8. **Non-response isn't proof of a wrongful cut.** The agency's reasons for paperwork losses include
   improper enrolment and other coverage (A17; an expectation, no count, T2-15); Nebraska says it is too early to determine trends (E8);
   in Arkansas, of a small sample who left coverage for any reason, 8.2% had employer coverage and 10.1%
   other insurance (B13); 1,981 of the 18,164 matched the state's new-hire records (B17, with caveats).
9. **The public backs the deal.** 62% (C20); 68% with exceptions (C22); 53% of working enrollees (C27).

**Gold's limits, stated:** the employment record for a requirement alone is null (C9, C6, B9); the 1990s
gains came with services the law doesn't fund on any record found (C35, UNRESOLVED) and didn't raise
family income (C14, C16); the data see earnings, not hours (E34); no state has published how many clear

enrolled a fraction of its projection (B21); and public support falls to 32–35% after the paperwork

T2-3); the law's fixes for ineligible and duplicate enrolment sit mainly in other provisions (T2-13,
T2-18); the rule's link between the work rule and improper enrolment is an expectation with no count
(T2-15); the supporters' own improper-enrolment paper doesn't argue for the work rule (T2-22); and the
rule's savings are counted as people leaving the rolls, not as improper payments recovered (T3-15).

### Where the record leans, said plainly (no false balance)

On the **empirical** questions the record leans toward Green. No study of a Medicaid work requirement has
found people working more (B9, B16, B23), and the SNAP studies built on state records rule out more than
a small gain (C6, C9; two survey-based SNAP studies find a small gain or a gain for one subgroup, C9,
C10); the work gains supporters cite came from 1990s programmes that attached
services and didn't usually raise family income (C14, C16). In the first state, almost all of the
coverage losses were people who didn't answer, not people found idle (E1), and the rule's own words
concede its data can't see hours (E34). Coverage fell measurably in Arkansas (B9, B16). The record does
**not** settle the two points Gold leans on hardest: whether Nebraska's non-responders were eligible
(unmeasured, E8, E15), and whether a data-first design can shrink paperwork losses to something small
(promised in the rule, A20, A28; not yet shown, E18). Gold also holds a real fact (roughly half didn't
work 80 hours in a given month, D8) and the **value** ground: whether coverage should carry a duty is not
something evidence can decide, the supporters' own documents argue it on accountability and the dignity of
work (T2-14, T2-17), and majorities say it should (C20, C22). On **integrity** the top-up changes the
picture in one way: improper payments and duplicate enrolment are real at the official audits' scale
(T2-1, T2-3, T2-10, T2-11), but most of the improper money is missing paperwork, and the law tackles
ineligible and duplicate enrolment mainly elsewhere (T2-13, T2-18); so integrity is a real reason to check
eligibility, and on the record a thin one for this check in particular: the supporters' own documents rest
the work rule on accountability, not on a count of ineligible people (T2-15, T2-17, T2-22). On the **sick**, the rule excludes them and the court
widened that (T1-7, T1-8); what the record can't yet show is how the state-by-state proof works in
practice (T1-11 to T1-13). The episode says the lean once, plainly, and does not pretend the value
question has an answer.

## The reframe (tested against the record)

**As written:** *The fight sounds like "should people have to work for Medicaid?" The record says most of
the people it reaches already work, study, care for someone or are sick enough to be excused, and the
first state's check removed 43 people for not working and 490 for not answering. So the question isn't
only "should coverage depend on work?" It's "should coverage depend on answering the letter that asks?"*

**Test 1, "most already work, study, care or are excused": holds, with a limit.**
- For it: Nebraska 57% exempt + 34% qualified (E4); KFF's "nearly eight in ten" met the hours or had a
  likely-exempt reason (D2); 92% working or with a care, health or school reason on the annual measure
  (D5).
- Limit: on a one-month hours test, only about half worked 80 hours (D8), and every survey's exemptions
  are wider than the rule's (D2, D pass trap 4). Say "most work, study, care or are excused", never "most
  work 80 hours".

**Test 2, "43 for not working, 490 for not answering": holds as a count.**
- Limit: two months, one state, draft minutes (E17); the 490 may include people who no longer qualified
  or had other coverage (A17's reasons). Say "490 didn't answer; nobody yet knows how many were working."

**Test 3, "a test of the letter": holds for the rule's mechanics.**
- For it: data see earnings, not hours (E34); work the data can't confirm needs documents (E10, E36).
- Limit: household income of $580 clears a month with no hours log (A31, T4-26), and in Nebraska 2,368 people qualified
  that way or by hours (E2). The data DO test work for anyone with steady pay. Say "for anyone the data
  can't see, it tests the paperwork".

## Both sides quietly agree

- **The rule's own authors expect eligible people to lose coverage over paperwork.** The agency's model
  assumes it (A17), and its 12% noncompliance rate is built from KFF's survey data (D12). Both sides are
  arguing from the same picture of who works; they differ on what the letter costs and whether the
  condition is worth it.
- **Both want the data, not the letter, to do the checking.** The rule says check the data first (A28);
  every state that has started does (E10, E20, E27); the Commonwealth Fund's hospital brief warns that
  states may lack time to automate verification (D18). Supporters also want checks strict enough to stop
  improper enrolment (C29), and the law has them: address checks against duplicates from 2027 (T2-13) and
  six-month renewals (T2-22), separate from the work rule. The rule's own words say the data see
  earnings, not hours (E34).
- **Sick people are meant to be excluded.** The law and the rule exclude the medically frail, and the
  states' own lawsuit widened that exclusion (T1-7, T1-8). The fight is over who has to prove it, and how
  (T1-10 to T1-12).
- **Medicaid itself doesn't make people stop working.** A lottery experiment found no significant effect
  on employment either way (C34): it rules out a fall of more than 4.4 points or a rise of more than 1.2
  points, which undercuts "Medicaid discourages work" and "coverage is what lets people work" as LARGE
  effects alike (a smaller fall isn't ruled out).

## Verdict framing

**No winner.** The judge says what the record settles (it found no sign that the requirement alone makes
people work more; in the first state, losses were mostly people who didn't answer; the data see earnings,
not hours; about half worked 80 hours in a given month; whether the non-responders qualified is unknown)
and hands the viewer **one dial: "What should keeping Medicaid ask of an adult who can work?"** It runs
from Green to Gold. Every setting has a real institution behind it and a documented failure. The judge
never sets it. (A proposal; settings and order are pitch questions.)

1. **"Nothing extra."** (Green, full.) Coverage with no work condition, as expansion ran before 2026.
   Behind it: Medicaid expansion as states ran it until 2026; the appeals court that called coverage "a
   principal objective" of Medicaid (A35; later vacated as moot, A36); the Oregon experiment's finding
   that coverage doesn't reduce work (C34). Documented failure: no work rule didn't mean no
   paperwork losses: before the rule, 55% of Nebraska's renewal closures were already procedural (E9,
   all Medicaid categories; never beside the 490 of 533); and on a one-month test about half worked 80 hours (D8), the gap the law was
   written to close.
2. **"Let the data check it."** A condition, but the state confirms it from records and asks only what
   the records can't show. Behind it: the federal rule's data-first requirement (A28) and its 56%
   expectation (A20); Arkansas's 2018 data match that excused two-thirds (B6). Documented failure: the
   records see earnings, not hours (E34); Arkansas still closed 18,164 cases (B1); no live state has
   published how many clear automatically (E18).
3. **"Ask once, keep it light."** The federal floor: one month per review, $580 counts, 30 days to answer
   (A30–A32). Behind it: Nebraska's one month in twelve (E10). Documented failure: Nebraska's first two
   months, 490 of 533 losses for not answering (E1), with no added staff (E12).
4. **"Ask, and pay for the help."** A requirement bundled with job search, training and supports.
   Behind it: the 1990s welfare-to-work programmes (C14, C16) and the federal planning office's 2026
   brief, which finds requirements work best "with supports" (C35). Documented failure: only 3 of 11
   programmes raised employment by more than 3 points over five years, and family income usually didn't
   rise (C14, C16); the 2026 brief only says states "can consider" pairing with training (C35).
5. **"Ask often."** (Gold, full.) Monthly or multi-month proof. Behind it: Georgia's Pathways, with
   monthly reporting at the start (B21), and Montana's three months per review (E20). Documented failure:
   Georgia enrolled under 3,500 full-year-equivalents in 15 months against 25,000 projected (B21), and
   spent two-thirds of its early money on administration (B18, with B20's fall to 2.3%).

⚠ Settings 2–3 are where the two teams nearly meet; say so. The law fixes setting 3 as the floor from
2027 (A30); states may go further (A29). The viewer's dial is about what they think it should be.
