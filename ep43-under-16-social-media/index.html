Should Social Media Be Banned for Under-16s?

Should the law keep kids off social media until 16, cap how they use it, or leave it to parents?

Last changed 2026-10-25 · See every change

To keep a fifteen-year-old off social media, somebody has to be checked, maybe you. Why governments are doing it, the smoke labelled (sextortion, grooming, chatbots, trafficking), what a line keeps out and what the check keeps: two AI teams argue precaution or proof; you set the dial.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

On October 14 a federal appeals court said Florida's law, which keeps under-14s from creating accounts and makes 14- and 15-year-olds get a parent's consent, is likely constitutional for now, and the next day the same court lifted the block on Georgia's under-16 law without ruling on whether it's constitutional. Australia already bars under-16s from holding accounts.

Settled

What both sides accept

Both teams accepted the same record. The average harm is small but real, and the claim that social media is changing teen health across the whole population isn't established. Sextortion and grooming are reported at scale, chatbot harm is alleged, and nothing yet measures an age line reducing any of them. Teen sadness peaked in 2021 and has fallen since.

These harms aren't mood averages: there were more than 50,000 sextortion reports in 2025, and most victims had no known mental-health condition. A parent can't hold this line alone: opt-in tools reach almost nobody, and a law binds every platform. The harm in the research is real and small, and the worst smoke runs past sixteen: trafficked children are mostly 14 to 17, and Australia's hardest-hit sextortion group is 18 to 24. In Australia, most kids who had accounts kept them or made new ones, and face scans let more than half of 14- and 15-year-olds through, with a fix that checks older teens and adults. What the shouting gets wrong

It isn't ban against freedom. The smoke is real and comes in different kinds, and the proof is thin both ways: nothing yet shows an age line clears the worst of it, and nothing shows waiting is safe either. So the question is precaution or proof: how much risk justifies acting before the evidence is in, and what you accept in return. And the age check is the hinge: every line needs one, and it's weakest right at the line it's drawing.

The verdict

What the record supports
This is precaution or proof: how much risk justifies acting before the evidence is in, and what you accept in return. And a limit lasts only as long as whoever holds its key wants it to.
What didn't hold up
Social media isn't proven to cause the teen crisis, and it isn't harmless. Australia's law hasn't been shown to work or to backfire. Florida didn't ban under-sixteens, chatbots aren't proven to push kids over the edge, and a two-hour default isn't a hard cap.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: Until what age, and who holds the key?

The judge left one dial: until what age, and who holds the key. These are the three keyholders the judge named, and nobody was declared the winner. The judge put it as precaution or proof: precaution says act now and fix the leaks; proof says show it reaches the harm first. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1The law, off until sixteen
    What it buys
    A line that binds every platform, so a parent doesn't hold it alone: opt-in tools reach almost nobody. It acts on credible risk before the proof is in. These harms aren't mood averages: most sextortion victims had no known mental-health condition, and the trials and the teen data point one way, toward real harm. In Australia, kids aged 10 to 15 holding an account fell from about half to about two in five in three months, wellbeing didn't fall, and fewer parents felt pressure to match what other parents allow.
    What it costs
    Australia's law hasn't been shown to work or to backfire. Most kids who had accounts kept them or made new ones, and the commonest reason was that the app never asked their age. Using those platforms, with or without an account, barely moved: more than four in five still did. Parents who didn't know what their child was using rose from about one in four to one in three. And the victims' ages run past sixteen: sextortion victims are typically boys aged 14 to 17, and trafficked children are mostly 14 to 17.
    Not measured
    Whether an age line reduces sextortion, grooming, trafficking or chatbot harm: no study yet measures it. Nor has anyone measured what a line does to the kids who may lean on social media most.

    On the record: row 88 of the fact-check log, row 91 of the fact-check log, row 99 of the fact-check log, row 321 of the fact-check log, row 327 of the fact-check log

  2. 2A parent, through a line with a consent key, a guardian account, or a parent-held default
    What it buys
    A parent holds the key, three ways. A line with a consent key, as in Florida: no new account at 13, and at 14 and 15 an account with a parent's consent. A guardian account, as in Europe's proposal: a limited account at 13 and 14 that a guardian sets up, capped at no more than an hour a day. Or a parent-held default, as in the TikTok settlement: two hours a day that only a linked parent can loosen. Defaults seem to stick when someone other than the teen holds the key: Meta reports 97% of 13-to-15-year-olds stayed in teen defaults that under-sixteens can't loosen without a parent.
    What it costs
    A limit lasts only as long as whoever holds its key wants it to. Where a parent has to opt in, almost nobody does: Snapchat's supervision tool had about 200,000 parents for about 20 million American teens. The 97% is the company's own figure. A two-hour default isn't a hard cap: messages, Search and long videos don't count toward it, and TikTok certifies its own age model until enough other states sign. Europe's guardian account is only a proposal.
    Not measured
    Whether any default reduces a measured harm: no independent study yet shows it, and the first trial in teenagers won't report until 2027 at the earliest.

    On the record: row 9 of the fact-check log, row 116 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 64 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 209 of the fact-check log, row 221 of the fact-check log

  3. 3The teen, where a limit is a passcode away
    What it buys
    The teen keeps the choice, and the kids who may lean on it most keep their access: LGBTQ+ young people say social media helps them feel less alone far more than their peers do.
    What it costs
    A limit is a passcode away: TikTok's older 60-minute limit for teens only asks for a passcode to keep watching, and in TikTok's own test the prompt cut teen use by about a minute and a half a day. The trials and the teen data point one way, toward real harm, and these harms aren't mood averages.
    Not measured
    Whether a limit the teen holds changes any harm: the only effect number is TikTok's own test, which measured minutes of use, not harm.

    On the record: row 212 of the fact-check log, row 213 of the fact-check log, row 246 of the fact-check log, row 250 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pick: Every setting leaks without a reliable age check, and the check is weakest exactly at the line it's drawing: Australia's face scans let more than half of 14- and 15-year-olds through a sixteen-plus gate, and the fix reaches adults. And a limit lasts only as long as whoever holds its key wants it to.

What our fact-check caught

37 of the 390 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidFlorida banned social media for under-16s.

    The record saysFlorida's governor vetoed that bill. The law he signed keeps under-14s from creating accounts, and lets 14- and 15-year-olds have one with a parent's consent.

  2. Draft saidAI chatbots are pushing kids over the edge.

    The record saysThat's alleged in lawsuits, and none has reached a finding. Of seven suits filed against one chatbot maker, six were for adults. No study shows chatbots cause teen self-harm, and the FTC is still studying it.

  3. Draft saidTeen sadness keeps rising.

    The record saysIn the federal survey of high-school students, it rose from 28% in 2011 to a peak of 42% in 2021, then fell to 33% in 2025. That's still above 2011.

  4. Draft saidTikTok agreed to a two-hour daily time limit for teens.

    The record saysIt's a default, not a hard cap. Only a linked parent can loosen it, and messages, Search and long videos don't count toward it.

  5. Draft saidA ban fixes the "everyone else has it" problem.

    The record saysThree months into Australia's law, fewer kids and parents thought everyone that age was on social media, and parents feeling pressure to match other parents fell from about one in five to about one in six. But the worry that a child will be left out didn't move, and the regulator warns beliefs can shift faster than behaviour.
