## The pitch, in one line

**To keep a 15-year-old off social media, somebody has to be checked. Maybe you.** (Ruling 1.) **Why are governments
doing this at all?** Teens say they are on these apps for hours a day, and four in ten say they are online almost
constantly (log 267, 268); the share of US high-school students reporting persistent sadness rose from 28% in 2011 to
a peak of 42% in 2021 before falling to 33% in 2025 (log 271, 275); and the harms parents fear most are not mood
averages: more than 50,000 sextortion reports to the US tipline in 2025, chatbot lawsuits, grooming and trafficking
(log 314, 336, 313, 356). **The real question is "precaution or proof?"** (Ruling 5): how much smoke justifies acting
before the evidence is in, and what you accept in return.

**Every rule that draws a line at an age needs the same thing first: a way to tell a 15-year-old from a
16-year-old.** (Leaving it to parents, or targeting the features, needs no age check: log 10, 15, 163.) In Australia, where under-16s are barred from holding accounts, three months in most
kids who had accounts still had them, and the commonest reason was that the app had never asked
(log 90, 91). When the government tested face-scan age checks at the 16 line, more than half of 14-
and 15-year-olds got through (log 190). Florida's line, the American settlements' two-hour default and
the EU's guardian account all rest on that same check. **So the real setting is not "ban or freedom".
It is: at what age does a teen get an account of their own, and until then, who holds the key: the
law, the parent, or the teen?**

## The viewer's moment

Three people, and the viewer is one of them. (Invented and unnamed; ⛔ no persona voices a child or a
teen on air. A teen's view comes only from a sourced survey.) **On air the adult comes first** (Ruling 1: the cold
open), and the parent of a 13-year-old gets their own beat, "Find your row" (Ruling 3), below.

- **The parent of a 13-year-old** who says "everyone else has it". Under the Alabama decree, the
  two-hour default on a teen's TikTok can be loosened only by a linked parent (log 64, 65). Under
  Florida's law, a 14- or 15-year-old needs the parent's consent to open an account (log 9). And the
  tools a parent can switch on today are barely used where anyone has counted: about 200,000 parents
  on Snapchat's supervision tool, for about 20 million US teens on the app (log 221).
- **The 15-year-old** (or the adult remembering being one). In Australia the app may simply not have
  asked (log 91). A face scan at a 16+ gate may let them through, or may block a real 16-year-old (log
  190, 191). In the EU's proposal a 15-year-old gets their own account (log 116). Under the TikTok
  decree, the app stops at two hours unless a parent says otherwise, but messages, Search and long
  videos don't count (log 64).
- **★ The adult asked to prove their age.** The hook nobody expects, and every viewer is in it.
  Georgia's law makes platforms verify the age of **every** account holder, not only minors (log 27).
  The age-check trial's own fix for its error at 16 is a buffer of 2–3 years, which pushes the checks
  onto 16- to 19-year-olds (log 192); one ID vendor wrongly blocked up to half of 18- and 19-year-olds
  at an 18+ gate (log 197). And ID photos have leaked: about 70,000 from a vendor reviewing Discord's
  age appeals, about 13,000 verification images from a dating app (log 200, 202). (Neither was a
  legally mandated checker; log 207.)

The episode follows **one account**: the sign-up screen, the age box, the face scan, the parent's
phone, the two-hour wall, and the dial.

## The question

**Should the law keep kids off social media until 16, cap how they use it, or leave it to parents?**
Under it sits the question the record forces: **whichever you pick, how does anyone know who is 15,
and who pays for finding out?**

## Why is this happening? (the backdrop, early, at full strength for the case FOR a law)

The host's question at the pitch: why is this a topic, why Australia, why Florida? This beat comes **early**, right
after the cold open and **before** the age-check problem, so the case for a law is heard whole first.

**How much teens use it.** Four in ten US teens say they are online almost constantly (internet use, not social media
alone), and 36% say they are on at least one of five big platforms almost constantly (Pew, 2025; log 266, 267). Daily:
YouTube about three in four, TikTok 61%, Instagram 55% (log 264). In a 2023 survey teens estimated 4.8 hours a day
across seven apps, girls 5.3 and boys 4.4 (log 268, 269). Say "teens say" or "teens report": these are self-reports,
not device time. ⚠ "Nearly half online almost constantly" is 2024's figure; 2025's is four in ten (log 267).

**The teen mental-health trend, shown whole** (CDC Youth Risk Behavior Survey, US high-school students; log 271–276):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| persistent sadness or hopelessness, all | 28% | 30% | 30% | 31% | 37% | **42%** | 40% | 33% |
| girls | 36% | | | | | 57% | 53% | 43% |
| boys | 21% | | | | | 29% | 28% | 24% |
| seriously considered suicide, all | 16% | | | | | 22% | 20% | 14% |

(2025 exact: 33.3%, girls 43.4%, boys 23.9%, log 275.) **Ruling 6:** say the peak came in 2021, **during the pandemic
years**, as timing only. ⛔ Never "COVID caused it": no source in the record attributes it. Both readings stay: the
rise began well before 2020 (log 271), and the fall in 2023 and 2025 came while teens' reported use stayed high (log
265–267, 275). CDC itself calls sadness better over two years and worse over ten (log 276). Neither side may treat
the fall as decisive.

**The argument that links them, at full strength, and its critics.**
- *The thesis, in its authors' words:* the major platforms "are causing substantial harm to the mental health of
  adolescents, especially girls" (log 277); the book says phones and social media, with less independence and free
  play, "rapidly rewired childhood and caused a global mental health crisis" from the early 2010s (log 278). Its
  authors concede the population trend is harder to prove and call their chapter "the case for the prosecution" (log
  277, 146). Attribute by role: "a social psychologist who wrote the bestseller".
- *The critics:* a developmental psychologist reviewing the book in *Nature* says its rewiring-and-epidemic claim "is
  not supported by science", and names other drivers researchers cite, from guns and the opioid epidemic to economic
  hardship and isolation (log 279); hundreds of studies found "no, small and mixed" associations (log 162); technology
  use explains at most 0.4% of the variation in well-being, a reading the harm side has published a rebuttal to (log
  164, 165); only 17% of the cited work could support causal claims (log 166); the National Academies "did not support"
  population-level causation (log 169). Which way the arrow runs is not settled either way (log 161).

**Why Australia acted, in its own words.** "Social media is doing social harm to our kids. I’m calling time on it,"
the Prime Minister said when the state and territory leaders endorsed 16 on **8 November 2024** (log 283, 282). The
case was values and burden: kids taken "away from real friends and real experiences" (log 280); "a critical period of
development", with the onus on platforms, "not parents or young people" (log 284); "We want our kids to have a
childhood and parents to know we have their backs" (log 286); and "to do nothing is simply not an option" (log 286).
The stated mechanism was the logged-in features, such as notifications harming sleep, stress and attention, and the
law was compared to age limits on alcohol and cigarettes (log 287). ⚠ **Its evidence base was thin:** one correlational
UK study (log 288, 156) plus surveys of parents, 87% backing a minimum age in a New South Wales survey (log 289).
Campaigns pressed for it (log 290, APPROXIMATE: trade press), 77% of adults backed it in a poll at passage (log 291),
and it passed eight days after it was introduced (log 285, 286); the Senate committee had five, and almost all submitters objected to the time (log 292).
The objections it heard: weak evidence, kids "driven into hidden spaces where they may be less safe", harm to
vulnerable young people, privacy (log 293). The Prime Minister conceded "some kids will find workarounds" (log 285).
⛔ Never "Australia acted because the science proved harm".

**Why Florida acted.** ⚠ **The law itself states no purpose:** HB 3 has no findings section (log 296). Florida's "why"
lives in three places. The House staff analysis: experts "have tied" rising social-media use to rising teen
depression, anxiety and stress (log 297), and it calls the link "causally linked or proven", ⚠ **citing a 2018
university news story**, not a review: attribute it, never air it as fact (log 298). The signing (2024-03-25): the
Governor said "Social media harms children in a variety of ways," and the House Speaker called the internet "a dark
alley for our children where predators target them" (log 308, 309; a dated official act, a claim, not a finding).
In court, the state called the features psychological hooks that "overstimulate the reward center" (log 310). **And it
is a compromise:** the Legislature's first bill barred every under-16 and checked the age of every account holder
(log 302); the Governor vetoed it, saying a "superior bill" was coming and naming children, "parents' rights" and
adults' "anonymous speech" (log 303, 304). HB 3 moved the line to 14, with a parent's key for 14 and 15 (log 306), and
the same staff analysis records that parental-control apps "may actually" backfire (log 300). ⛔ Never "Florida banned
under-16s".

## Why now

_Peg hunt run 2026-10-20 (peg-hunt skill, all five phases), then re-checked by hand pass B at the
primary. Re-run it before scripting and on the day it posts._

**The hot peg (description): one court, two days.** On **2026-10-14** the US Court of Appeals for the
Eleventh Circuit ruled in *CCIA and NetChoice v. Uthmeier*, No. 25-11881, that **Florida's HB 3** is
**likely constitutional, for now**, as applied to the industry groups' members (log 1, 2). HB 3 stops
anyone under 14 from creating an account on platforms with algorithmic feeds and "addictive features"
(infinite scrolling, push notifications, auto-play, live-streaming, "personal interactive metrics"),
and stops 14- and 15-year-olds from doing so without a parent's consent (log 9, 10). The panel held the
law is likely a content-neutral rule that survives intermediate scrutiny. It **reversed** the
preliminary injunction as applied, **vacated** it on the facial challenge, and **sent the case back**
(log 3). No judge dissented (log 4). The parent's key for 14- and 15-year-olds is **inside** the
holding, not left open (log 6). The same court had already stayed the injunction on 2025-11-25, over a
dissent, so the law could already be enforced (log 19). The industry group says it is "evaluating all
of our options" (log 22). Primary: [opinion, filed 10/14/2026](https://media.ca11.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/files/202511881.pdf).

On **2026-10-15** the **same panel** lifted the injunction against **Georgia's SB 351**, which bars
under-16s from holding accounts without parental consent and makes platforms verify the age of every
account holder (log 24, 27). But it decided **no** First Amendment question: the industry group had
not carried its burden on a facial challenge, and the court "needn't" reach the merits (log 25).
⛔ **Never "a court said Georgia's law is valid"** or "cleared" it (log 26). Georgia is the second clause
of the peg, never its claim: it serves the adult-pays hook (every account holder checked), not the law
question.

**Why this one: thesis fit, not just recency.** Florida's law does two settings of the dial at once: a
hard line under 14, then a **parent-held key** to 16. It moves the American answer: the courts are now
**split**, with appellate results this year mostly letting states enforce (Ohio, the Florida stay and
ruling, Georgia, Texas's app-store law), while Virginia's time limit is still blocked and Tennessee was
sent back (log 40, 43, 49, 50, 55). ⛔ Not "US courts strike these laws down" (out of date) and not
"a court upheld a ban" (log 12).

## Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

**The law (as of 2026-10-20)**
- **Florida's HB 3 is likely constitutional at an early stage, as applied, and sent back** (log 2, 3).
  "Parents already have tools" lost on a **legal standard** (intermediate scrutiny needs no
  least-restrictive alternative), not on a finding that parental controls fail; "ineffective" is
  Florida's own brief (log 13). The court called the policy question "Maybe, maybe not." (log 16)
- **Georgia's ruling is procedural** (log 25). **Ohio**: a fractured panel reversed a final judgment
  for the state (log 40, 41). **Tennessee**: sent back (log 43). **Virginia's** one-hour default is
  still enjoined, argued 10-28 (log 32, 33).
- **The Supreme Court has not decided a social-media age law.** Mississippi was an emergency-docket
  denial, and only one justice wrote that the law is "likely unconstitutional" (log 44, 45). *Paxton*
  (2025, 6–3) allows age checks for adult-content sites under intermediate scrutiny (log 47).
- **Both settlements are defaults, not caps, and neither admits liability.** Alabama's is court-entered;
  Meta's is proposed (log 60, 64, 69, 74).
- **Australia's duty is "reasonable steps" to stop under-16s having accounts** (log 80). Platforms may
  not insist on government ID without an alternative (log 84). The maximum fine was raised; the
  government said it would double it (log 81, 83).
- **The EU's is a proposal:** 15, a guardian account for 13–14, every age check a zero-knowledge proof
  (log 114–117).

**The harm evidence**
- **Every completed randomised trial of cutting social media was in adults or college students.** The
  first adolescent trial (about 4,500 pupils aged 12–15 in Bradford, testing a one-hour daily budget
  and a 9 p.m. to 7 a.m. curfew) runs to May 2027 (log 128, 188).
- **In adults, cutting it helps a little.** The two large trials found well-being gains of 0.04–0.09
  standard deviations (log 133, 141): the median person moves from the 50th to about the 52nd
  percentile (log 137). Among US college students, Facebook's arrival raised poor mental health by about
  22% of the effect of losing a job (log 151).
- **In young teens, more use came before more symptoms a year later**, in a study of about 11,900 US
  children (log 159); sensitive windows fall at 11–13 for girls and 14–15 for boys (log 156). Both
  studies are observational, and the 2022 one found the link runs both ways (log 157).
- **The population-level claim is not established.** The National Academies: the literature "did not
  support the conclusion that social media causes changes in adolescent health at the population level"
  (log 169). The Surgeon General's "doubles the risk" overreaches its one cited study (log 167), and the
  same advisory says the evidence can't yet show social media is "sufficiently safe" (log 168).

**Australia at three months (eSafety's own evaluation, peer reviewed by its academic partner; log 103)**
- **Fewer accounts, nearly the same use.** Account holding among 10- to 15-year-olds fell from 52.4%
  to 42.1%; any use, with or without an account, from 85.9% to 81.5% (log 88, 89).
- **Most kept or remade accounts**, eSafety says, and the commonest reason was that the platform hadn't
  asked (half of those who kept one) (log 90, 91).
- **No measured change in wellbeing either way** (log 95); 65.4% said it had no impact on them (log
  97). eSafety says it is too early to judge outcomes (log 93).
- **More use that parents don't know about:** unaware parents rose from 23.3% to 33.3% (log 99). Use
  moved sideways, to messaging, Reddit and Pinterest, with little fringe-app uptake (log 100, 101).
- **Nobody has measured the kids who might lose most:** no LGBTIQ+, rural or disability result yet
  (log 102).

**Age checks**
- **They are weakest exactly at the line.** At a 16+ gate, the face-scan systems tested passed more
  than half of 14- and 15-year-olds, even after the two worst were dropped (log 190). Average error at
  13–16 is about 2.5 years (log 193). The fix is a 2–3-year buffer (log 192).

**Does a law change what OTHER families do?** (the collective-action case, top-up X2) This is the "nobody wants
their kid to be the only one without it" argument, measured where it can be:
- **Measured, Australia at three months (perceived, not actual):** children agreeing "most kids my age use social media"
  fell from 85.1% to 74.0%; parents agreeing it's common for their child's peers to have accounts fell from 73.4% to
  60.0% (log 375, 376). Parents feeling "pressure to conform to what other parents are allowing" fell from 22.6% to
  17.3%, mostly among parents of 10–12s (log 377). ⚠ But the worry that a child will be left out **did not move**
  (27.7% to 28.4%, log 378), only about one parent in five felt that pressure before the law (log 377), and eSafety
  warns perceived norms can move faster than behaviour, with no comparison group (log 380). Air the fall and the
  non-move together.
- **Measured, US college students (average age 21, not 13):** they had to be paid about $55 and $47 to quit TikTok and

  once the cost of being left out was counted (log 382, 383). The harm thesis's authors call this a "collective action

  recorded mental-health need fell by about 8% against matched schools (log 386: one working paper, not peer reviewed;
  need, not phone ownership). Pledge counts are the movements' own (log 388, 389). **Whether a law does this better
  than a pact is untested** (log 386).
- ⛔ Never "a ban solves the collective-action problem" (log 390), and never read Pew's 65% of parents backing a ban as
  a measure of that pressure (log 232, 377).

## Contested (the real fight)

**The frame since Ruling 5: "precaution or proof?"** Not "ban or freedom", but how much risk justifies acting before
the evidence is in, and what you accept in return. Labels stay on every piece of smoke (established, alleged,
reported, measured), so the viewer can see how much there is.

**Side A (Green) — "Draw the line in law."** (Off until 16, or a line with a parent's key; Australia and
Florida.)
**Precaution, at full strength:** when the possible harm is serious and the people at risk are children who can't
protect themselves, you act on credible risk without waiting for proof. **How much smoke do you need before you call
it a fire?** The smoke, by kind: more than 50,000 sextortion reports in 2025 (log 314, reported), at least 20 suicides
in the FBI's count and at least three dozen teenage boys in NCMEC's (log 318, 320, agency counts), victims who mostly
had no prior mental-health condition (log 321); chatbot lawsuits brought by families (log 336, alleged); trafficking
reports and the Justice Department's warning that social media gives offenders "easy access to victims" (log 356, 357,
reported); and teen sadness still well above 2011, 33% against 28% (log 271, 275, measured). Governments said as much:
"to do nothing is simply not an option" (log 286). And waiting has a price: the first teen trial reports in 2027 at
the earliest (log 188). **A line can also do what no parent can alone:** fewer kids and parents in Australia now think
"everyone's on it", and parents' pressure to conform fell (log 375–377, perceived, three months); students would pay to
have everyone off at once (log 383).
The harm is small, but where it has been tested it is causal. Cutting social media improves adults'
well-being in randomised trials (log 133), and Facebook's arrival at US colleges hurt students' mental
health by about a fifth of the effect of losing a job (log 151). In young teens, more use came before
more depressive symptoms a year later, and not the other way round (log 159). The sensitive windows,
11–13 for girls and 14–15 for boys, sit under 16 (log 156). The mechanisms are concrete: for girls,
about a third of the measured link ran through cyberbullying, the largest single route (57% of the 58% that was explained; for boys little was explained) (log 174), and a former Meta engineer told the
Senate that as many as one in five 13–15-year-olds had been bullied on Instagram in the past week (log
187, his account of an initial readout). Waiting for a teen trial means waiting until 2027 at the
earliest (log 188). **A parent can't hold this line alone:** opt-in tools reach almost nobody (log 221),
and the defaults that seem to stick are the ones someone other than the teen holds, by company and survey figures, with no independent evidence either way (log 209, 212, 215, 229). A law
binds every platform. A settlement binds one company, where a state sued, with money that depends on
other states signing and an age model the company grades itself (log 63, 67). **Australia's leak is an
enforcement gap, not proof a line can't work:** account holding fell by about a fifth in three months,
wellbeing did not fall, few kids went to fringe apps, and the main leak was platforms not asking, which
the new Act's tougher penalties and powers target (log 88, 91, 95, 101, 81). **The courts now allow it**
(log 2, 13), and **the public wants it:** 56% of US adults and 65% of parents back an under-16 ban (log
230, 232); 74% in Britain (log 241); 82% of Australians back restricting kids' access (log 112).
Teens themselves backed companies requiring age verification, 56 to 16 (log 235).

**Side B (Gold) — "Fix the defaults, keep the door open."** (Caps and parent-held defaults, design rules,
parents' tools; the US settlements and the EU's guardian account.)
**A rule that leaks is not protection.** Its own smoke: in Australia use moved where parents can't see it (unaware
parents rose from about 1 in 4 to 1 in 3, log 99); the check misses at the line (more than half of 14- and 15-year-olds
passed a 16+ face scan, log 190); and every adult gets checked (log 27, 192), with data the rules only partly cover
(log 368, 370). **The worst smoke isn't where the line is drawn:** nothing yet shows an age line reduces sextortion
(log 327), and Australia's most-affected sextortion complainants are 18 to 24 (log 325); trafficked children are
mostly 14 to 17, so a line at 16 misses many (log 356); a social-media line doesn't reach ChatGPT or Character.AI
(log 340); and no source shows an age line reduces trafficking or chatbot harm (log 358, 343). Teen sadness fell to
33% in 2025 while teens' reported use stayed high (log 275, 267). Australia's own law rested on one correlational
study and parent surveys (log 288, 289). Offenders already move children to encrypted apps (log 324), and when one
platform was shut, the market moved and got harder to police (log 355).
The harm is real and **small**: in the best adult trial, about 2 percentile points, the same as 3.8
people in 100 moving from feeling happy "sometimes" to "often", about a fifth of what an average
therapy does (log 137–139). Technology use explains at most 0.4% of the variation in teen well-being
(log 164), and life satisfaction across 36 countries showed no overall change (log 178). The headline
"doubles the risk" overreaches its own study (log 167), and the National Academies did not find
population-level causation (log 169). **Bans so far leak:** in Australia most kids kept or remade
accounts, use of those platforms with or without an account barely moved (85.9% to 81.5%; the law bars accounts, not logged-out viewing), one in ten got a parent's help to get round it, and more parents lost track
of what their child uses (log 89, 90, 92, 99). **The check is weakest at the line:** more than half of
14- and 15-year-olds passed a 16+ face scan (log 190), and the fix, a 2–3-year buffer, puts ID checks
on 16- to 19-year-olds and on adults (log 192, 197). Georgia already makes every account holder verify
(log 27), ID photos have leaked from vendors (log 200, 202), and the Supreme Court answered privacy
fears with platforms' "every incentive" to protect data (log 48). **The kids a ban removes are the ones
who lean on it most:** LGBTQ+ young people say social media helps them feel less alone far more than
their peers (74% vs 52%), call online spaces their most affirming setting, and lie about their age to
get in far more often (59% vs 39%); Australia hasn't measured them (log 246, 244, 249, 102). **Target the
features instead:** Florida's own law survived because it regulates features (log 10, 15); both
settlements change defaults (log 64, 75); a leading skeptic calls age bans "unlikely to be effective"
(log 163). Large majorities of parents and children back teen accounts with stronger protections (log
239, an industry-funded poll), and teens backed parental consent 46 to 25 while splitting on time
limits (log 234, 236).

**The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:**
- *B at A:* **Australia hasn't shown a benefit yet either.** Wellbeing was flat, not better, and eSafety
  itself says most kept or remade accounts and that it is too early to judge (log 90, 93, 95). **The
  developmental windows don't pick 16:** they fall at 11–13 for girls (log 156), and the college study
  says nothing about under-16s (log 155). **The public number depends on the question:** offered a ban
  or tighter regulation of features, UK adults split 44 to 39 (log 242).
- *A at B:* **Caps have no outcome evidence either.** The only effect number is TikTok's own test of a
  prompt, about a minute and a half a day, and TikTok measured success by media coverage (log 213); the
  97% is a lock a parent holds, self-reported (log 209); the UK regulator says it can't tell how much its
  design code changed (log 217). **The cap model needs the same age check:** the TikTok decree runs on an
  age-prediction model the company certifies itself (log 67). **Displacement cuts both ways:** reliance
  is measured, but what removing access does is measured nowhere, and in Australia few kids reported
  harder contact and fringe-app use stayed small (log 250, 97, 101).
- *B at A (the precaution case):* **precaution needs the rule to reach the harm.** The sextortion age profile runs
  past 16 (log 319, 325), trafficked children are mostly 14 to 17 (log 356), the chatbots in the lawsuits sit outside
  any social-media line (log 340), and nothing yet measures an age line cutting any of them (log 327, 343, 358). The
  reports rose partly because a 2024 law made platforms report more (log 313, 357). Florida's "causally linked" cites
  a news story (log 298).
- *A at B (the proof case):* **the sextortion victims weren't mood averages**: most had no known mental-health
  condition (log 321), and more than half of the sextorted 16–18s in Australia's survey were first targeted before 16
  (log 326, APPROXIMATE: the regulator's report). "No evidence it works" is not evidence it doesn't (log 327). And
  where nobody holds a shared line, the measured pressure is real for some parents (log 377), while the defaults that
  seem to stick are the ones someone else holds (log 209, 215).
- *Both:* teens are not one voice. Children 10–17 backed an under-16 ban at only 36–38% in an
  industry-funded poll, and just over half of Australian 12–15s oppose it, yet the same age group backed
  age verification and stronger teen accounts (log 238, 113, 235, 239). None of these polls is a
  probability sample of under-16s (log 237).

## The reframe

**Precaution or proof?** (Ruling 5.) The shouting is **"ban" against "freedom"**, as if the choice were whether kids
are on the apps. The real choice is how much risk justifies acting before the evidence is in, and what you accept in
return. The smoke is real and of different kinds: measured mood effects, small on average (log 137, 159); reported
sextortion, grooming and trafficking, large in count (log 313, 314, 357); alleged chatbot harm (log 336). The proof is
thin both ways: no study yet shows an age line reduces the contact harms (log 327, 343, 358), and no study shows
waiting is safe either (log 168, 188). Precaution says act now and fix the leaks; proof says a rule that leaks is not
protection, and it costs every adult a check (log 190, 27). **What you accept in return** is the second layer, and the
record says two narrower facts decide it.

1. **Every setting needs to know who is a minor, and that check is weakest at 14–16.** Florida's
   parent key needs it; Alabama's default runs on a TikTok age model graded by TikTok (log 67);
   Australia's leak was platforms not asking, or guessing wrong (log 91); and the face scans tested
   passed more than half of 14- and 15-year-olds at a 16+ gate (log 190).
2. **Defaults seem to stick when someone other than the teen holds the key, but only company and survey figures say so** (no independent outcome evidence either way, log 215). Where a parent holds it,
   Meta reports 97% staying in its teen defaults (log 209, company figure). Where the teen holds it,
   TikTok's limit is a passcode away (log 212) and 85% of UK 16–18s say they'd switch off a curfew (log
   229). Where the parent must opt in, almost nobody does (log 221).

The law has quietly moved the same way. Florida's law survived **because** it regulates features, not
speech (log 10, 15). Both settlements set defaults, not doors (log 64, 75). The EU's middle step is a
guardian account (log 116). **So the live question is not "ban or freedom" but "precaution or proof?", and the dial
under it is: at what age does a teen get an account of their own, who holds the key until then (the law, the parent,
or the teen), and who pays for the age check that makes any of it work?**

## Both sides quietly agree

**On "precaution or proof?", both sides accept the same facts:** the contact harms are real and reported in large
numbers (log 313, 314, 320); nothing yet measures an age line reducing them (log 327, 358); a social-media line does
not reach stand-alone chatbots, which need their own law (log 340, 334, 341); teen sadness rose for a decade and has
fallen since 2021 (log 271, 275). They disagree only on what to do before the proof arrives.

**Almost everyone in the record wants young teens' defaults changed, and wants some way to know who
is young.**
- **The skeptic researchers:** one leading skeptic calls age bans "unlikely to be effective" (log 163);
  another is a principal investigator on the first teen trial, which tests a one-hour daily budget and
  a night curfew (log 188).
- **The states:** Florida's law targets feeds and features, and the settlements they signed set
  parent-held defaults (log 10, 64, 75).
- **The platforms:** Meta keeps under-16s in teen defaults they can't loosen alone, and backs age
  checks at the app store with a parent's approval (log 209, 225). (Both are self-interested: one is
  Meta's own figure, the other moves the check to Apple and Google.)
- **The teens:** in Pew's probability sample they backed companies requiring age verification, 56 to
  16, and parental consent, 46 to 25 (log 235, 234). In an industry-funded poll, 77–80% of children and
  74–77% of parents backed teen accounts with stronger protections (log 239).

**The fight is narrower than it sounds** (our reading of the record, not a finding): whether a hard
account line adds anything that parent-held defaults and an age signal would not, and who should bear
the cost of the check.

- **Survives (precaution or proof):** **there is real smoke of several kinds, and the proof that a line clears it is
