## The research · Four tries in fifteen years, and the new test

**Nia:** Go.
**Kate:** Twenty eleven: a rule that tested whether a program's students were repaying their loans. A court struck it down the next year. Twenty fourteen: a new rule, with a debt test. **Nia:** Not one?
**Kate:** Not one. Twenty twenty-three, a third try: only programs at for-profit colleges, and certificate programs, could lose aid. Everyone else just had to disclose.
**Nia:** And the old rule on defaults?
**Kate:** A school loses aid if too many of its borrowers default three years running. It catches a handful of schools a year, and most win on appeal. Government auditors found schools hiring consultants who pushed borrowers behind on their payments into a pause, which blunts that three-year count.
**Nia:** So what's new?
**Kate:** The twenty twenty-five law. From July twenty twenty-seven, nearly every program gets tested. If its graduates, four years out, earn less than working adults aged twenty-five to thirty-four with only a high-school diploma, in two years out of three, the program loses federal student loans.
**Nia:** That sounds tough.
**Kate:** What it misses: it never subtracts the price. It counts only graduates, never the students who don't finish. It doesn't touch the parent's loan. And the Education Department projects it fails about one bachelor's program in ninety. For undergraduate certificates, about one in four.
**Nia:** One in ninety?
**Kate:** At the bachelor's level, yes. It bites hardest on certificates and for-profit colleges: about four in ten for-profit schools fail a second, school-wide test that can cost their failing programs all federal aid.
**Nia:** Did anyone try making the college pay?

**Nia:** So the college was on the hook, and then it wasn't.
