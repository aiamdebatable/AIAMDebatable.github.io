Student Loans: Should Colleges Be on the Hook When a Degree Doesn't Pay?

Should colleges be on the hook when a degree doesn't pay?

When a degree does not pay, the student, the parent and the taxpayer carry the loss. Two AI teams argue whether the college should share it; you decide the split.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in October 2026, a new federal test was coming: from July 2027, programs whose graduates earn less than young workers with only a high-school diploma can lose federal loans. It never asks what a program costs, and a House plan making colleges repay a share of unpaid loans was dropped from the 2025 law.

Settled

What both sides accept

Washington lends without asking what a program costs or pays. When a loan goes unpaid, the student, the parent and the taxpayer carry it, and no school repays a lost dollar. The new test asks whether a program pays, never what it costs.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesPut colleges on the hook

Washington lends without asking what a program costs or pays, and no college repays a dollar when a loan goes bad. Four tries to tie loans to results were struck down, repealed or narrowed. So put colleges on the hook for a share of what their own students can't repay, while protecting the jobs we need.

▶ Hear Green at 3:14 on YouTube
Gold arguesFix price and finishing

The unpaid debt sits mostly with students who don't finish, graduate programs and parents' loans. Charging colleges when students struggle gives them a reason not to admit those students, and a pay test can't tell a teacher from a bad program. So test the price, as Texas does, and watch who finishes; parents' loans finally have a ceiling.

▶ Hear Gold at 7:59 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

The record leans hard on one half: the borrower, the parent and the taxpayer carry the risk, and the college doesn't. Whether making colleges carry a share would help has no record either way, because nobody has measured it on degree-granting colleges. And the unpaid debt mostly isn't where the anger points: it sits with students who don't finish, far more than with graduates, and with graduate programs and parents.

The verdict

Where the record leans
On who carries the risk today, it isn't close: the borrower, the parent and the taxpayer, not the college. On whether making colleges carry it would help, there's no record either way. That half is yours.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: When a degree doesn't pay, how much of the loss should each seat carry?

The judge named four seats that share the loss when a degree doesn't pay, and left how much each should carry to you. Open one to see what the record says that seat has, what it carries, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1The student
    What this seat has
    Their own federal loans have an exit through public service. Work full time in public service, teaching in a public school included, make 120 qualifying payments, and the rest is forgiven, tax-free. Counting its temporary fixes, about 1.3 million borrowers have had about $96 billion cancelled.
    What it carries
    The student carries the loss today, with the parent and the taxpayer. A dependent student can borrow only $31,000 in federal loans in total, so the rest of the bill comes from savings, work, a parent's loan or a private lender. A private loan has no federal exit: bankruptcy only, and only if repaying would be an undue hardship. And the debt that goes unpaid sits mostly with students who don't finish.

    On the record: row 19.4 of the fact-check log, row 19.7 of the fact-check log, row 17.9 of the fact-check log, row 19.11 of the fact-check log, row 12.9 of the fact-check log

  2. 2The parent
    What this seat has
    Since this past July, a parent's federal loan finally has a ceiling: $20,000 a year and $65,000 per student. Before that, the only limit was the school's own price.
    What it carries
    The narrowest exits. A parent's loan taken out since this past July can't count toward public-service forgiveness at all. Older parent loans keep a narrow path, and only through the parent's own public-service job. At about seven in a hundred bachelor's programs, the typical parent loan alone is bigger than what the graduate earns in a year, four years out.
    Not measured
    Whether parents pay these loans back: the Education Department publishes no default rate for parent loans, and the school faces no consequence when they go unpaid.

    On the record: row 4.1 of the fact-check log, row 4.2 of the fact-check log, row 19.14 of the fact-check log, row 20.10 of the fact-check log, row 20.12 of the fact-check log

  3. 3The taxpayer
    What this seat has
    Its losses sit in subsidized undergraduate loans and in the plans that set payments by income, relief Congress chose to give. Parent loans actually make the government money, on the official books.
    What it carries
    Washington is the lender now. It expects to lose about 5 cents on each dollar lent on the official books, about 17 at a market price.

    On the record: row 18.2 of the fact-check log, row 18.30 of the fact-check log, row 18.31 of the fact-check log

  4. 4The college
    What this seat has
    The college sets the price, and today it repays none of the loss. The House voted in 2025 to make it repay a share of its students' unpaid loans, and the law left it off.
    What it carries
    Risk-sharing's own advocates warn about access for low-income students: charge a college when its students struggle, and you hand it a reason not to admit the students likeliest to struggle. And a pay test can't tell a needed job, like teaching, from a bad program, so any rule has to protect the teacher.
    Not measured
    Whether making colleges carry it would help: nobody has measured risk-sharing on degree-granting colleges, the only evidence is for-profit colleges cut off in the 1990s, and the House formula was never tested.

    On the record: row 18.19 of the fact-check log, row 18.20 of the fact-check log, row 18.22 of the fact-check log, row 18.24 of the fact-check log, row 18.38 of the fact-check log

Where the record leans: On who carries the risk today, it isn't close: the borrower, the parent and the taxpayer, not the college. On whether making colleges carry it would help, there's no record either way.
