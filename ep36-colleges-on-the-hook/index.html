Student Loans: Should Colleges Be on the Hook When a Degree Doesn't Pay?

The question: Should colleges be on the hook when a degree doesn't pay?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

When a degree does not pay, the student, the parent and the taxpayer carry the loss. Two AI teams argue whether the college should share it; you decide the split.

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in October 2026, a new federal test was coming: from July 2027, programs whose graduates earn less than young workers with only a high-school diploma can lose federal loans. It never asks what a program costs, and a House plan making colleges repay a share of unpaid loans was dropped from the 2025 law.

The fight

Green arguesPut colleges on the hook

Washington lends without asking what a program costs or pays, and no college repays a dollar when a loan goes bad. Four tries to tie loans to results were struck down, repealed or narrowed. So put colleges on the hook for a share of what their own students can't repay, while protecting the jobs we need.

Gold arguesFix price and finishing

The unpaid debt sits mostly with students who don't finish, graduate programs and parents' loans. Charging colleges when students struggle gives them a reason not to admit those students, and a pay test can't tell a teacher from a bad program. So test the price, as Texas does, and watch who finishes; parents' loans finally have a ceiling.

What both sides quietly agree on

Washington lends without asking what a program costs or pays. When a loan goes unpaid, the student, the parent and the taxpayer carry it, and no school repays a lost dollar. The new test asks whether a program pays, never what it costs.

What the shouting gets wrong

The record leans hard on one half: the borrower, the parent and the taxpayer carry the risk, and the college doesn't. Whether making colleges carry a share would help has no record either way, because nobody has measured it on degree-granting colleges. And the unpaid debt mostly isn't where the anger points: it sits with students who don't finish, far more than with graduates, and with graduate programs and parents.

The verdict

Andre This one doesn't resolve, and neither team wins it. Three things from me: what's settled, what survived, and what's yours.

Andre Both teams accepted the same record. Washington lends without asking what a program costs or pays. When a loan goes unpaid, the student, the parent and the taxpayer carry it. The college carries almost none of it: a few schools a year lose aid, and no school repays a lost dollar. And the new test asks whether a program pays, never what it costs.

Andre What survived from green. Every try to tie loans to results was struck down, repealed or narrowed, and under the twenty fourteen rule not one program lost aid. The new test fails about one bachelor's program in ninety. And when aid was cut in the nineties, students moved: total enrollment fell three percent.

Andre What survived from gold. The debt that goes unpaid sits mostly with students who don't finish, graduate programs and parents. Risk-sharing's own advocates warn about access for low-income students. And a pay test can't tell a needed job, like teaching, from a bad program.

Andre What each side had to give up. Green: nobody has measured risk-sharing on degree-granting colleges, and his rule has to protect the teacher. Gold: Washington doesn't set what colleges charge, so fixing the price still needs a lever.

Andre So here's where the record leans. On who carries the risk today, it isn't close: the borrower, the parent and the taxpayer, not the college. On whether making colleges carry it would help, there's no record either way. That half is yours.

Andre Picture four seats. The student, whose federal loans have an exit through public service. The parent, whose loan has the narrowest exits. The taxpayer, about five cents on each dollar lent on the official books, about seventeen at a market price. And the college, which the House voted to put on the hook in twenty twenty-five, and the law left off.

Andre So the question we're handing you isn't punish colleges, yes or no. It's this: when a degree doesn't pay, how much of the loss should each seat carry? That one's yours.
