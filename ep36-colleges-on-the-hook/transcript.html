Cold open

NarratorShould colleges be on the hook when a degree doesn't pay? Picture a teacher, four years out of college.

NarratorThey earn about forty-seven thousand dollars, typical for that degree. Say they borrowed eighty thousand to get there. As a dependent student, only thirty-one thousand could be their own federal loan. The rest is a parent's loan, or a private one.

NarratorIf that debt can't be paid, the teacher, the parent and the taxpayer carry it. The college that set the price repays none of it.

The research · The aid offer: who lends, and on what test

KateStart with the aid offer. A family opens one this fall.

NiaWhat does it actually say?

KateAfter grants, a typical state university costs about fifteen thousand dollars a year. A private nonprofit, about twenty-six thousand. Over four years, roughly sixty thousand, or a hundred and five.

NiaAnd how much of that can the student borrow?

NiaWho decides how much a student can borrow? Does anyone look at the program?

KateNo. Federal student loans are open to every eligible student at an approved school. How much depends on your year in school and whether you're counted as your parents' dependent. Not on the field. Not on the price. Not on what graduates earn.

NiaSo who sets the price?

KateThe college. Washington doesn't set what a college charges. And until this past July, a parent's federal loan had no ceiling but the school's own price. Now it stops at twenty thousand a year, and sixty-five thousand per student.

NiaSo the test for the loan is basically: are you enrolled?

KateAt an approved school. The test isn't what the program costs, and it isn't whether it'll pay.

NiaThen why is that allowed?

The research · Four tries in fifteen years, and the new test

KateFour tries in fifteen years. Let me walk you through them.

NiaGo.

KateTwenty eleven: a rule that tested whether a program's students were repaying their loans. A court struck it down the next year. Twenty fourteen: a new rule, with a debt test. It was repealed after one year of results. Not one program ever lost aid, though more than two thousand were failing or close to it.

NiaNot one?

KateNot one. Twenty twenty-three, a third try: only programs at for-profit colleges, and certificate programs, could lose aid. Everyone else just had to disclose.

NiaAnd the old rule on defaults?

KateA school loses aid if too many of its borrowers default three years running. It catches a handful of schools a year, and most win on appeal. Government auditors found schools hiring consultants who pushed borrowers behind on their payments into a pause, which blunts that three-year count.

NiaSo what's new?

KateThe twenty twenty-five law. From July twenty twenty-seven, nearly every program gets tested. If its graduates, four years out, earn less than working adults aged twenty-five to thirty-four with only a high-school diploma, in two years out of three, the program loses federal student loans.

NiaThat sounds tough.

KateWhat it misses: it never subtracts the price. It counts only graduates, never the students who don't finish. It doesn't touch the parent's loan. And the Education Department projects it fails about one bachelor's program in ninety. For undergraduate certificates, about one in four.

NiaOne in ninety?

KateAt the bachelor's level, yes. It bites hardest on certificates and for-profit colleges: about four in ten for-profit schools fail a second, school-wide test that can cost their failing programs all federal aid.

NiaDid anyone try making the college pay?

KateYes. The House version of that same law made every college repay a share of its students' unpaid loans, starting in twenty twenty-eight. The Senate swapped it for the earnings test. The law that passed has no such section.

NiaSo the college was on the hook, and then it wasn't.

KateOn the table, and off it, in the same year. Hold that.

The research · Who pays when a loan isn't repaid

KateThree places the loss can land: the borrower, the taxpayer, and the school.

NiaStart with the taxpayer.

KateWashington is the lender now. The Congressional Budget Office expects to lose about five cents on each new dollar it lends, on the official books. Price the risk the way a bank would, and it's about seventeen cents.

NiaAnd the school?

KateNothing. Two economists who proposed a fix, writing for Brookings, put it plainly: colleges do not currently incur any costs when the loans systematically fail to be repaid.

NiaSo it's the borrower and the taxpayer. Where's the debt that never gets paid back?

KateMostly not where people point. Among bachelor's programs, the typical graduate's federal debt is bigger than a year's pay in about three programs in a thousand. Among master's programs, about eight in a hundred.

NiaSo it's graduate school.

KateThat's part of it. Graduate students take about half of new federal loans, and they're about one student in six. But the biggest group is people who never finished. Among students who started in two thousand three, forty-five percent of borrowers who left with no credential defaulted within twelve years. Bachelor's graduates: eight percent.

NiaForty-five against eight.

KateAnd the third place is the parent. At about seven in a hundred bachelor's programs, the typical parent loan alone is bigger than what the graduate earns in a year, four years out.

NiaDoes anyone track whether parents pay those back?

KateNot officially. Brookings found the Education Department publishes no default rate for parent loans, and the school faces no consequence when they go unpaid.

NiaAnd the taxpayer's losses. Where do those sit?

KateIn subsidized undergraduate loans and in the plans that set payments by income. Parent loans actually make the government money, on the official books.

NiaSo the loss lands on borrowers, parents and taxpayers. The school that set the price repays none of it. Hold that.

The research · The teacher: a needed degree, a split bill

KateBack to the teacher from the start. Four years out, the typical teaching graduate earns about forty-seven thousand dollars.

NiaIs the degree overpriced?

KateNot by the usual yardstick. Four years at a state school, after grants, comes to about a year and a quarter of that pay. At a private nonprofit, nearly two years.

NiaAnd do we need teachers?

KateThe Bureau of Labor Statistics projects about two hundred and thirty thousand openings a year for school teachers, kindergarten through high school. And the Economic Policy Institute, a think tank that leans toward labor, estimates teachers earn about twenty-seven percent less a week than similar college graduates.

NiaSo we need them, and we pay them less. What happens to that eighty thousand?

KateSplit it in two. Their own federal loans, up to thirty-one thousand, have an exit. Teach full time in a public school, make a hundred and twenty qualifying payments, and the rest is forgiven, tax-free. That's Public Service Loan Forgiveness.

NiaTen years. What do they pay in the meantime?

KateUnder the new repayment plan, at forty-seven thousand, our own arithmetic puts it at about a hundred and fifty-seven dollars a month, before any credit for children.

NiaDoes that forgiveness actually happen? I remember it didn't.

KateThe early program approved almost no one. Now, counting its temporary fixes, about one point three million borrowers have had about ninety-six billion dollars cancelled.

NiaAnd the rest of the eighty thousand?

KateThat's the part with no real exit. A parent's loan taken out since this past July can't count toward that forgiveness at all. Older parent loans keep a narrow path, and only through the parent's own public-service job. And a private loan has no federal exit. Bankruptcy only, and only if repaying would be an undue hardship.

NiaSo the federal part has a door, and the parent's part mostly doesn't.

KateThat's the teacher's case, and it's the hard case for any rule. When the Education Department wrote the new test, commenters asked it to exempt teaching, social work and counseling. Its answer: students still need enough earnings to afford and repay their loans.

NiaIs teaching unusual?

KateTake one yardstick for every field: the New York Fed's figures on recent graduates. Teaching and social services land jobs that need the degree, at below-average pay. The fields that fall short on both counts are led by criminal justice, the performing arts and fine arts, with many general degrees beside them, from business management to biology.

NiaSo needed and well paid are two different questions. Hold that.

MaraSo fix these instead. Test the price, not just the pay: Texas already counts a credential only if it beats its cost. Watch finishing: England's regulator reviews courses where too many students don't continue or complete. And the parent loan finally has a ceiling.

MaraAnd price is more than tuition. At a state university, the biggest part of a student's bill after grants is housing and food, about two-thirds of it. A rule about the college's balance sheet doesn't touch that line.

The verdict

AndreThis one doesn't resolve, and neither team wins it. Three things from me: what's settled, what survived, and what's yours.

AndreBoth teams accepted the same record. Washington lends without asking what a program costs or pays. When a loan goes unpaid, the student, the parent and the taxpayer carry it. The college carries almost none of it: a few schools a year lose aid, and no school repays a lost dollar. And the new test asks whether a program pays, never what it costs.

AndreWhat survived from green. Every try to tie loans to results was struck down, repealed or narrowed, and under the twenty fourteen rule not one program lost aid. The new test fails about one bachelor's program in ninety. And when aid was cut in the nineties, students moved: total enrollment fell three percent.

AndreWhat survived from gold. The debt that goes unpaid sits mostly with students who don't finish, graduate programs and parents. Risk-sharing's own advocates warn about access for low-income students. And a pay test can't tell a needed job, like teaching, from a bad program.

AndreWhat each side had to give up. Green: nobody has measured risk-sharing on degree-granting colleges, and his rule has to protect the teacher. Gold: Washington doesn't set what colleges charge, so fixing the price still needs a lever.

AndreSo here's where the record leans. On who carries the risk today, it isn't close: the borrower, the parent and the taxpayer, not the college. On whether making colleges carry it would help, there's no record either way. That half is yours.

AndrePicture four seats. The student, whose federal loans have an exit through public service. The parent, whose loan has the narrowest exits. The taxpayer, about five cents on each dollar lent on the official books, about seventeen at a market price. And the college, which the House voted to put on the hook in twenty twenty-five, and the law left off.

AndreSo the question we're handing you isn't punish colleges, yes or no. It's this: when a degree doesn't pay, how much of the loss should each seat carry? That one's yours.
