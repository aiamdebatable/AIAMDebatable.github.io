## The pitch, in one line

**When a degree doesn't pay, the student, the parent and the taxpayer carry the loss, and the college that
set the price carries none of it.** Washington lends to every eligible student without asking what the
program costs or what it pays; every past attempt to tie those loans to results was struck down,
repealed or narrowed; and the House's 2025 plan to make colleges repay part of the loss was dropped for
an earnings test that ignores price. Should colleges be on the hook, or would that shut the door on the
poorer students and the low-paid jobs the country needs?

## The viewer's moment

Two people. (Invented and unnamed; never a real or identifiable person.)

- **A graduate, or a parent, holding a debt the school never shared.** A typical in-state public
  degree costs about **$61,000** over four years after grants, a typical private nonprofit about
  **$106,000** (log 17.2, 17.3). The student can borrow **$31,000** in federal loans in total (log
  17.9); the rest came from savings, work, a parent's PLUS loan or a private lender (log 17.10, 17.11).
  If the degree doesn't pay, the bill doesn't shrink. The school was paid in full on day one.
- **A teacher four years out, earning about $47,000** (the national median for teacher-education
  graduates, log 8.1), who borrowed about $80,000 between their own federal loans and a parent's or
  private loan (a hypothetical built from the $31,000 cap). Teaching is a job the country needs, with
  about 228,000 K–12 openings a year (log 21.27). Their own federal loans have an exit; the parent's loan
  and any private loan mostly don't (log 19.4–19.14).

The episode follows the money from the aid offer to the unpaid balance and asks, at each step, who set
the price, who lent the money, who checked whether it would pay, and who is left holding it.

## The question

**Should colleges be on the hook when a degree doesn't pay?**

Green: **yes**, make colleges carry part of the risk, and stop lending for programs that don't pay.
Gold: **no**, that cuts access for poorer students and hits needed low-pay fields; the real killers are
price and not finishing.

## Why now

Under a final rule published 2026-07-01 and effective **2027-07-01**, an **undergraduate program** fails
if its graduates' median earnings, in the fourth tax year after finishing, are **less than** those of
**working adults aged 25–34 with only a high-school diploma** in the school's state (or nationally). A
program that fails in **two of any three** years loses federal **Direct Loans**; ED's analysis puts the
first sanction year in the award year starting **July 2028**
([Federal Register 2026-13286](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-01/pdf/2026-13286.pdf);
log 1.10, 2.1, 2.5). ED projects, in the final rule's Table 5.12, that **1.1% of bachelor's programs**
(0.6% of their students) fail, against **6.0%** of associate programs and **24.1% of undergraduate
certificates** (27.6% of certificate students); **5.2%** of all programs. At the institution level, a
school with half its aid recipients or aid in failing programs can lose all federal aid for those
programs unless it bars loans in them, and ED's Table 5.11 has **43% of for-profit institutions** failing
that test (both tables typed by hand from ED's images; re-read the images before airing a table figure;
log 3.1, 3.4). ED says it will publish the program-level data in **2027**
([FSA, GENERAL-26-49](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/knowledge-center/library/electronic-announcements/2026-08-11/guidance-fvt/ge-data-reporting-stats-early-implementation-and-next-steps-publication-updated-aug-28-2026);
log 1.12).

The test comes from the **2025 reconciliation law** (Public Law 119-21), which put an earnings-only test
in statute for all programs and dropped the old debt-to-earnings test (log 2.13, 18.14). The same law
changed what families can borrow from **2026-07-01**: **Parent PLUS** is capped at **$20,000 a year and
$65,000 per student**, where before it ran to the school's full cost of attendance; **Grad PLUS** ends for
new loans; and a new **Repayment Assistance Plan** replaces the income-driven plans for new loans (log
4.1–4.9, 18.5, 18.7;
[FR 2026-08556](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-05-01/pdf/2026-08556.pdf);
[P.L. 119-21](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ21/html/PLAW-119publ21.htm)).

And the road not taken: the **House-passed** version of that bill made every school **repay part of its
students' unpaid loans** from award year 2028–29, 100% where completers' value-added earnings were
negative ([H.R. 1, Engrossed in House](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr1eh/html/BILLS-119hr1eh.htm);
log 18.19). The Senate swapped that for the earnings test, and the enacted law has no such section (log
18.20).

**So Washington has written down its first real answer to "why is that allowed?", and the answer is a
floor: beat a young high-school graduate's pay.** It does not subtract the price. It does not count the
students who don't finish. It does not touch the parent's loan. At the bachelor's level it fails about
one program in ninety (log 3.1). The House's answer, that the college should pay part of the loss, was
on the table in 2025 and came off it. That is the debate.

- **Description (hot):** the 2027 earnings rule cutting off student loans to programs that fail; the
  dropped House plan in one clause. Keywords: `student loan`, `2027 earnings rule`.
- **Script (structural):** "From July 2027, a program whose graduates earn less than a young worker with
  only a high-school diploma, two years out of three, loses federal loans. That's the first time
  Washington has asked whether a program pays. It still doesn't ask what it costs, and the college still
  pays nothing back."

## Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these. The blocks below are the system record; the money a family pays (TOTAL
COST), the exits (HOW GRADUATES GET OUT) and the one-yardstick table also serve the companion episode and
are stated once, briefly.

### WHY IT'S ALLOWED: who lends, and on what test

- **The lender is Washington, and the law tells it to lend to every eligible student** at an approved
  school: Direct Loans are funded with "such sums as may be necessary" (20 USC 1087a; log 18.1–18.2).
  Undergraduate loan size depends on **class year and dependency**, never on field, price or likely pay
  (log 18.3–18.4). The only credit screen is PLUS's "adverse credit history" check (log 18.6, APPROXIMATE
  as "the only"). Schools are approved by accreditors and states, not on outcomes (log 18.8,
  APPROXIMATE). There is **no federal cap on what a college charges**; price is disclosure-only in the
  2027 rule (log 18.35, APPROXIMATE as an absence; log 2.10).
- **The caps leaked through parents and grad school:** until 2026-07-01, Parent PLUS and Grad PLUS were
  limited only by the school's cost of attendance (log 18.5); now Parent PLUS is $20k/$65k and Grad
  PLUS is ended (log 18.7).
- Spoken: "The test for a federal student loan is: are you enrolled at an approved school. Not: what does
  it cost, and will it pay."

### EVERY ATTEMPT TO TIE LOANS TO RESULTS — narrowed, struck down, repealed or gamed

- **2011:** the first gainful-employment rule was vacated in court in 2012 (log 18.9).
- **2014:** a program could lose aid after failing a debt-to-earnings test in 2 of 3 years (log 18.10);
  the rule was **rescinded** (published 2019, effective 2020) after one year of rates, with **no program
  ever losing aid** though 2,050 were failing or in the zone (log 18.11–18.12).
- **2023:** loss of aid covered only gainful-employment programs, mostly for-profits and certificates;
  everyone else got disclosure (log 18.13).
- **90/10** applies to for-profits only (log 18.15). **Cohort default rates** cost a school its aid only
  after three straight years at 30%+ (log 18.16) and hit a handful of schools a year, most winning appeal
  (12 sanctioned for FY2017, 10 won; APPROXIMATE, log 18.17); GAO found schools pushing borrowers into
  forbearance to run out the clock (log 18.18). The official default rate, 0.4% for FY2023, is a
  payment-pause artefact (log 10.5).
- **2025:** the law put an **earnings-only** test in statute for **all** programs and dropped the debt
  test (log 18.14). A failing program loses only Direct Loans; a tie passes; appeals are for calculation
  errors only; a failing program is out for two years (log 2.2, 2.7, 2.8, 2.9).
- **Does cutting off aid change behaviour?** In the 1990s, sanctioned for-profits lost ~40% of enrolment,
  community colleges took many of those students, and total enrolment fell just 3% (NBER w22967, 2016
  version, log 18.38). Under the 2014 rule colleges closed failing programs though none lost aid
  (APPROXIMATE, an author's blog on a working paper, log 15.2).

### WHO PAYS WHEN LOANS AREN'T REPAID: the borrower and the taxpayer, never the school

- **CBO:** about **5 cents per dollar lent** on the official basis, about **17 cents** on a fair-value
  basis, for 2027 (log 18.30). The losses sit in subsidized undergraduate loans and income-driven
  repayment, while **Parent PLUS makes money for the government** (log 18.31).
- **GAO:** 25 years of Direct Loans first booked as $114 billion income were by FY2021 estimated to cost
  $197 billion, $102 billion of the swing from pandemic relief (log 18.32, before RAP). The 2025 law moved
  the estimate back by about $50 billion, mainly through RAP (log 18.33).
- **The school:** "do not currently incur any costs when the loans systematically fail to be repaid"
  (Looney and Yannelis, log 18.22). A "share never repaid" is UNRESOLVED and is not aired (log 18.34).
- About **1,800 institutions** have non-repayment rates of 25% or more (FSA, August 2026, log 10.6).
- Spoken: "On every new dollar Washington lends, it expects to lose about a nickel on its own books, about
  seventeen cents if you price the risk like a bank would. The college that set the price loses nothing."

### THE ROAD NOT TAKEN: risk-sharing, 2025

- The House bill: every school remits part of its students' unpaid loans from 2028–29, **100%** where
  completers' value-added earnings are negative, **0%** where they exceed the price (log 18.19). The
  enacted law dropped it (log 18.20). CBO's "more than $6 billion" saving reaches us only through NPR
  (log 18.21, APPROXIMATE). Who would have paid most: for-profit undergraduate and private nonprofit
  graduate programs (one analyst's estimate, APPROXIMATE, log 18.28).

### THE 2027 TEST: what it sees and what it misses

- It counts **only graduates**, working and not enrolled, four years out (log 2.5, 2.11). It does **not**
  subtract price or debt (log 2.10). The undergraduate bar does **not** vary by field (log 2.1).
- **Who fails:** bachelor's 1.1% of programs, associate 6.0%, undergraduate certificates 24.1%; 5.2% of
  all programs (log 3.1, 3.3). 43% of for-profit institutions fail the institution-level test (log 3.4);
  18.0% of for-profit students are in failing programs vs 1.8% at publics (log 3.5). "93%" is
  cosmetology **certificate** programs (log 3.6).
- **Price-aware tests already exist elsewhere:** Texas counts a "credential of value" only if earnings
  beat both the cost of education and high-school earnings, and ties community-college funding to it (log
  14.1–14.2); Colorado finds 93% of bachelor's programs meet its threshold, no penalty (log 14.3); England
  nets out price (about 20% of women and 30% of men can expect a negative return, log 14.7), and its
  regulator reviews courses on continuation and completion, as a trigger, not a breach (log 14.10).
- ED's answer to the commenters who asked to exempt teaching, social work and counselling: "students
  need sufficient earnings to afford and repay their Title IV student loans" (log 18.27).

### TOTAL COST — what a degree really costs a family (ruling 4) [shared with the companion]

- **After grants, four years** (ED's College Scorecard, our arithmetic; first-time full-time aided
  students, in-state at publics, 2023–24; enrollment-weighted median): public in-state **$15,364 a year,
  $61,456 for four**; private nonprofit **$26,505, $106,020** (log 17.1–17.3); by family income, public
  **$10,126 → $22,742** a year, private nonprofit **$18,557 → $33,731** (log 17.5).
- **The federal cap:** a dependent undergraduate can borrow **$31,000** in total, unchanged by the 2025
  law (log 17.9, 18.4). So **about $30,000** of a typical public degree, **about $75,000** of a private one,
  comes from somewhere else (our arithmetic).
- **How families cover it** (Sallie Mae/Ipsos 2025; ⚠ Sallie Mae is the largest private student lender:
  attribute): 48% of families borrowed: 11% Parent PLUS, 12% private student loans; 17% withdrew
  retirement savings (log 17.10–17.11); 35% of borrowing families say loan access made them consider
  pricier schools (self-report, log 17.12). Private loans: about $13.9 billion a year (College Board
  estimate, log 17.13).
- **Price is set by the school, not the field; pay is what moves** (four-year net cost ÷ ED's median pay
  four years out; our arithmetic): teacher ed **1.3** years of pay at a public, **1.9** at a private
  nonprofit; drama **1.6 / 3.1**; nursing and computer science about half to two-thirds of a year at a
  public (log 17.15–17.19).
- Housing and food are **$13,900 of a $21,340** full net cost at a public in-state university, about
  **two-thirds**; net tuition is $2,300 (College Board 2025–26, log 22.24, 9.4). A different measure from
  Scorecard's: never in one sum or chart.

### WHERE THE DEBT THAT NEVER GETS PAID BACK SITS

- **Not in bachelor's federal debt, mostly:** 57 of 21,801 bachelor's programs (**0.3%**) have median
  federal debt above graduates' median pay, against **7.9% of master's programs** (graduates who
  borrowed, federal only, our arithmetic, log 20.5). Master's over 1× pay cluster in private schools and
  in counselling, therapy and arts fields (log 20.9); master's drama debt is about **2×** pay (log 20.7).
  Graduate students take ~**48%** of new federal loans while being ~16–17% of students (log 20.14);
  **half of outstanding federal debt is held by the 11% who owe $80,000+** (log 20.15).
- **Parent PLUS is the invisible part:** $115 billion outstanding for 3.5 million students (log 20.11);
  ED publishes no Parent PLUS default rate and schools face no consequence (Brookings, APPROXIMATE, log
  20.12); at **7.1% of bachelor's programs** the parent's PLUS median alone tops the graduate's pay (log
  20.10); drama parents borrow a median ~$62,000 (log 20.4).
- **Non-completers are the largest group:** **45%** of borrowers who left with no credential defaulted
  within 12 years, vs **8%** of bachelor's finishers (NCES, 2003–04 cohort, log 12.9); in 2024, **41%**
  were behind on payments vs 11% of bachelor's holders (log 12.5). 61% of 2019 starters finished within
  six years; about 3 in 10 left with nothing (log 11.1–11.2); 43.1 million Americans have some college and
  no credential (log 12.1), earning about a tenth more than a high-school diploma (log 12.2). The 2027
  test never sees them (log 2.11).
- **CBO's forgiveness shares (⚠ DATED, February 2020):** of loans on income-driven plans, ~21% of
  undergraduate and ~56% of graduate dollars were projected to be forgiven (log 20.18). "In 2020, CBO
  projected."
- **FREOPP (attribute; a model):** 23% of bachelor's and 43% of master's programs have negative ROI;
  ~29% of Pell and loan dollars went to negative-ROI programs (log 20.20, 20.24).

  typical graduate on both counts". **34 of 73 fall short on both, 17 on one, 22 pass** (our arithmetic,

### HOW GRADUATES GET OUT [shared with the companion]

- **PSLF:** what is left of a **Direct Loan** is cancelled, tax-free, after **120 qualifying payments**
  while working full time in public service; public-school teaching and public child/family social work
  are named (log 19.4, 19.10). RAP payments count (log 19.5). The early program approved almost no one
  (TEPSLF 1%, 661 of ~54,000, log 19.6); **about 1.29 million borrowers have had $96.4 billion discharged**
  through August 2026 (log 19.7; re-read the sheet rows before airing). From 2026-07-01 ED can disqualify
  employers with a "substantial illegal purpose" (log 19.8).
- **Teacher Loan Forgiveness:** after **5 consecutive years in a low-income school**, up to **$5,000**, or
  **$17,500** for secondary math/science or special education (log 19.1); about **13,800** got it in
  FY2025, down from ~45,000 in FY2018 (log 19.2). The same years can't count for both TLF and PSLF (log
  19.3).
- **RAP / income-driven forgiveness:** after **360 payments (30 years)** under RAP (log 4.8, 19.9); ⚠
  **taxable** if granted from 2026 (plain reading of the statute, log 19.10).
- **Bankruptcy:** federal and private student loans survive unless repaying is an "undue hardship" (log
  19.11). Since November 2022 a DOJ/ED attestation lets government lawyers agree to discharge; in the
  first 10 months 632 cases were filed, and in **99% of the cases a court had ruled on** the government
  recommended and the court granted full or partial discharge (log 19.12–19.13).
- **Parent PLUS:** a parent borrowing on or after 2026-07-01 gets only a Tiered Standard plan, **no
  PSLF**; older Parent PLUS keeps a PSLF route only through consolidation before 2026-07-01 plus an IDR
  plan by 2028-07-01, and PSLF turns on the **parent's** job (log 19.14, 4.9, 4.10).

### THE TEACHER CASE — the heart of this episode (Lucas, Q3)

A teacher on **$47,000** who borrowed about **$80,000** (hypothetical; federal + parent/private). As a
dependent student they could borrow at most **$31,000** themselves; the other ~**$49,000** is Parent PLUS
(their parent's debt) or private loans (log 17.9, 18.4). Their own Direct Loans have an exit: **PSLF**
after ten years in a public school, tax-free; on RAP at $47,000 their payment would be about **$157 a
month** before the $50-per-dependent credit (our arithmetic on RAP's 4% bracket, flat pay; not an ED
example; log 19.4, 19.5, 4.6). TLF needs five years in a low-income school and those years can't also
count for PSLF (log 19.1, 19.3). **The parent's PLUS loan has an exit only through the parent's own
public-service job, and only on the narrow legacy path; the private loans have no federal exit except
bankruptcy's undue-hardship test** (log 19.14, 19.11).

One line: **the federal part has an exit; the parent and private parts mostly don't.** Green asks why the
school that set the price was never at risk. Gold asks whether a rule that punishes the school would have
closed the program the country needs: teacher education costs about **1.3 years of pay at a public** (log
17.15), and it is exactly the kind of field ED's commenters asked to exempt (log 18.27).

### What a degree pays, on average [shared, one line]

A bachelor's holder earns about **60% more** than a high-school graduate, raw (BLS 2025: $1,578 vs $966 a
week; NCES 2024: $74,480 vs $46,380; log 6.1, 6.2), not the 75% that counts graduate degrees (log 6.4).
Neither team argues that college doesn't pay on average; they argue about who carries the risk when one
program doesn't.

## Contested (the real fight)

**GREEN — "Put colleges on the hook": make colleges carry part of the risk, and stop lending for programs
that don't pay.**

The college sets the price, picks who enrols and decides what to offer, and when the loan goes bad it
loses nothing: Looney and Yannelis's words are that colleges "do not currently incur any costs when the
loans systematically fail to be repaid" (log 18.22), and AEI's Cooper argues "colleges reap benefits from
student loans without bearing much of the risk" (log 18.23). Washington lends to every eligible student
with no test of field, price or likely pay (log 18.1–18.4), and until July 2026 the parent loan had no
ceiling but the school's own price (log 18.5). Every rule that tried to tie loans to results was struck
down, repealed, narrowed to for-profits or gamed by forbearance (log 18.9–18.18); in 2014–2019 not one
program lost aid (log 18.11). Taxpayers lose about 5 cents per dollar on the official books and 17 on a
fair-value basis (log 18.30). Cutting off aid doesn't empty classrooms, it moves students: in the 1990s
total enrolment fell just 3% as community colleges took the students of sanctioned for-profits (log 18.38).
The 2027 test is a start but ignores price (log 2.10) and fails about one bachelor's program in ninety
(log 3.1); price-aware tests already run in Texas and England (log 14.1, 14.7). The House passed exactly
this in 2025 (log 18.19). Green adds the teacher: the school that charged a family $80,000 for a
$47,000 job never had to ask whether that bill could be paid.

**GOLD — "Fix price and finishing": a penalty on colleges cuts access for poorer students and hits
needed low-pay fields; the real killers are price and not finishing.**

Risk-sharing's own designers list the side effects: "reduced access to education for low-income
students, pass-through tuition increases, gaming of accountability metrics" (Looney and Yannelis, log
18.24); a college that pays when students struggle has a reason to stop admitting the students likeliest
to struggle. The House formula skipped defaulted loans and needed data that, in one expert's words, "we
do not have and have never had" (named experts via NPR, APPROXIMATE, log 18.25), and its incentives would make schools reconsider offering loans
at all (Urban's Blagg, APPROXIMATE, log 18.26). The fields a pay test hits are the ones the country needs:
commenters asked ED to exempt teaching, social work and counselling (log 18.27); teachers earn about 27%
less per week than comparable graduates (EPI, log 22.43) while K–12 schools need about 228,000 hires a
year (log 21.27). The taxpayer's losses sit mostly in income-driven relief Congress chose to give, and
Parent PLUS makes money (log 18.31). The bachelor's debt problem is small at the program level (0.3% of
programs over 1× pay, log 20.5); the unpayable debt sits in **non-completers** (45% default vs 8%, log
12.9), **graduate programs** (log 20.5–20.9, 20.14) and **parent loans** (log 20.10–20.12). And the
biggest piece of a public student's bill isn't tuition at all: housing and food are about two-thirds
(log 22.24). Fix what drives the loss (price, finishing, the parent loan), not the school's balance
sheet.

**The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:**

- *Gold at green:* **the record on risk-sharing for degree-granting colleges is empty.** The only
  behavioural evidence is 1990s default sanctions on for-profits (log 18.38) and the 2014 rule's
  closures (APPROXIMATE, log 15.2). **The House formula was not tested** and its savings figure reaches us
  only through NPR (log 18.21). **A pay test can't tell a needed low-pay field from a bad program**; the
  teacher case is a needed degree at a below-average wage. **Green's villain is mostly not the bachelor's
  degree:** 1.1% of bachelor's programs fail the 2027 floor; certificates (24.1%) and for-profits (43% of
  institutions) are where it bites (log 3.1, 3.4).
- *Green at gold:* **"fix price" with what lever?** There is no federal cap on what a college charges
  (APPROXIMATE as an absence, log 18.35); the loan is the only lever Washington holds. **The access
  warning comes from the plan's own designers, who still proposed it** (log 18.22, 18.24). **Income-driven
  relief exists because the bill couldn't be paid**: blaming Congress's relief for the losses moves the
  cost, it doesn't explain it. **Non-completion and parent loans are the school's business too**: the
  school admits the student and is paid with the parent's loan, and no one tracks a Parent PLUS default
  rate (log 20.12, APPROXIMATE). **Cutting aid moved students rather than shutting them out** (total
  enrolment −3%, log 18.38).

**Where the record leans, said plainly.** On **who carries the risk today**, the record is not 50/50: the
borrower, the parent and the taxpayer do, and the school does not (log 18.22, 18.30–18.31). On **whether
making colleges carry it would help**, the record is thin on both sides: nobody has measured risk-sharing
on degree-granting colleges, the one natural experiment is 1990s for-profits, and the access warning is
a prediction, not a measurement. Equal length on "documented failures" would misrepresent this: green
has the stronger record on the problem, and neither side has a record on the cure.

## The reframe

The shouting asks "should colleges be punished for useless degrees?" The record says three things that
question misses.

1. **The unpayable debt is mostly not where the anger points.** Bachelor's federal debt rarely tops a
   year's pay (0.3% of programs); the loss piles up with people who **don't finish**, with **graduate**
   programs, and with **parents** (log 20.5, 12.9, 20.10–20.15). A rule aimed at "useless majors" misses
   most of it.
2. **Washington's new test asks "does it pay?" but never "at what price?"** Price is disclosure-only and
   the bar doesn't move with cost (log 2.10, 18.35). Two programs with the same pay pass or fail together,
   whatever they cost.
3. **"Needed" and "well paid" are different questions.** Teaching and social work land jobs at
   below-average pay (log 21.11); criminal justice, the arts and many general degrees fall short on both
   counts (log 21.10, 21.12). Any rule that ties loans to pay has to say what it does with a needed job
   that pays less.

---

## Both sides quietly agree

**Nobody checks the price.** Green wants the college to pay when the bill can't be repaid; gold wants the
bill to be smaller in the first place. Both are reaching for the same missing lever: no federal test
compares what a program **costs** with what it **pays** (log 2.10, 18.35), and the price-aware tests that
exist are in Texas and England, not Washington (log 14.1, 14.7).

And both agree **the student who doesn't finish and the parent who borrowed are the people nobody
measures.** The 2027 test counts only graduates (log 2.11); ED publishes no Parent PLUS default rate (log
20.12, APPROXIMATE); and those are where the defaults are (log 12.9).

## Verdict framing

"Where the record leans, and the verdict you give." Candidates, each to be **earned in the debate** and
dropped if it is not:

- **Settled, said by the judge:** today the risk of a degree that doesn't pay is carried by the student,
  the parent and the taxpayer, and never by the college that set the price.
- **Leans, said by the judge:** the 2027 floor will catch certificates and for-profits far more than
  bachelor's programs, and it ignores price, non-completers and parent loans. Whether making colleges
  pay part of the loss would help, or would shut out the students it is meant to protect, has **no
  record** for degree-granting colleges: that half is the viewer's.
- **Fell:** "Useless majors are why the debt can't be paid" (the unpayable debt sits mostly with
  non-completers, graduate programs and parent loans). "The 2027 rule will close bad bachelor's programs"
  (about 1 in 90 fail). "Colleges already carry some risk" (they don't when loans go unpaid). "Risk-sharing
  is proven" or "risk-sharing would destroy access" (neither is measured for degree-granting schools).
- **The viewer's verdict — the judge hands it over, never sets it:** **who should carry the risk when a
  degree doesn't pay?** The four seats, each with its price tag from the record: (1) **the student**,
  as now, with exits that work for federal loans and public-service jobs; (2) **the parent**, whose loan
  has the narrowest exits; (3) **the taxpayer**, about 5 cents per dollar officially, 17 at fair value;
  (4) **the college**, which the House voted for in 2025 and the law left out, with its own designers'
  warning on access.

  The research earned the price tag on each seat. It did **not** earn a right answer.

## Make it Matter — the viewer's own debt, and a teacher's

- **The story, ep21-style: follow one bill.** A family's aid offer: what the school charges after grants,
  what the student can borrow ($31,000), and the gap that becomes the parent's loan or a private loan.
  Then four years later: who holds which part, which part has an exit, and what the college that set the
  price owes if it can't be paid (nothing). Signpost: "Why is that allowed? Hold that."
- **The teacher** appears twice: at "who pays", as the case where the federal part has an exit and the
  parent's part doesn't; at the verdict, as the case gold asks green to answer: would your rule have
  closed this program? Always "they" or "a teacher". Never mocked, never blamed.
- **The history beat:** four tries in fifteen years (2011 vacated, 2014 rescinded, 2023 narrowed, 2025
  earnings-only), and the House's risk-sharing on the table and off it within 2025. One chain, drawn
  large.
- **Lead with the fields the yardstick names** (criminal justice, the performing arts, fine arts), and
  show where the fields people argue about land, mid-pack, beside business management, biology and
  political science.
- **What to check yourself** (information, never financial advice): which of your loans are Direct Loans
  and which are Parent PLUS or private; whether your job could qualify for PSLF; what plan your parent's
  loan is on.
- **The picture:** one bill split into three colours (the student's federal loan, the parent's loan, the
  private loan) with an exit drawn from only one; one "who loses when it isn't repaid" bar (borrower,
  taxpayer, school at zero).

## Script traps

- ⛔ **Ruling 7: a generic teacher is "they" or "a teacher", never "she/her"** — in the script, the
  on-screen text and every research file. Cast and visuals do not default to a woman.
- ⛔ **Never cite a real family or anyone's own case**, on air or in any published file. "A family" only,
  invented and unnamed.
- ⛔ **No field is singled out by politics.** The yardstick names the fields (criminal justice, the
  performing arts, fine arts first); other fields are reported where they land. Gender studies has no
  row of its own: never give it a number other than ED's pooled $51,710.
- ⛔ **Total cost is the measure, not federal debt** (ruling 4). Federal bachelor's debt is capped at
  $31,000; if it is mentioned at all, it is the secondary measure, beside total cost.
- **CBO and DOJ figures come from browser excerpts** (cbo.gov and justice.gov refuse automated fetches):
  re-open CBO's February 2026 baseline PDF and DOJ's November 2023 release before airing the 5¢/17¢, the
  $50 billion reestimate, the Parent PLUS profit or the 99%.
- **DOJ's 99% is of cases a court had ruled on,** not of everyone who filed; 632 cases in 10 months.
- **CBO's 21% / 56% forgiveness shares are a February 2020 projection,** before SAVE and RAP.
- **CBO's "$6 billion" for the House risk-sharing reaches us only through NPR** (APPROXIMATE); the House
  formula's critics are named experts quoted by NPR. Attribute both.
- **The House plan was risk-sharing; the enacted law is an earnings test.** Never say the 2025 law makes
  colleges repay anything.
- **Flag ≠ rule bar.** The FAFSA notice is per school, information only; the rule is per program,
  against working adults 25–34 with only a high-school diploma. Never "the same bar".
- **A tie passes** the 2027 test; appeals are for calculation errors only.
- **Pell:** a failing program loses only Direct Loans; the institution-level test can cost all federal aid
  for those programs. Never "loses Pell after three years".
- **Failure rates:** final rule only (bachelor's 1.1% / 0.6% of students; associate 6.0%; certificates
  24.1% / 27.6%; all 5.2%). Not proposal-era figures. Table figures were typed by hand from ED's images:
  re-read the images before airing. **Cosmetology's 93% is certificate programs.**
- **"No federal cap on price" is an absence** (APPROXIMATE): say "Washington doesn't set what a college
  charges", not "it's illegal to cap".
- **Accreditors and states approve schools** is ED's own description (APPROXIMATE); don't detail the
  statute.
- **Cohort-default sanctions (12 for FY2017, 10 won)** are from New America's table, APPROXIMATE; the
  official 0.4% default rate is a payment-pause artefact (FSA says so).
- **The 1990s sanctions study is NBER w22967, the 2016 version;** it is about for-profits, not
  degree-granting colleges in general.
- **Looney and Yannelis's threshold ("about half of institutions in 2009")** is their own proposal, on old
  data; Cooper's argument is AEI's catalog summary, the chapter not opened.
- **RAP is bracketed** on total income with a $120-a-year floor, not a smooth "1–10%".
- **IDR/RAP forgiveness from 2026 is taxable** on the plain reading of the statute; PSLF and TLF are not.
  Don't say "all forgiveness is tax-free".
- **PSLF and TLF figures sit in FSA spreadsheet rows:** re-read the named rows before airing.
- **Parent PLUS consolidation** before 2026-07-01 is the PSLF path, needs an IDR plan by 2028-07-01, and is
  lost on any new Direct Loan. PSLF turns on the **parent's** job.
- **"Teacher on $47k with $80k borrowed" is a hypothetical** built from the $31k cap; the $157 RAP payment
  is our arithmetic (4% bracket, flat pay), not an ED example.
- **Two net prices, never mixed.** Scorecard $15,364 (median, aided, 2023–24) for every comparison and
  ratio; College Board $21,340 (average, all first-time full-time, 2025–26 projection) for the
  housing-and-food share only. Never one chart, never one sum.
- **Cost ÷ pay is four years of net price over ONE year's pay** four years out. Not a lifetime return, not
  debt.
- **Sallie Mae is the largest private student lender:** attribute its survey every time.
- **Housing and food are two-thirds of the net bill, not "two-thirds spent on the experience":** a
  non-student pays rent and food too.
- **Defaults:** prefer NCES's 45% vs 8% (2003–04 cohort, say so).
- **Openings per graduate misleads:** never air "jobs per graduate" (criminal justice shows more openings
  than graduates yet has the worst underemployment, because police enter with a high-school diploma).
- **NY Fed vs Strada underemployment are different definitions;** never side by side as one measure.
  NY Fed by-major figures are 2024 data, released February 2026.
- **The yardstick is relative:** about half of majors fall short by construction. Say "worse than the
  typical graduate on both counts", not "worthless".
- **"27% work in a job related to their major" is NY Fed (Abel and Deitz), 2010 data,** not Census, not
  current.
- **BLS teacher and social-worker medians are all ages;** ED's $47k is four years out. Never side by side
  unlabelled. **EPI's 27% teacher pay penalty is EPI's estimate:** attribute.
- **FREOPP is a model** (attribute); its older "psychology usually negative ROI" line does not air.
- **No politics.** The 2025 law and the 2027 rule are described by what their text does; no line frames
  them for or against a party or administration.
