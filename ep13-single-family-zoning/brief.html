The question

Should a city be allowed to keep whole neighbourhoods zoned for detached single-family homes and nothing else — and if not, who gets to end it: the city, or the state over the city's head?

★ The spine: two dials, not one switch. The public fight is framed as "abolish or keep". The evidence splits on two different questions. Dose — a broad, by-right reform (three-quarters of a city upzoned, no strings) behaves nothing like a narrow reform with strings (a duplex you may build if you live there, under a 20-foot height limit, for a fee). Decider — every instrument in the news this year is a state setting a floor a city has to implement, and every lawsuit is about whether the city drew the map honestly. Ending single-family-only zoning has been done in very different doses by very different deciders, and the results track the dose and the decider, not the slogan.

Why now

**In 2026 the question stopped being whether a city should end single-family-only zoning. Three states and Congress answered it in different ways, and the live fight is what happens when the city says no.**

California — the state upzoned near transit; the cities wrote around it; the cases landed

dateevent
2026-07-01SB 79 (Chapter 512, Statutes of 2025) becomes operative for cities. Within a quarter-mile and a half-mile of qualifying transit, a city may not impose height, density or floor-area limits below the statute's floors for projects of five or more units. Operative text read directly.
2026-06-30Los Angeles enacts Ordinances 188967 (the Low-Rise Ordinance) and 188968 (Phased Implementation, an urgency ordinance) — effective the day before SB 79's operative date. The city asserts that, via its own incentive programme, every parcel already qualifies for the statute's temporary deferral, which runs until one year after the next housing element (the city estimates about 2030).
2026-05-05San Francisco passes Ordinance 082-26 (9–0), permanently excluding "industrial employment hub" parcels. HCD found it substantially compliant on 1 July 2026, on condition the city not enforce a clause barring density-bonus projects.
2026-05-21San Diego finalises Ordinance O-22099, exempting sites with no walking path under one mile to a stop "using sidewalks" — a word the statute does not use.
2026-07-31⭐ Californians for Homeownership, the California Housing Defense Fund and YIMBY Law file a petition for writ of mandate against San Francisco and HCD (CPF-26-520062). Court stamp read directly.
2026-08-20The plaintiffs announce all three actions — San Francisco, San Diego (26CU043967C) and Montebello (26STCP02862, over an alleged moratorium) — in one release. ⚠ 20 August is the announcement; the San Francisco filing was 31 July. The San Diego and Montebello pleadings were not obtained; their allegations are reported here as allegations.
2026-09-30Governor Newsom's deadline to act on SB 1116, AB 956 and SB 677, all on his desk since the session ended 31 August. Their operative content was not verified here; the episode names the deadline, not what they do.

⚠ The thing to say carefully on air: SB 79's own text (§65912.161(b)(1)) authorises the deferral Los Angeles used, for six categories of site, and §65912.160(e) authorises the permanent exclusions San Francisco and San Diego used. Whether a city's mapping into those categories is honest is what the courts are being asked. "Defiance" is the wrong word for Los Angeles; "whether the 50 %-capacity claim is true" is the right one. HCD's review of the Los Angeles ordinance was still pending at the time of research (the statutory clock runs into October or November 2026) — the episode must not say HCD approved or rejected it.

The other three answers, on three clocks

  • ✅ Texas SB 785, in force 2026-09-01. Every city with zoning must permit new HUD-code manufactured homes by right in at least one residential or dedicated district actually applied to land in the city. Enrolled text read directly. ⚠ It is a one-district floor, not manufactured homes in every single-family district. The National Zoning Atlas's May 2025 brief found 44 % of the 579 Texas municipalities it sampled would have to change their zoning to comply (33 % did not explicitly allow manufactured housing; 11 % allowed it only by special permit) — and a quarter of those could comply by mapping a district they had already written. The industry's "roughly 40 % of cities, more than 1,200" figure is a different count on a different denominator; the Atlas number is the one used here.
  • ✅ Massachusetts votes 2026-11-03 on Question 7. "An Act to Allow Single-Family Homes on Small Lots in Areas with Adequate Infrastructure": no city or town may prohibit, or require a special permit for, a single-family home on a residentially zoned lot of at least 5,000 square feet with 50 feet of frontage and public water and sewer. The question number comes from press reporting of the Secretary of the Commonwealth's 21 July 2026 ballot-order announcement; the Secretary's own page blocks automated readers. ⚠ It keeps single-family-only zoning and lowers the lot minimum — a dose question, not an abolition.
  • ✅ **Montana Supreme Court, Montanans Against Irresponsible Densification v. State, 2026 MT 53, decided 2026-03-17, unanimous. It affirmed that the 2023 duplex (SB 323) and ADU (SB 528) mandates survive an equal-protection challenge, reversed an injunction against the Land Use Planning Act, and vacated as advisory** the lower court's ruling on private covenants. Opinion read directly. ⚠ The court did not rule on the merits of the mandates, and whether they override HOA covenants is undecided. Press says 18 March; the opinion says 17 March.
  • ✅ Public Law 119-101, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, enacted 2026-07-11. Section 208 authorises — does not appropriate — $200 million a year for FY2027–31 in competitive grants to localities that show measured growth in housing supply, and states in its own text that nothing in it lets HUD preempt local zoning. Enrolled text read directly.
  • **The counter-current — New Jersey Supreme Court, Monarch Communities v. Montville, 2026-07-13**, unanimous. Before a zoning board may grant a variance for an "inherently beneficial" use, the applicant must now show it will not substantially impair the zoning plan. ⚠ It concerns use variances for senior housing, schools and the like — not single-family zoning — and the 165-unit project was neither approved nor denied.

Settled (not the debate)

  1. Where the upzoning was broad and by right, it produced housing. Auckland upzoned about three-quarters (75.2 %) of its residential land in 2016. Greenaway-McGrevy and Phillips (Journal of Urban Economics, 2023) estimate 21,808 additional dwellings permitted over five years, about 4.1 % of the region's stock, after adjusting for construction that merely moved from non-upzoned areas. Paper read in full. ⚠ Permits, not completions; the authors' own completion rates imply roughly 20,700–21,600 homes.
  2. And rents fell relative to what they would have been. Greenaway-McGrevy and So, Economic Inquiry, published 11 July 2026: eight years after the reform, Auckland rents were 23 % lower than the synthetic-control counterfactual under the preferred specification. Published abstract read; the full text is paywalled, so the per-specification range is not aired.
  3. Legalising is not building. California SB 9 (duplexes and lot splits on single-family lots, 2022): in its first year Los Angeles received 211 unit applications and approved 38; San Diego received 7 and approved none; statewide the Terner Center found activity "limited or non-existent", naming owner-occupancy rules, height limits, fees and costs. Report read directly. ⚠ First-year figures (to November 2022). No verified statewide count for 2024–25 exists; the episode says "in its first year", never "SB 9 failed".
  4. Where the rules were written to be usable, they were used. Charlotte's 2023 Unified Development Ordinance: 8,537 duplex, triplex and quadplex units submitted or approved since 1 June 2023, against "hundreds" a year before (Charlotte Ledger analysis via WFAE, 13 August 2026). ⚠ Applications, not completions; the article itself says "thousands" built is a guess.
  5. Minneapolis built a lot and its rents rose slowly — and its own Federal Reserve Bank says the zoning may not be why. Pew (January 2024): housing stock +12 %, rents +1 %, 2017–2022, against +4 % and +14 % for the rest of Minnesota; but just 1 % of the ~21,000 permitted units were in 2–4-unit buildings — the corridor apartments and the end of parking minimums did the work, not the triplex rule. The Minneapolis Fed (25 August 2025) found rents grew 25.7 % 2019–23 on Census data, 3.2 points below its 126-city comparison group, and concluded the gap "may result more from a negative housing demand shock specific to the Minneapolis market in 2020 than the expected impact of changes in the housing supply". Both read directly. ⛔ Do not attribute the Minneapolis rent result to the zoning change.
  6. Houston's small lots built homes. After the 1998 and 2013 minimum-lot reforms, Gray and Millsap count 25,269 new residential parcels under 5,000 square feet created 1999–2016 from county appraisal data; other peer-reviewed counts run to ~39,000 (2005–18). ⚠ The "nearly 80,000" figure circulating is a Mercatus tally, not a peer-reviewed count, and is not aired. Houston never had use zoning, so it is a lot-size case, not a single-family-zoning case.
  7. The state can lawfully do this to its cities. Montana, unanimous, March 2026 (above); Huntington Beach's challenge to California's housing mandates was denied review by the US Supreme Court in February 2026 (scout-level; not independently verified here and not aired as a fact).

Contested (the real fight)

GREEN — "End it everywhere, and make it usable." The rent evidence exists only where the reform was broad and by right: Auckland, not SB 9. A duplex you cannot finance, split or permit is single-family-only zoning with better branding — Minneapolis's own numbers show the triplex rule built almost nothing while the corridors built everything. Every California city that says it is complying with SB 79 is complying by shrinking the map; the petitions are what enforcement of a real reform looks like. The state is the right decider because the people a city excludes cannot vote there.

GOLD — "Keep the neighbourhood's say, and price the change honestly." The broad-reform successes are permits, not homes, and the flagship rent result has been undercut by the city's own Fed. Upzoning on paper without local buy-in produced Los Angeles: a lawful deferral, a lawsuit and no units. Charlotte, which wrote its own rules, built; Los Angeles, which had the state's rules imposed, litigated. The statute itself wrote local deferral into law; HCD approved San Francisco's map; Montana's court decided a procedure, not the policy. And infrastructure, schools and stormwater were sized to the zoning — somebody pays for the change.

⚠ Fairness note, on the record: GOLD's "somebody pays" premise has no verified municipal fiscal-impact figure in this research — the fan-out looked for one for Minneapolis, Portland and Charlotte and found none, and the only sourced cost material (Portland's fee waivers, ADU costs, Florida's impact-fee cap) shows fees suppressing infill. GOLD is argued on air from the decider question and from the thinness of GREEN's numbers, which is where its evidence actually is. It does not claim a cost figure the research cannot support.

The reframe

None of the instruments in the news abolishes single-family-only zoning. SB 79 is transit-corridor upzoning for five-plus-unit projects. SB 785 guarantees one manufactured-home district. Question 7 keeps single-family-only and lowers the lot size. Monarch is about variances. The ROAD Act disclaims preemption. The one instrument that literally ends single-family-only zoning — Montana's duplex and ADU mandates — survived only a constitutional challenge, and no permit data from any Montana city was found. So "should cities abolish it" is the wrong shape of question. The real one is how much, by right or with strings, and decided by whom — and every verified California document this year is a fight over who draws the map inside a statute that wrote local discretion into itself.

Both sides quietly agree

Legalising alone does nothing. GREEN says so to argue for more; GOLD says so to argue for less. Both also accept, on the verified record, that the state now sets a floor and the city keeps discretion in how to meet it — every plaintiff's theory attacks the honesty of a city's mapping, not the existence of its discretion. And both benches' favourite numbers are thinner than their talking points: GREEN's rent figures are one published paper and a result its own Fed disputes; GOLD's cost figure does not exist.

Verdict framing

No winner. The viewer is handed the two dials: how much density a rule actually permits by right, and whether the state or the neighbourhood decides. ⚠ Genuinely open, and the episode must not resolve: whether HCD upholds Los Angeles's deferral (decision due October–November 2026); whether the Minneapolis rent result survives the demand-shock correction; whether Massachusetts Question 7 passes on 3 November.
