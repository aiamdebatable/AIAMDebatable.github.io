Research brief — ep51: We still need deer hunters. So why are they leaving?

The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video.

Scope, said early on air: white-tailed deer in the US, with Michigan and Wisconsin as the home ground (their gun seasons open Nov 15 and Nov 21): why we still need hunters, why fewer people hunt every decade, what states have tried to bring them back, and what the other road (paid professionals, predators, broad taxes) looks like. The pitch, in one line

"We still need deer hunters. So why do so many drift away, and do we fight to win them back or plan for a future with fewer of them?" Wisconsin's herd hit a record of about 1.89 million in the same year as its biggest total harvest since 2012 (C1, C2), and nothing else in the record removes deer at that scale (C16, C34). Yet Wisconsin has about 80,000 fewer deer hunters than in 2005 (A11) and Michigan almost a third fewer license buyers than in 1995 (A10). The records show how they leave: not through a slammed door but a revolving one. About one in four license buyers doesn't buy the next year (T1-1, T1-7), and eight in ten newcomers who say they'll hunt every year for five years don't (T1-4). What states have tried mostly keeps people who were already half in (T2-4); the one fix with decent numbers, mentoring adults, is small (T2-11); and nothing in the record has turned Michigan's or Wisconsin's line around (T2-19, T2-38).

The question

We still need them: so why are hunters leaving, and what should we do about it? The debate it stages: bring hunters back, or plan for fewer of them? (Working side labels below; the Gold label is a question for the pitch.)

Why now

Peg hunt run 2026-11-01 (peg-hunt skill); every peg fact re-checked at the primary by hand pass D (D1–D25, D36–D41). Re-run 2026-11-04: Amendment 83 FAILED. About 43–44% yes against the 55% it needed, in unofficial election-night returns (Summit Daily, Nov 3, "failed to receive the votes necessary to pass on election night"; Colorado Springs Gazette, Nov 3: 43% yes, 56% no). Certified totals were not yet published. The paragraph below was written to hold either way and still does. Michigan's HB 4445 (baiting): no Senate action found after its Feb 11, 2026 referral to the Natural Resources and Agriculture committee.

Written to hold either way: On November 3, 2026, Colorado voters were asked whether to write a right to hunt and fish into their state constitution, Amendment 83, and to declare that "hunting and fishing are THE preferred means of responsibly managing fish and wildlife populations" (D3). It needed 55 percent (D3). It was the only right-to-hunt measure on any state ballot this year (D25, Ballotpedia, secondary), and the first of its kind, on our reading of Ballotpedia's table, to reach a ballot by citizen petition rather than by a legislature (D10). Whatever Colorado decided, the question it asked is this episode's question: do we still need hunters to manage wildlife, and should that answer be locked in? The state's own ballot guide prints both halves of our debate: supporters say license money funds the wildlife agency and the tradition needs lasting protection; opponents say a constitution should not promote "one method of managing wildlife above others" (D5).

  • If it failed: "Colorado said no: only the second state ever to turn one down, after Arizona in 2010 (D7), in a state whose voters brought wolves back in 2020 and kept lion hunting in 2024 (D24)."

It lands two weeks before Michigan's firearm deer season opens (Nov 15) and Wisconsin's (Nov 21), in a fall when Wisconsin's DNR is cataloguing about 2,400 suspected EHD deer deaths across eight southeastern counties, more than seven times any earlier outbreak on its own records (C36, C37, D40; our sum of the DNR's county reports, not confirmed deaths); when it confirmed chronic wasting disease in a new county, Marinette, which extends that county's existing baiting and feeding ban three more years (C28, D38); and while a Michigan bill to re-allow baiting (HB 4445) has sat in a Senate committee since February (D36). State Farm's latest figures put Michigan drivers' odds of an animal-collision claim at 1 in 68, and its US payout for the year at $1.9 billion, for all animals (B6, B8, D39).

Topic shape (the guardrail verdict)

"Do we need them?" is answered fast; "what do we do about it?" is the live, two-sided fight.

  • Answered in a minute (C rows). Every non-hunting tool has worked only on small or closed sites: Rock Creek Park, Valley Forge, Ann Arbor, Solon (C13, C21, C14, C22). Michigan's DNR says sterilization "is not meant to be a widespread population management tool" and is "not viewed as a replacement for recreation hunting" (C16). Michigan's hunters took an estimated 373,300 deer in 2024 (D33, D34) and Wisconsin registered 339,481 in 2025 (C2), at no cost to the public. Illinois, the textbook case for government culling, has "permanently suspended targeted sharpshooting" (C34), and its own report calls the change a "transition to dependence upon increased hunter harvest" (T2-35). No source in the record prices replacing a state's hunt (hand pass C, "For the script" §2). So the episode does not stage "hunters vs no hunters"; the verdict says plainly that nothing replaces hunters at state scale today.
  • Genuinely two-sided: the fix. Green has real evidence that some programmes keep hunters (T2-3, T2-11) and that licenses are still the backbone of state wildlife money (A18, A21). Gold has real evidence that the cheap fixes show no measured effect on numbers (T2-34; the $5-license evidence is a columnist's reading of DNR data, T2-16, T2-17, APPROXIMATE), that the proven ones are small and mostly keep the already-keen (T2-4, T2-11), that Wisconsin's gun-deer sales fell 10.4% while every programme ran (T2-19), and that a broad tax can carry a wildlife agency (A32). Neither side's evidence settles it.
  • The honest limit on "why": the records show HOW hunters leave far better than WHY. The reasons rest on what lapsed hunters say, the studies asking it are old or small (T1-8), and the one cause measured head-on, CWD, explains only a small slice (T1-29, T1-30). The episode says so on air (his guardrail).

The viewer's moment, and the cold open

His rulings (2026-11-01): open on the paradox (Wisconsin's record herd in the same year as its biggest harvest since 2012, C1, C2) plus the road stake (deer were 20.1% of all Michigan crashes in 2025, B2), with "and there are fewer of them" added after the rescope; then the question. The license-buying family moves after it. The "yes, still needed" answer is held until the paradox lands.

The cold open's raw material (≤ ~100 words on air, few rounded numbers; the writer owns the words):

  1. Premise first, in one breath (the Make it Matter rule, "premise before the object"): this is about the people who hunt deer, and why there are fewer of them. Then the paradox, so the herd reads as the carrier, not the subject.
  2. The paradox. Wisconsin, 2025: about 1.89 million deer after the hunt, "highest in history" (C1), in the same year hunters took the most deer since 2012 (C2). They shot more, and there are more.
  3. The road stake. Next door in Michigan, about one crash in every five involved a deer (B2: 20.1%, 58,271 crashes in 2025).
  4. "And there are fewer of them." ⚠ Not "fewer every year" (W6, CORRECTED): Michigan's license buyers rose 1.6% in 2024 and Wisconsin's participation rose slightly that year (A10, A12). True versions: Wisconsin has about 80,000 fewer deer hunters than in 2005 (A11); its gun-license sales are as low as the mid-1970s (A15); Michigan has almost a third fewer license buyers than in 1995 (A10). Keep the state on each number.
  5. The question and the journey: do we still need them (yes, and here's why), so why are they leaving, and what do we do about it. The viewer is the jury.

⚠ "Record herd" is a WISCONSIN fact. Michigan's harvest fell with its hunters (D33: the DNR's estimate was 373,300 in 2024, against a late-1990s peak whose DNR figure is still open). Michigan's officials "estimate there may be" about 2 million deer (C5); nothing in the record says Michigan's herd is at a record. Never generalise the paradox.

The household (after the cold open; invented and unnamed, built only from figures in the log). A Michigan family in the week the gun season opens:

  • The license she skips. She buys a deer license most years, not every year. That is the typical hunter: a license 2.8 years out of 5 (T1-2, all hunting licenses, 12 states). This year her shift lands on opening weekend, one of the reasons Wisconsin's Learn to Hunt coordinator lists for casual hunters sitting a season out (T1-12, his description). It costs $31 here and $24 next door in Wisconsin (A25).
  • The kid nobody invited. Her 15-year-old doesn't go; Michigan's youth hunters are down 19% since 1995 (A10). The thing new hunters most often say got them out in a given year is "an invitation from a friend" (T1-6, STATED).
  • The drive home at dusk. November is when Michigan's deer crashes peak (B2). Her odds of an animal-collision claim this year: 1 in 68 (B8, all animals).
  • Her name in a budget formula. Her license fee helps pay for the state's game and fish work, and Michigan's House Fiscal Agency ties falling license numbers to cuts in those programmes (A19). As a paid license holder she is also counted in the federal formula that splits the excise-tax pot between states (A22, W1).
  • The question lands: if she and people like her keep skipping years, who takes the deer, and who pays?

Beat by beat

  1. The paradox. As above. End: "Hold that: more deer, more shot, fewer hunters."
  2. Do we need them? (one minute, held until the paradox lands). The other tools work on a park or a suburb: Valley Forge fell from 241 deer a square mile to 44 with federal sharpshooters (C21); Rock Creek from nearly 100 to about 19 (C13); Solon paid $611 a deer all-in (C22); Ann Arbor's sterilized does cost $1,572 each (C16). Michigan's DNR says sterilization can't replace hunting (C16). Illinois suspended its sharpshooters for good and now depends on "increased hunter harvest" (C34, T2-35). Hunters take hundreds of thousands of deer a year in each home state (D33, D34, C2). Ethics, one honest line (his ruling): whether hunting for sport is right is a real argument; this episode's question is the job, and who does it. End: "So yes. Which makes the next part matter."
  3. Who's leaving, and how. The revolving door, not the slammed one. About one in four license buyers doesn't buy the next year (T1-1: 22% in a 12-state study; T1-7: 21–27% a year on the recent dashboard); in Wisconsin it was 28% of deer hunters in 2014, and only 39% bought a deer license in all seven seasons 2005–2011 (T1-10). The typical hunter buys 2.8 years in 5; only 13% bought in each of the last 10 (T1-2). Even the Midwest, the most loyal region, has fewer than half buying every year for five years (T1-3: 47%). Skipping isn't quitting: the pool of people who hunt over five years is about half again as big as any one year's buyers (T1-11). Say vs do: 80% of new hunters say they'll hunt every year for five years; 20% actually do (T1-4). Who drifts: younger hunters and women renew less; rural hunters renew at 74%, suburban 71%, urban 66% (T1-5). The generation: participation holds steady until about 65, and baby boomers hunted more than people born after 1980, who aren't replacing them (T1-15, a peer-reviewed reading of earlier work). It isn't only boomers ageing out: Wisconsin lost hunters in every age group (A12, T1-13), and hunter-education certifications fell about 11% from 2013 to 2021 (T1-17). Ringmaster: "Wait: most of them don't quit, they skip?" End: "Hold that."
  4. Why, when you ask them. Say first that this is the weaker evidence (his guardrail): it is what people tell surveys, and beat 3 just showed how far saying drifts from doing (T1-4). When asked, lapsed hunters put life first: "life and family" was one of the most cited reasons in Wisconsin's 2019 survey, with answers about not getting time off (T1-20, no share given). Then access and crowding: Wisconsin's lapsed hunters leaned on public land that focus groups called overcrowded (T1-19, APPROXIMATE, old study known from meeting notes); Wisconsin and Michigan are the third and fifth most hunter-dense states (T1-25: 11.4 and 9.5 deer hunters per square mile); 88% of the deer harvest nationally, 91% in the Midwest, happens on private land (T1-24). The agencies' own explanations are lists, not measurements: Wisconsin's DNR names urbanization, generations, fewer young hunters, life and family, other hobbies, and says its list is "not exhaustive" (T1-21); Michigan's points to ageing and "societal changes", with no Michigan lapsed-hunter survey found (T1-22). And the field admits it: the newest national study of what pulls hunters away is 14 years old (T1-8). End: "So that's what they say. Now what they don't."
  5. The myths, tested. Four reasons people give on the comment threads, against the record: - "CWD scared them off." In Wisconsin, permit sales dropped by about 10% the year after CWD was found in 2002; a peer-reviewed model puts the lasting effect at about 5% of demand, a real shock but a small slice of the long decline, and the authors say their model can't explain the decline since 2008 (T1-29, T1-30). In Michigan in 2019, hunter numbers fell least in the CWD core area (T1-31). - "People turned against hunting." Most Americans approve of legal hunting, and approval rose from the mid-1990s through 2021 while hunter numbers fell; it dipped in 2023 (T1-35, APPROXIMATE: a chart read in order, commissioned by a pro-hunting foundation; no figures on air). - "Licenses got too expensive." Wisconsin's resident fees have not changed since 2005 while prices rose 64%, so the license is cheaper in real terms than it was, and hunters still left (A25, A11; the conclusion is T1-28, APPROXIMATE). A year of hunting costs far more than the license: the average hunter spent $857 on trips and $550 on gear in 2022 (T1-27, all hunting, national). - "The baiting ban drove Michigan's hunters away." The DNR says 2019 license sales fell about 3%, in line with past years, despite predictions (T1-33, D35, both APPROXIMATE: one DNR sentence, the survey series unreachable). Michigan's big fall came long before the 2019 ban (D33). Say "the DNR found no faster drop", never "the data prove". - (If time) "Crossbows pushed out the gun hunters." Wisconsin's DNR evaluation found archery-season buck kills were a contributing factor for only about 4–5% of lapsed gun hunters, more lapsed hunters felt positive about crossbows than negative, and the DNR concludes crossbows are not a major contributor (T2-31). - Optional pointer (council card's top practical question): "Is the venison safe with CWD around?" is ep50's subject; one line, no new claims.
  6. What's been tried. Lead with what worked, then what didn't, then what nobody measured. - Mentoring adults has the best numbers. Wisconsin's Hunt for Food takes adults who have mostly never hunted: 59% bought a license that year, 48% a year later, 41% two years later (T2-11, the DNR's own records, no comparison group). About four in ten still buying two years on. It is small: DNR staff say they are near capacity (T2-11). - Events help, mostly the already-keen. The strongest study matched 60,000 event-goers in eight states (not Michigan or Wisconsin, hunting and fishing mixed) with lookalikes: attendees skipped years 11–36% less in seven of the eight states (T2-1–T2-3). But 27–93% had already bought a license before the event: "compensatory recruitment, rather than additive" (T2-4). Adult events beat youth events, and the authors tell agencies to put adults first (T2-5). Wisconsin's oldest programme, Learn to Hunt, was mostly reaching kids already from hunting families (T2-8), median age about 13 (T2-10). - Price: little or no measured effect. Wisconsin's $5 first-time license (T2-15) did not move deer-license sales off a forecast made before the cut, on a columnist's reading of the researchers' numbers (T2-16, T2-17, APPROXIMATE); it forgoes about $4 million a year (T2-14); first-time buyers still fell about 4.5% from FY16 to FY21 (T2-18). Michigan's 2014 overhaul RAISED prices and lost slightly fewer buyers than the DNR expected (T2-23, APPROXIMATE). Florida's auto-renew had no statistically significant effect on hunting licenses (T2-34). - Rules: helps does, not headcount. Pennsylvania's first year of Sunday hunting still lost adult resident licenses (T2-32, APPROXIMATE: one season, newspaper relay, figure off air); the "117,500 more hunters" was a 2011 advocacy forecast never checked (T2-33). Crossbows kept some 40-to-65-year-olds who couldn't draw a bow, but didn't recruit (T2-29, T2-30). In 2024 Michigan opened antlerless seasons to public land and added a $5 antlerless license; antlerless harvest rose 18% while buyers rose only 1.6% (T2-24: one year, the DNR doesn't credit the change; earlier loosening got "no big response", A46). More antlerless deer from about the same hunters. - Access: never measured for hunters. Michigan's Hunting Access Program dates from 1977 (T2-25); the national federal-grant evaluation measured spending, not hunters, and was cut short when Congress ended its funding (T2-26); Wisconsin's programme isn't enrolling new land (T2-27). Access might matter (beat 4); no study here shows it brings hunters back. - The pandemic beat every programme. Wisconsin's new gun-deer buyers jumped over 36% in 2020, and total gun-deer sales still fell 1.3% that year; over 2016–2021, with everything above running, gun-deer sales fell 10.4%, about 52,000 fewer residents (T2-19). Nationally, churn didn't notably drop in the pandemic (T1-7) and new recruits fell 9% in 2021 (T1-9). No Michigan or Wisconsin programme has a measured retention effect with a comparison group (T2-38). End: "Hold that."
  7. The other road (Gold's ground). Who else can do the job, and who pays: - Paid professionals work where hunters can't go: Illinois's own report lists shooting only where the disease is, working on land that bars hunting, and removing sick deer at a higher rate (T2-36). Their records are small sites at a price (C13, C21, C22, C16), and the sterilization results come almost entirely from one contractor (C26). - Illinois is the warning both ways: it suspended sharpshooting for good (C34) and moved to "dependence upon increased hunter harvest" (T2-35), yet its hunters in the 28 CWD counties took 30,926 deer in 2025–26, about the 31,037 they took in 2001–02 (T2-35). Leaning on hunters means leaning on a harvest that hasn't grown in 24 years; its adult CWD prevalence hit a record 10.4% in the endemic area (C34). - Wolves, ONE beat (his ruling): in Wisconsin, wolves' arrival cut deer crashes about 24% in the average county, three-quarters of it by changing deer behaviour (B17, B18); the authors' own interpretation is that hunting is "compressed into a few weeks each year" (B20). One state, a county panel ending 2010 (B17); livestock and pet losses are partly compensated (B19). No candidates, no politics (the peg hunt rejected the wolf thread for side-taking risk). - Who pays: Missouri's one-eighth-cent conservation sales tax supplies 61.8% of its conservation department's revenue, with no general revenue (A32); Arkansas passed its version with 50.5% (A33); Minnesota's runs to 2034 (A34). About three-quarters of the gun-and-ammunition excise already comes from non-hunting purchases, an industry estimate (A28, A29). Wisconsin already backfills its account from the forestry account, itself filled from the general fund (A27). The federal version passed the House 231–190 in 2022 and has never passed the Senate (A35, A36; T2-37).
  8. The dial. See Verdict framing.

Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

We still need hunters

  • Nothing in the record removes deer at state scale except hunters: the alternatives' records are parks and suburbs (C13, C14, C21, C22); Michigan's DNR says sterilization is no replacement (C16); Illinois suspended sharpshooting and depends on hunter harvest (C34, T2-35); no source prices a statewide replacement (hand pass C).
  • Hunters remove deer at volume: Michigan's estimate was 373,300 in 2024 (D33, D34); Wisconsin registered 339,481 in 2025 (C2).

The paradox (Wisconsin)

  • Post-hunt herd about 1.89 million, "highest in history" (C1); highest buck harvest since 2007 and highest total since 2012 (C2); the nine-day gun season itself was down 0.8% (C3).
  • Virginia is the cleanest side-by-side: deer license holders fell from over 343,000 (1986) to 177,541 (2025/26) while the herd rose from about 1.1 to 1.3 million (2014–2024) (C7).

Fewer hunters, and how they leave

  • National: 14.1 million hunters (1991) to 11.5 million (2016) on one comparable method (A2); deer hunters were 8.1 million of them, 71% (A3). The 2022 survey must not be compared with any earlier year (A1, A6).
  • Michigan: 604,088 deer-license buyers in 2024 (+1.6% on 2023), down 31% since 1995; youth down 19% (A10).
  • Wisconsin: about 80,000 fewer individual deer hunters, 2005–2022 (−13%), women up 29% (A11); losses in every age group (A12); male hunters projected to fall another 27–40% by 2040 in all four projections, women up 5–44%, not enough (A13); gun-license sales back to mid-1970s levels (A15).
  • Churn (MEASURED): 22% of resident hunters don't renew the next year (T1-1, 12 states, 2015); 21–27% a year over the recent five years, not notably lower in the pandemic (T1-7); 2.8 years in 5, 13% in all 10 (T1-2); Midwest 47% buy every year for five years, the highest region (T1-3); Wisconsin deer churn 28% in 2014, 39% bought in all seven seasons 2005–2011 (T1-10, the DNR's data relayed by a columnist); the five-year pool is about 50% larger than any one year's buyers (T1-11).
  • Say vs do: 80% of new hunters say they'll hunt each of the next 5 years; 20% do (T1-4).
  • Who lapses: younger hunters and women renew less; rural 74%, suburban 71%, urban 66% (T1-5).
  • Cohort, not age: participation is "largely invariant to age until the hunter becomes ~65"; boomers hunted more than those born after 1980 (T1-15).
  • The pipeline: Wisconsin's hunter-education certifications 31,103 (2013) to 27,598 (2021); gun-deer sales −10.4% from 2016 to 2021 (T1-17).

The myths, as far as the record goes

  • CWD: Wisconsin's permits dropped 102,710 (over 10%) after 2002; modelled lasting effect 5.4% of demand; the model "does not provide insight" into the decline since 2008 (T1-29, T1-30). Michigan's CWD core lost the fewest hunters in 2019 (T1-31).
  • Price: Wisconsin's fees frozen since 2005 while prices rose 64% (A25).
  • Crossbows: not a major contributor to Wisconsin's gun-hunter decline (T2-31).

What's been tried, and what it showed

  • Hunt for Food: 59.3% / 48.3% / 41.2% buying a license that year / one year / two years on, no comparison group, near capacity (T2-11); its participants are about 46% women, median age mid-30s (T2-12).
  • The eight-state matched study: attendees churn 11% (North Dakota) to 36% (Connecticut) less, in seven of eight states; 27–93% already bought before attending; adults beat youth (T2-1–T2-5, T2-39).
  • Learn to Hunt mostly reached kids from hunting families (T2-8); too few records to follow graduates (T2-9, T2-10).
  • Florida's auto-renew: no statistically significant effect on hunting licenses (T2-34).
  • Crossbows retained some hunters aged 40–65; 0.7% a year of gun-and-bow hunters went crossbow-only (T2-29, T2-30).
  • Wisconsin new gun-deer buyers +36% in 2020 while total gun-deer sales fell 1.3% (T2-19); national new recruits −9% in 2021 (T1-9).
  • Access programmes evaluated for spending only (T2-26); none found with a hunter outcome (T2-25, T2-27).
  • Mentored licenses are growing: Wisconsin 18,286 in 2021 (T2-13), Michigan mentored-youth deer licenses 12,437 in 2024 (T2-22). Uptake, not retention.

Who pays for wildlife

  • License money is the biggest single block: 43% of state agencies' dedicated revenue nationally (FY2019), Pittman-Robertson another 18% (A21); 92.7% of Wisconsin's Fish and Wildlife Account (A18); Michigan's licenses about $68.4 million, with its House Fiscal Agency tying falling license numbers to programme cuts (A19).
  • The federal formula: half of the main pot by each state's share of all states' paid license holders (A22, W1).
  • About three-quarters of the excise from non-hunting purchases, an industry estimate (A28, A29).
  • Wisconsin's account runs about $14.9 million a year short (A23), mainly frozen fees (A25), backfilled from the forestry account (A27).
  • Missouri 61.8% from its sales tax (A32); Arkansas 1996, 50.5% (A33); Minnesota to 2034 (A34); the federal bill passed the House 231–190 in 2022 and never the Senate (A35, A36).

The road stake

  • Michigan 2025: 58,271 deer crashes, 20.1% of all crashes; 13 killed, 8 of them motorcyclists; crashes peak in November, deaths in June (B2). Michigan drivers' odds of an animal claim: 1 in 68 (B8).

Contested (the real fight)

Working side labels (data-driven, not pro/con):

  • Team Green — "Bring hunters back." We need hunters and nothing replaces them at scale, so the job is to keep the ones who drift: mentor adults, invite friends, open land, give the hunters we have room to take does. The money follows them.
  • Team Gold — "Plan for fewer hunters" (sharpened from his proposed "Stop depending on them"; a pitch question). Every projection says fewer (A13), and nothing tried has bent the line (T2-19, T2-38). Keep hunters, but build the backup now: paid professionals where hunters can't go, broad funding so the wildlife budget doesn't rise and fall with license sales.
  • Why sharpen Gold: "Stop depending on them" asks Gold to argue against a settled fact (nothing replaces hunters at state scale, C16, C34, T2-35), so the verdict would knock it down and Gold would read as the straw side. "Plan for fewer" is Gold at full strength, and it is the Illinois lesson (T2-35).
  • ⚠ Neither side is anti-hunting. Hunters are on both sides of the Colorado vote (D17); Gold's best evidence includes the hunters' own association (A48) and the state agencies' own reports (T2-19, T2-35).

1. Can the decline be turned around?

  • Green: yes, at the edges, and the edges add up. Mentoring adults keeps about four in ten brand-new adults buying two years on (T2-11); events cut lapsing 11–36% in seven of eight states (T2-3); the authors say aim at adults (T2-5); mentored licenses are climbing (T2-13, T2-22). New hunters say a friend's invitation is what gets them out (T1-6), so the cheapest recruiter is a hunter. Public approval isn't the barrier (T1-35, APPROXIMATE).
  • Gold: not at the scale that matters. The proven programmes are small (T2-11, near capacity) and mostly keep people already in (T2-4, T2-8); the cheap fixes show little or no measured effect (T2-16, T2-17, T2-34; Sunday hunting T2-32); Wisconsin's gun-deer sales fell 10.4% while everything ran (T2-19); the generation that hunted most is ageing out and isn't being replaced (T1-15); the DNR projects further male declines in every scenario (A13).

2. Who takes the deer?

  • Green: hunters take hundreds of thousands a year free to the public (D33, D34, C2); professionals work only on small ground and at a price (C13, C21, C22, C16); even Illinois turned back to hunters (C34, T2-35). And given room, the hunters we have take more antlerless deer: Michigan's antlerless harvest rose 18% in 2024 on roughly the same hunters (T2-24, one year).
  • Gold: Wisconsin's herd hit a record despite the big harvest (C1, C2); hunters take too many bucks (C5: 1.3 bucks per doe in Michigan; A48: antlerless harvest "is not keeping pace", the National Deer Association's words) and earlier loosening got "no big response" (A46). Illinois's hunter harvest in its CWD counties hasn't grown in 24 years (T2-35), and its own report lists what paid shooters can do that hunters can't (T2-36). Wolves cut Wisconsin crashes all year round (B17, B20).

3. Who pays?

  • Green: licenses are the largest block of state wildlife money (A21), 92.7% of Wisconsin's account (A18), and Michigan's legislature ties falling license numbers to cuts (A19). Half of the main federal pot is split by each state's share of license holders (A22, W1). Keep the hunters and the money follows.
  • Gold: most of the excise already comes from people who don't hunt (A28, A29, industry estimate); Wisconsin's hole is mainly fees frozen since 2005 (A25) and is already patched from general-fund money one step removed (A27); Missouri shows a broad tax carrying most of the load (A32). If everyone gets the benefit of fewer deer on the road, everyone can pay.

4. What do the hunters who leave actually need? (Where the two sides partly meet.)

  • Green: lapsed Wisconsin hunters leaned on crowded public land (T1-19, APPROXIMATE); most deer are shot on private land (T1-24); open more of it (T2-25, T2-27).
  • Gold: access programmes have never been measured for hunters (T2-26); one of the most-cited reasons is life and family (T1-20, APPROXIMATE), which no programme fixes; Wisconsin's VPA isn't even enrolling (T2-27).

Where the record lands, said plainly (no false balance)

  • We still need hunters. Settled; the verdict says so.
  • Nothing tried has reversed the decline in Michigan or Wisconsin (T2-19, T2-38). Gold wins that.
  • Mentoring adults is the one fix with decent numbers (T2-11, T2-3, T2-5), and the authors of the best study say put adults first. Green wins that, at small scale.
  • The leak is churn more than quitting, and a generation not replaced (T1-1, T1-2, T1-15). The reasons behind it are survey-thin (T1-8, T1-4); the popular explanations (CWD, anti-hunting, price, baiting) each explain little or nothing on the record (T1-29–T1-31, T1-35, A25, T1-33).
  • The money question is a choice, not a fact. The record shows who pays now; it can't say who should.

The reframe (tested against the record)

As written*The fight sounds like "more hunters or fewer." The record says hunters don't leave

through a slammed door; they leave through a revolving one. About one in four buyers skips the next year, newcomers rarely stick, and most of what states try keeps the people already half inside. So the question isn't only "how do we bring them back?" It's "how much of the deer job, and the bill, do we keep resting on a revolving door?"*

Test 1, "a revolving door, not a slammed one": holds, with limits.

  • For it: 22% don't renew the next year (T1-1), 21–27% a year recently (T1-7), 28% of Wisconsin's deer hunters in 2014 (T1-10); the typical hunter buys 2.8 years in 5 (T1-2); the five-year pool is about half again as big as any year's buyers (T1-11).
  • Limit 1: the 12-state churn figures are ALL hunting licenses, from 2015 (T1-1, T1-2). The deer-specific number is Wisconsin's (T1-10).
  • Limit 2: the door also loses people for good: the cohort that hunted most is ageing out without being replaced (T1-15), and Wisconsin's projections fall in every scenario (A13). Say "mostly skip", never "nobody quits".

Test 2, "most of what states try keeps the people already half inside": holds.

  • For it: 27–93% of event-goers had already bought a license (T2-4); Learn to Hunt mostly reached kids from hunting families (T2-8).
  • Limit: Hunt for Food's participants had mostly never hunted (T2-11), the one clear exception, and it is small. Say "most", never "all".

Both sides quietly agree

  • Nothing replaces hunters across a state today (C16, C34, T2-35). Gold's case is "plan for fewer", never "do without".
  • Nobody really knows why they leave. The field's own grant says so (T1-8); the reasons are self-reports (T1-20, T1-23).
  • If you're going to spend on recruiting, spend it on adults. The best study says it (T2-5) and Wisconsin's best programme is adult (T2-11).
  • The license base is shrinking, and someone else is already paying more (A13, A23, A27).
  • A constitution won't settle it. Colorado's own analysis says no immediate change (D1), and every court ruling found upheld the regulation it was asked to strike (D14).

Verdict framing

No winner. The judge says what the record settles (we still need hunters; they mostly leave by skipping years, and a generation isn't being replaced; mentoring adults is the one fix with decent numbers; nothing tried has turned the line around; the money is a choice) and hands the viewer one dial: "How hard should your state bet on hunters?" It runs from Green to Gold. Every setting comes with a bill and a documented failure; the judge never sets the dial.

  1. "Win them back." (Green, full.) Mentor adults, cheap first licenses, friends recruiting friends. Behind it: Wisconsin DNR's Hunt for Food and its $5 first-time license with a recruiter reward (T2-11, T2-15); the eight-state R3 study (T2-1–T2-3); Colorado's "THE preferred means" (D3). Who pays: license buyers, plus forgone revenue (about $4 million a year on Wisconsin's new-buyer licenses, T2-14). Documented failure: every Wisconsin programme was running while gun-deer sales fell 10.4% (T2-19); Hunt for Food is near capacity (T2-11); the $5 license didn't bend the forecast (T2-16, T2-17, APPROXIMATE).
  2. "Aim the hunters we have." Fewer hunters, but give them room to take does where the deer are. Behind it: Michigan's 2024 public-land antlerless seasons and $5 antlerless license (T2-24); Indiana's deer-reduction zones beside the worst roads (B12, A49). Who pays: hunters, plus small state programmes. Documented failure: Michigan's earlier loosening got "no big response" (A46); Indiana prevented about 70 crashes in five years (A49): real, but small; Michigan's commission cut antlerless opportunity in 2026 against its biologists, per Michigan Farm Bureau, which opposed the vote (C6).
  3. "Hire help where hunters can't go." Keep hunting, and pay professionals for parks, suburbs and disease hot spots. Behind it: the National Park Service at Rock Creek and Valley Forge (C13, C21), USDA sharpshooters in Solon (C22), Ann Arbor (C14), Illinois's own case for sharpshooters (T2-36). Who pays: the town or the taxpayer, $611 a deer in Solon (C22), $1,572 per sterilized doe in Ann Arbor (C16). Documented failure: Michigan's DNR says it can't scale (C16); Illinois suspended it permanently (C34).
  4. "Make everyone pay." (Gold, full.) Stop tying the wildlife budget to license sales. Behind it: Missouri's conservation sales tax, 61.8% of its department's revenue (A32); Arkansas and Minnesota (A33, A34). Who pays: every shopper. Documented failure: the federal version passed the House and has never passed the Senate (A35, A36); Wisconsin's indirect version is a yearly patch on a $14.9 million shortfall (A23, A27).

The fifth setting, "prevent the conflict" (crossings, fences, living with more deer): it gave way to this dial under his rescope ruling, and the writer found no natural place for it: the record has no research on fences or crossings and no institution with a documented failure to put behind it (council card). The nearest fact, that traffic, road type and housing predicted where Virginia's Clarke County crashes happened better than deer density did (B14), belongs to the road, not to this question. Recommendation: drop it (a pitch question).

⚠ Wolves stay out of the dial: one beat inside "the other road" (his ruling), structural, no candidates.

  • Eight in ten new hunters say they'll go every year for five years; two in ten do (T1-4).
  • Of every ten brand-new adults in Wisconsin's Hunt for Food, about four still buy a license two years later (T2-11).
  • A Wisconsin resident deer license costs about as much as 3.5 pounds of ground beef ($24 against $6.79 a pound; our arithmetic on A25 and A37), against $857 the average hunter spent on trips in 2022 (T1-27, all hunting, national).
  • Illinois's hunters in its CWD counties took about as many deer last season as in 2001–02 (T2-35: 30,926 against 31,037).
  • ✂ Cut (his ruling 2026-11-01): the replacement-cost illustration (our arithmetic on a cull price and a state harvest). Off air and off screen. Don't multiply any cull price by any harvest anywhere in the script.
