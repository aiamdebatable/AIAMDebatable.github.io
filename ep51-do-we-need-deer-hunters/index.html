Deer Hunting: Where Did All the Hunters Go?

Should we fight to bring deer hunters back, or plan for fewer of them?

We still need deer hunters. So why do so many drift away, and do we win them back or plan for fewer?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

On November 3, Colorado voters turned down Amendment 83, which would have written into the state constitution that hunting and fishing are the preferred means of managing wildlife; it fell short of the 55% it needed. Meanwhile Michigan has almost a third fewer people buying a deer license than in 1995, and about one crash in five there last year involved a deer.

Settled

What both sides accept

Both teams accepted that we still need hunters: nothing else removes deer across a whole state today, and Gold's case isn't to do without them, only not to bet everything on them. Most hunters leave by skipping years, and a generation isn't being replaced. Mentoring adults is the one fix with decent numbers, and nothing tried has turned Michigan's or Wisconsin's line around.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesBring hunters back

Hunters are the only tool already doing the job across a whole state, and their licenses are the biggest single slice of state wildlife agencies' dedicated money, 43%. Most hunters skip years rather than quit, so you can call them back, and mentoring adults keeps about four in ten still buying two years on. Given room, Michigan's hunters took 18% more antlerless deer in one season, with about the same number of hunters.

▶ Hear Green at 2:34 on YouTube
Gold arguesPlan for fewer hunters

With every Wisconsin program running, its gun-deer license sales still fell about 10% from 2016 to 2021, and its own forecasts say male deer hunters fall another 27 to 40% by 2040. Wisconsin's record herd came in the same year as its biggest harvest since 2012, and Illinois's hunters in its disease counties take about what they took 24 years ago. So hire help where hunters can't go, and fund wildlife the way Missouri does, so the budget doesn't rise and fall with license sales.

▶ Hear Gold at 11:10 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

It sounds like a fight about whether hunting is dying. But most hunters don't leave through a slammed door; they leave through a revolving one. About one in four buyers skips the next year, most newcomers don't stick for five, and most of what states try keeps the people already halfway in. So the question isn't only how to bring them back. It's how much of the deer job, and the bill, we keep resting on a revolving door.

What didn't hold up
that disease drove them off, that people turned against hunting, that licenses got too expensive, or that Michigan's baiting ban emptied the woods. Each explains little or nothing on the record. And who should pay is a choice the record can't make for you.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: How hard should your state bet on hunters?

The judge left one dial: how hard should your state bet on hunters. These are the four settings the judge named along it, in order, and nobody was declared the winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Win them back
    What it buys
    Mentor adults, cheap first licenses, friends recruiting friends, like Wisconsin's Hunt for Food. Most hunters leave by skipping years, not quitting: about one in four buyers doesn't buy the next year, but the pool who hunt at least once in five years is about half again as big, so people can come back. Hunt for Food takes adults who have mostly never hunted: about six in ten bought a license that year, and about four in ten were still buying two years later. In the strongest study, from eight other states, event-goers skipped years 11% to 36% less than lookalikes, in seven of the eight.
    What it costs
    Every Wisconsin program was running while its gun-deer sales fell about 10%, from 2016 to 2021: about 52,000 fewer residents. Hunt for Food is small, staff say it's near capacity, and its numbers have no comparison group. The events mostly keep people already in: between 27% and 93% of attendees, depending on the state, had already bought a license. The cheap fixes barely registered: after Wisconsin's $5 first licenses, sales landed where a forecast made before the cut said they would, on a columnist's reading, and Florida's auto-renew had no significant effect on hunting licenses.
    Not measured
    Whether any Michigan or Wisconsin program keeps hunters better than doing nothing: none has been measured against a comparison group.

    On the record: row T2-11 of the fact-check log, row T1-11 of the fact-check log, row T2-3 of the fact-check log, row T2-19 of the fact-check log, row T2-4 of the fact-check log

  2. 2Aim the hunters we have
    What it buys
    Give them room to take female deer where the herd is, like Michigan's public-land antlerless seasons. In 2024 Michigan opened its antlerless seasons to public land and added a $5 antlerless license. That year the antlerless harvest rose 18%, while the number of license buyers rose only 1.6%: more does, taken by about the same hunters. Michigan's hunters take 1.3 bucks for every doe, the highest ratio in the Great Lakes states.
    What it costs
    Michigan's earlier loosening got no big response, and this year its commission voted to cut antlerless chances from 2027, over its biologists, says Michigan Farm Bureau, which opposed the vote. The 2024 rise is one season, and the DNR doesn't credit the change. And taking more isn't the same as enough: Wisconsin's hunters took more deer in 2025 than in any year since 2012, and the state still estimated its biggest herd on record, about 1.89 million.
    Not measured
    Whether opening public land caused the 2024 rise: it's one season, and the state doesn't say so.

    On the record: row T2-24 of the fact-check log, row A46 of the fact-check log, row C6 of the fact-check log, row C1 of the fact-check log

  3. 3Hire help where hunters can't go
    What it buys
    Sharpshooters for parks, suburbs and disease hot spots, paid by the town or the taxpayer. At Valley Forge, federal sharpshooters took the herd from 241 deer a square mile to 44. Illinois's own report lists what paid professionals can do that hunters can't: shoot only where the disease is, work on land that doesn't allow hunting, and remove sick deer at a higher rate. Leaning on hunters alone has its own limit: in Illinois's disease counties, hunters took about as many deer last season as in 2001, a harvest that hasn't grown in 24 years.
    What it costs
    About $610 a deer in Solon, Ohio, and the record is parks and suburbs. Sterilizing female deer in Ann Arbor ran about $1,572 each, and the sterilization results come almost entirely from one contractor. Michigan's DNR says sterilization is no replacement for hunting, and Illinois suspended its sharpshooting for good. Nothing else on the record removes deer across a whole state.
    Not measured
    What it would cost to replace a state's hunt with paid help: no source prices it.

    On the record: row C21 of the fact-check log, row C22 of the fact-check log, row C16 of the fact-check log, row C34 of the fact-check log, row T2-35 of the fact-check log

  4. 4Make everyone pay
    What it buys
    A broad conservation tax like Missouri's: a one-eighth-cent sales tax, about 62% of its conservation department's money, paid by every shopper, with no state general revenue. The budget then doesn't rise and fall with license sales. People who don't hunt already pay something: by an industry estimate, cited in a report written for the Fish and Wildlife Service, about three-quarters of the federal gun-and-ammunition excise comes from non-hunting purchases.
    What it costs
    The federal version has never passed the Senate: it passed the House in 2022. Arkansas passed its own sales tax with just over half the vote, 50.5%. And until a broad tax passes in more places, licenses are still the biggest single slice of state wildlife agencies' dedicated money nationally, 43%.

    On the record: row A32 of the fact-check log, row A28 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row A35 of the fact-check log, row A33 of the fact-check log, row A21 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pick: We still need hunters: nothing else removes deer across a whole state. Where your state sets that dial decides who takes the deer, and who pays, and who should pay is a choice the record can't make for you.

What our fact-check caught

16 of the 243 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidChronic wasting disease scared the hunters off.

    The record saysIt was a real shock, but a small slice. Wisconsin's permit sales dropped more than 10% after the disease was found in 2002, but a peer-reviewed model puts the lasting effect at about 5% of demand, and it can't explain the decline since 2008. In Michigan in 2019, hunter numbers fell least where the disease is concentrated.

  2. Draft saidPeople turned against hunting.

    The record saysMost Americans approve of legal hunting, and approval rose from the mid-1990s through 2021, while hunter numbers fell. It dipped in 2023. That's one survey series, paid for by a pro-hunting foundation.

  3. Draft saidHunting licenses got too expensive.

    The record saysWisconsin's resident fees haven't changed since 2005, while prices rose 64%. The license got cheaper in real terms, and hunters left anyway. A Wisconsin deer license is $24.

  4. Draft saidMichigan's baiting ban cut the deer harvest from about 600,000 to under 300,000.

    The record saysThe state's own estimate since the ban is about 350,000 to 375,000 deer a year. The 600,000 was the 1998 record, two decades before the 2019 ban, and license sales in the ban's first season fell about 3%, in line with past years.

  5. Draft saidThere are fewer deer hunters every year.

    The record saysThe fall is decades long, not every year. Michigan has almost a third fewer deer-license buyers than in 1995, but the number rose 1.6% in 2024, and Wisconsin's deer hunters rose slightly that year too.
