## The research · Who's leaving, how, and what they say
**Andre:** Picture a Michigan hunter who buys a deer license most years, not every year. She's typical. In a twelve-state study of license records, the typical hunter bought a hunting license two point eight years out of five. Only about one in eight bought one every year for a decade.
**Nia:** So people are quitting?
**Andre:** Mostly skipping. About one in four license buyers doesn't buy the next year. But the pool of people who hunt at least once in five years is about half again as big as any one year's buyers. Many come back.
**Nia:** And in Wisconsin, where it's deer?
**Andre:** Steeper. In twenty fourteen, twenty-eight percent of Wisconsin's deer hunters didn't buy again. Only thirty-nine percent bought a deer license in all seven seasons from two thousand five to twenty eleven. Those are the DNR's figures, relayed by a columnist.
**Nia:** Why would someone skip a year?
**Andre:** Wisconsin's Learn to Hunt coordinator has a list. The kid has a choir recital, a spouse has to work, a buddy is feeling sick, or the forecast calls for high winds and cold rain, and suddenly they're not so eager. That's his description, not a survey.
**Nia:** And the new ones? The people just starting?
**Andre:** Here's the gap. Eighty percent of new hunters say they'll hunt every year for the next five. Twenty percent actually do. It's the New Year's resolution of the woods.
**Nia:** Who drifts the most?
**Andre:** Younger hunters and women renew less, and so do city people: rural hunters renew most, urban hunters least. And one peer-reviewed reading finds hunting holds steady with age until about sixty-five. The baby boomers hunted more than people born after nineteen eighty, and they aren't being replaced.
**Nia:** So it's just boomers getting older?
**Andre:** Not only. Wisconsin's DNR found hunter numbers falling in every age group, and fewer people passed its hunter-education course in twenty twenty-one than in twenty thirteen. Fewer are coming in the front door, too.
**Nia:** OK, but why? What do they say when you ask?
**Andre:** First, a warning. What people tell a survey is weaker evidence than what license records show, and you just heard how far saying drifts from doing. With that said: in Wisconsin's twenty nineteen survey of deer hunters who sat a season out, one of the most cited reasons was life and family.
**Nia:** Life and family how?
**Andre:** Not getting time off, being too busy with other things. Then places to hunt. In an older state study, lapsed Wisconsin hunters leaned on public land that focus groups called overcrowded. Wisconsin and Michigan are the third and fifth most crowded states for deer hunters per square mile.
**Nia:** And the private land?
**Andre:** That's where the deer are taken: nearly nine in ten nationally. As for the agencies, they have lists, not measurements. Wisconsin's DNR names urbanization, generations, life and family, and other hobbies, and says its list isn't exhaustive. Michigan's points to ageing and societal changes; we found no Michigan survey of lapsed hunters.
**Nia:** So nobody really knows?
**Andre:** Not well. A twenty twenty-five conservation grant says the newest national study of what pulls hunters away is fourteen years old. The records show how they leave far better than why.

## The research · The myths, tested, and what's been tried
**Andre:** Four reasons people give, against the record. First: disease scared them off. In Wisconsin, permit sales dropped more than ten percent after chronic wasting disease was found in two thousand two. A peer-reviewed model puts the lasting effect at about five percent of demand.
**Nia:** So the disease did it.
**Andre:** A real shock, but a small slice. The authors say their model can't explain the decline since two thousand eight. And in Michigan in twenty nineteen, hunter numbers fell least in the disease's core area.
**Nia:** Then people turned against hunting?
**Andre:** Most Americans approve of legal hunting, and approval rose from the mid-nineties through twenty twenty-one, while hunter numbers fell. It dipped in twenty twenty-three. That's one survey series, commissioned by a pro-hunting foundation.
**Nia:** Licenses got too expensive?
**Andre:** Wisconsin's resident fees haven't changed since two thousand five, while prices rose sixty-four percent. So the license got cheaper in real terms, and the hunters left anyway. A Wisconsin deer license is twenty-four dollars, Michigan's thirty-one. The average hunter nationally spent about eight hundred and sixty dollars on trips in twenty twenty-two.
**Nia:** And Michigan's baiting ban? I hear that one a lot.
**Andre:** Michigan's DNR says license sales in twenty nineteen, the first season under the ban, fell about three percent, in line with past years, despite predictions. Its deer specialist compared limiting bait to not hosting a dinner party when you're sick. And Michigan's big fall came long before it.
**Nia:** So none of the usual suspects did it. What have states tried?
**Andre:** The best numbers are for mentoring adults. Wisconsin's Hunt for Food takes adults who have mostly never hunted. About six in ten bought a license that year, and about four in ten were still buying two years later. That's the DNR's own count, with no comparison group, and staff say it's near capacity.
**Nia:** What about the big learn-to-hunt events?
**Andre:** The strongest study matched sixty thousand event-goers in eight other states, hunting and fishing mixed, with lookalikes. Attendees skipped years eleven to thirty-six percent less, in seven of the eight. But depending on the state, between a quarter and nearly all of them had already bought a license before the event.
**Nia:** So the events mostly keep the people already in.
**Andre:** Mostly, and the authors tell agencies to put adults ahead of youth. Wisconsin's oldest program, Learn to Hunt, was mostly reaching kids from families that already hunted.
**Nia:** Cheaper licenses?
**Andre:** Wisconsin cut first-time licenses to five dollars. On a columnist's reading of the researchers' numbers, sales landed where a forecast made before the cut said they would, and a DNR survey reportedly found most first-timers hadn't noticed the discount. Florida's auto-renew had no statistically significant effect on hunting licenses.
**Nia:** Easier rules? More seasons?
**Andre:** They moved the harvest more than the headcount. In twenty twenty-four Michigan opened antlerless seasons on public land and added a five-dollar antlerless license. That year the antlerless harvest rose eighteen percent, while the number of license buyers barely moved. One season, and the DNR doesn't credit the change. Earlier loosening got no big response.
**Nia:** And opening up more land to hunt?
**Andre:** Never measured for hunters. Michigan's access program dates from nineteen seventy-seven. The national evaluation of the federal access grants counted spending, not hunters, and was cut short when Congress ended its funding. And right now, Wisconsin's program is not signing up new land.
**Nia:** So did anything turn the line around?
**Andre:** The biggest jump came from the pandemic. Wisconsin's new gun-deer buyers rose over thirty-six percent in twenty twenty, and total gun-deer sales still fell. From twenty sixteen to twenty twenty-one, with all of this running, they fell about ten percent.
**Nia:** Anywhere else?
**Andre:** We found no Michigan or Wisconsin program with a measured effect on keeping hunters, against a comparison group. Nothing tried has turned either state's line around.
