AI Copyright Law: Is Training on Copyrighted Work Fair Use, or Theft?

The question: Is training AI on copyrighted work fair use, or theft?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

Two 2025 rulings split the fight — and left the biggest question to you.

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in August 2026, authors, artists, publishers and newsrooms had filed more than 70 copyright lawsuits against AI companies in US courts. One company, Anthropic, had already agreed to pay $1.5 billion to settle a claim over pirated books.

The fight

Green arguesTransformative Use

Learning from work you're allowed to read is different from stealing it. Two courts in 2025 called training transformative, and a model learns patterns, the way a child learns to write by reading. Punish the piracy, license what should be licensed, and let the learning go on.

Gold arguesConsent & Compensation

The work was copied without asking and without paying, and the product then competes with the people it learned from. A court ruled that an AI trained on a rival's legal database was not fair use. Licensing deals already exist, so if work is worth training on, it's worth licensing.

What both sides quietly agree on

AI models are trained by copying huge amounts of human work, and some of it was pirated. Courts treat that piracy as a separate wrong, and it already cost one company $1.5 billion. Both sides also expect creators to get paid somehow.

What the shouting gets wrong

Training is neither automatically theft nor automatically fair use. Courts have said the learning itself can be transformative, and stealing the books to do it is still stealing. The open question is whether a flood of AI work shrinking the market creators earn from is a harm the law must make someone pay for, and that argument hasn't yet won in court.

The verdict

Watched it already? Read the verdict as it aired

Walt So where does that leave us? Start with what is not in dispute.

Walt AI models are trained by copying enormous amounts of human work. Some of it was pirated, and courts have said that piracy is a real, separate wrong, one that already cost a company one and a half billion dollars. Both sides accepted all of it.

Walt The disagreement is narrower, and more interesting, than the shouting suggests. Both sides even agree on the destination: a licensing market where creators can actually be paid.

Walt The fight is about what the law compels. Is training transformative enough to be fair use? Or does the harm to creators' markets override that?

Walt The case for fair use says learning from work isn't the same as copying it, that two judges called the training transformative, and that the fix for bad data is to license it, not ban the learning, though it leans on rulings that are still under appeal.

Walt The case for compensation says the work was taken without consent, that when AI output competes it stops being transformative, and that a market already proves this can be paid for, though the dilution harm it rests on hasn't yet actually won in court.

Walt And notice what both sides quietly agreed on, without ever quite meaning to: nobody defends the piracy, and everybody expects creators to get paid somehow. The fight is only over whether the law forces it, or the market does.

Walt So here's the charge, and you're the jury. The settled facts are settled, the piracy, the transformation, nothing there to deliberate. But the real question, whether uncompensated training is fair use or a harm the law must price, is yours to weigh. You've heard both cases. Now you decide.
