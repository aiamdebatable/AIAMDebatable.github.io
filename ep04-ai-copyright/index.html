AI Copyright Law: Is Training on Copyrighted Work Fair Use, or Theft?

Is training AI on copyrighted work fair use, or theft?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

The verdictYour dialNumbers you heardUp next

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

Two 2025 rulings split the fight — and left the biggest question to you.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in August 2026, authors, artists, publishers and newsrooms had filed more than 70 copyright lawsuits against AI companies in US courts. One company, Anthropic, had already agreed to pay $1.5 billion to settle a claim over pirated books.

Settled

What both sides accept

AI models are trained by copying huge amounts of human work, and some of it was pirated. Courts treat that piracy as a separate wrong, and it already cost one company $1.5 billion. Both sides also expect creators to get paid somehow.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesTransformative Use

Learning from work you're allowed to read is different from stealing it. Two courts in 2025 called training transformative, and a model learns patterns, the way a child learns to write by reading. Punish the piracy, license what should be licensed, and let the learning go on.

Gold arguesConsent & Compensation

The work was copied without asking and without paying, and the product then competes with the people it learned from. A court ruled that an AI trained on a rival's legal database was not fair use. Licensing deals already exist, so if work is worth training on, it's worth licensing.

What the shouting gets wrong

Training is neither automatically theft nor automatically fair use. Courts have said the learning itself can be transformative, and stealing the books to do it is still stealing. The open question is whether a flood of AI work shrinking the market creators earn from is a harm the law must make someone pay for, and that argument hasn't yet won in court.

Walt So where does that leave us? Start with what is not in dispute.

Walt AI models are trained by copying enormous amounts of human work. Some of it was pirated, and courts have said that piracy is a real, separate wrong, one that already cost a company one and a half billion dollars. Both sides accepted all of it.

Walt The disagreement is narrower, and more interesting, than the shouting suggests. Both sides even agree on the destination: a licensing market where creators can actually be paid.

Walt The fight is about what the law compels. Is training transformative enough to be fair use? Or does the harm to creators' markets override that?

Walt The case for fair use says learning from work isn't the same as copying it, that two judges called the training transformative, and that the fix for bad data is to license it, not ban the learning, though it leans on rulings that are still under appeal.

Walt The case for compensation says the work was taken without consent, that when AI output competes it stops being transformative, and that a market already proves this can be paid for, though the dilution harm it rests on hasn't yet actually won in court.

Walt And notice what both sides quietly agreed on, without ever quite meaning to: nobody defends the piracy, and everybody expects creators to get paid somehow. The fight is only over whether the law forces it, or the market does.

Walt So here's the charge, and you're the jury. The settled facts are settled, the piracy, the transformation, nothing there to deliberate. But the real question, whether uncompensated training is fair use or a harm the law must price, is yours to weigh. You've heard both cases. Now you decide.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: When AI is trained on creators' work without paying for it, is that fair use, or a harm the law must price?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Fair use
    What it buys
    The case for fair use says learning from work isn't the same as copying it, that two judges called the training transformative, and that the fix for bad data is to license it, not ban the learning. One of those judges called training on books exceedingly transformative, and held that digitizing books you lawfully bought is fair use.
    What it costs
    It leans on rulings that are still under appeal. The rulings so far all come from trial courts, and no appeals court has given a binding answer yet. The judge who called training highly transformative also said that doesn't make it lawful, and that market dilution will often cause plaintiffs to decisively win. The authors in front of him made the wrong arguments.

    On the record: entry 1 in the fact-check log: Alsup called LLM training "exceedingly/spectacularly transformative", entry 2 in the fact-check log: Digitizing lawfully-purchased print books held fair use, entry 3 in the fact-check log: Kadrey v. Meta (June 2025, Judge Chhabria); 13 authors; "highly transformative" but ruling doesn't make it "lawful"; plaintiffs "made the wrong arguments", entry 4 in the fact-check log: Chhabria: "market dilution will often cause plaintiffs to decisively win the fourth factor"

  2. 2A harm the law must price
    What it buys
    The case for compensation says the work was taken without consent, that when AI output competes it stops being transformative, and that a market already proves this can be paid for. A court ruled that an AI trained on a rival's legal database to build a competing tool was not fair use, with the market effect decisive. The US Copyright Office called use that competes for the same audience at best modestly transformative.
    What it costs
    The dilution harm it rests on hasn't yet actually won in court. And the ruling against the legal-database AI is on appeal.
    Not measured
    Whether a flood of machine-made work actually shrinks the market creators are paid from: that question hasn't been fought out in court on a full record.

    On the record: entry 1 in the fact-check log: Thomson Reuters v. Ross: NOT fair use; AI trained on Westlaw to build a rival; factor four (market) decisive; on appeal to 3rd Cir., entry 2 in the fact-check log: US Copyright Office Part 3 (May 2025): AI training "not categorically fair use"; competing-audience use "at best modestly transformative"; rejects "inherently transformative"; market dilution cognizable, entry 3 in the fact-check log: Chhabria: "market dilution will often cause plaintiffs to decisively win the fourth factor"

Whichever you pick: Nobody defends the piracy, and everybody expects creators to get paid somehow. Both sides even agree on the destination: a licensing market where creators can actually be paid. The fight is only over whether the law forces it, or the market does.
