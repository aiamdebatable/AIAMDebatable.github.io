## The research · How much it swallowed
**Lena:** First, where things actually stand. To build these models, companies copy text at a scale that's hard to even picture. The dominant source, Common Crawl, is about nine and a half petabytes. Over three hundred billion web pages.
**Ryan:** Three hundred billion pages. How much of a model is that?
**Lena:** For GPT-three, more than eighty percent of the training text came from that one crawl. And these models train on hundreds of billions, up to around twenty trillion, words.
**Ryan:** So they're copying whole books, not just quoting a line here and there?
**Lena:** Whole works. Cover to cover. One notorious dataset, called Books-three, was about a hundred and ninety-six thousand books. And they were pirated, pulled from a shadow library.
**Ryan:** Pirated? That sounds less like research and more like a heist.
**Lena:** That's exactly the line the courts ended up drawing. And one study found about forty-five percent of Common Crawl was material whose own terms of service forbade this kind of for-profit training.
**Ryan:** So nearly half of it was taken against the site's stated wishes?
**Lena:** Roughly. And how the data was gathered turns out to be the first thing that's genuinely settled.

## The research · Who's suing — and the bill
**Lena:** This didn't stay quiet. Authors, artists, publishers, and newsrooms have filed more than seventy copyright lawsuits against AI companies in US courts.
**Lena:** In one case, thirteen novelists sued Meta. In another, the New York Times sued OpenAI. And in April twenty twenty-five, twelve of these cases were bundled into a single mega-case in New York.
**Ryan:** Seventy lawsuits. Has anyone actually won anything yet?
**Lena:** One line is now settled. In June twenty twenty-five, a judge ruled that Anthropic downloading seven million pirated books to keep was infringement. Not the training. The taking.
**Lena:** Anthropic then agreed to pay one and a half billion dollars, about three thousand dollars per book, to settle just that piracy claim.
**Ryan:** So the piracy part is basically decided?
**Lena:** For that company, yes. And nobody in this debate now stands up to defend downloading pirated books. The fight moved somewhere narrower.
**Lena:** The lawsuits, the pirated datasets, the settlement. That part is on the record. It's not where the real argument lives.

## The research · The line the courts drew
**Lena:** The settled fact that cuts deepest is a single distinction one judge drew. And it reframes the whole thing.
**Lena:** In Bartz versus Anthropic, Judge Alsup separated two questions people constantly blur together. What you do with the data. And how you got it.
**Ryan:** And those two came out differently?
**Lena:** Opposite ways. Training a model on books, he called exceedingly transformative. A likely fair use. But downloading pirated copies to keep was a separate wrong, headed to trial.
**Ryan:** So the same company was both in the clear and in trouble, at the same time?
**Lena:** Exactly. He even said digitizing books you lawfully bought is fine, like a schoolchild learning to write by reading. The problem was the theft, not the learning.
**Ryan:** Then why isn't the whole thing just settled?
**Lena:** Because he answered how you got it, and whether learning is transformative. He did not answer the question that pays a creator's bills. And that's where the real fight begins.

## The research · Two words that decide it
**Lena:** Two phrases do an enormous amount of quiet work in this fight. Both of them deserve a warning label.
**Lena:** The first is transformative. In court, it means the training serves a different purpose than the book did. Not that the output merely looks different, or that the model is impressive.
**Ryan:** So transformative isn't a magic pass?
**Lena:** Not at all. The US Copyright Office warned that when a model is trained to make work that competes for the same audience, it is, quote, at best modestly transformative.
**Lena:** The second phrase is market dilution. Not one AI book replacing one human book. But a flood of machine-made work shrinking the entire market that creators are paid from.
**Ryan:** And has a court actually taken that seriously?
**Lena:** One judge said the authors who lost their case likely would have won on dilution. That it will, quote, often cause plaintiffs to decisively win. They simply argued the wrong thing.
**Lena:** Transformative, and dilution. Hold onto both. They decide whether training is fair use, or a bill that eventually comes due.

## Quick check
**Ryan:** Before we hand this over to the debaters, one quick gut-check for you at home.
**Lena:** Of everything we just covered, which of these is actually settled? That US courts have ruled AI training is always fair use, so companies never have to pay? Or that downloading pirated books to train was ruled infringement, and cost one company one and a half billion dollars?
**Lena:** If you said the pirated-books ruling, that's the settled one. Training is always fair use is a myth. No court has ever said that.
**Lena:** What's genuinely up for debate starts now.

## The reframe
**Lucas:** So here's what happened. I submitted a simple question: is training AI on copyrighted work theft, yes or no? One big fight, the way it plays out online.
**Lucas:** But the research came back and told me something uncomfortable. Parts of this aren't a debate anymore.
**Lucas:** Courts have said the learning itself can be transformative. And they've said stealing the books to do it is still stealing. That's not fifty-fifty, and I'm not going to stage a fake fight over it.
**Lucas:** Pretending training is automatically theft, or automatically fair use, would just be manufacturing controversy. So we won't.

**Lucas:** Both AI teams had to accept the settled facts. Neither one gets to relitigate the piracy. Here's the case for calling it fair use.

## The debate · Team Green · Transformative Use
**Raj:** The case for calling this fair use doesn't deny that creators matter. It argues that learning from work you're allowed to read is not the same as stealing it.
**Raj:** Start with what a model actually is. When it trains, it isn't filing away copies to hand back out. A UK court found that one big image model doesn't even store the training pictures.
**Raj:** It's extracting patterns. How language works, how sentences flow. Two separate courts in twenty twenty-five called that purpose transformative. Different in kind from what a book is for.
**Raj:** That's why Judge Alsup compared it to teaching a schoolchild to write well by having them read widely. We don't make children license every book they ever learn from.
**Raj:** Yes, some of this data was acquired badly. This case does not defend that. Buy the books legally, and the very same training is fair use. The wrong was the theft, not the learning.
**Raj:** And copyright has never let an author lock up the ideas or the style inside a book. Only the exact words. A model learns the patterns, not the prose.
**Raj:** There's even a market forming on its own. News Corp signed a licensing deal reported at over two hundred fifty million dollars. Creators are choosing to license. Proof this can be paid for without banning it.
**Raj:** So don't outlaw the learning. Draw the line at the theft, license what should be licensed, and let models keep learning from the culture, the way we all do.

## The debate · Team Gold responds
**Kate:** Notice the move there. It's just learning quietly turns a company copying millions of works into a curious schoolchild. It is not one.
**Kate:** A child reads one book, at human speed, and forgets most of it. These systems copy the entire library, perfectly, to build a product that's sold back to us.
**Kate:** And buy the books legally doesn't rescue the argument. Even the judge who praised the training left the question that actually matters, the harm to creators' markets, wide open.
**Kate:** Calling it transformative doesn't answer who gets paid when the thing you trained replaces the very people you trained it on.

## The debate · Team Gold · Consent & Compensation
**Kate:** The case for consent and compensation starts with one simple fact. This was built on work that was never offered.
**Kate:** Millions of books, articles, and images, copied without asking and without paying. And when the product then competes with the very people it learned from, that's not a tribute. It's a taking.
**Kate:** And the law already sees this. In Thomson Reuters versus Ross, an AI was trained on someone else's legal database to build a rival tool. A court said plainly: not fair use.
**Kate:** The reason was factor four. Market effect. When your output substitutes for the original, the whole transformation argument collapses.
**Kate:** Now scale that up. It isn't one AI book beating one human book. It's a flood of machine work shrinking the entire market a writer or an illustrator earns from. Judges have a name for it. Market dilution.
**Kate:** And a licensing market already exists. News Corp, Reddit, and others signed real deals. Which means taking the rest for free isn't necessary. It's just cheaper.
**Kate:** The Copyright Office looked hard at the it's-inherently-transformative claim, and rejected it. Competing for the same audience, they said, is at best modestly transformative.
**Kate:** So the ask here isn't ban AI. It's consent, and compensation. If the work is valuable enough to train on, it's valuable enough to license.

## The debate · Team Green responds
**Raj:** The compensation point is fair. Where work is genuinely taken, pay for it. But that's a licensing problem to solve, not a reason to outlaw the learning itself.
**Raj:** And Thomson Reuters is a narrow case. An AI built to directly replace the exact product it copied. A general model that writes a poem doesn't replace any one novel.
**Raj:** Market dilution is still a theory looking for a verdict. No court has actually ruled that a model diluted a market. The judge who floated it admitted the plaintiffs never even proved it.
**Raj:** So the honest version isn't fair use versus theft. It's this: license what's taken, punish the piracy, and don't ban a machine for learning the way people always have.

## The verdict
**Walt:** So where does that leave us? Start with what is not in dispute.
**Walt:** AI models are trained by copying enormous amounts of human work. Some of it was pirated, and courts have said that piracy is a real, separate wrong, one that already cost a company one and a half billion dollars. Both sides accepted all of it.
**Walt:** The disagreement is narrower, and more interesting, than the shouting suggests. Both sides even agree on the destination: a licensing market where creators can actually be paid.
**Walt:** The fight is about what the law compels. Is training transformative enough to be fair use? Or does the harm to creators' markets override that?
**Walt:** The case for fair use says learning from work isn't the same as copying it, that two judges called the training transformative, and that the fix for bad data is to license it, not ban the learning, though it leans on rulings that are still under appeal.
**Walt:** The case for compensation says the work was taken without consent, that when AI output competes it stops being transformative, and that a market already proves this can be paid for, though the dilution harm it rests on hasn't yet actually won in court.
**Walt:** And notice what both sides quietly agreed on, without ever quite meaning to: nobody defends the piracy, and everybody expects creators to get paid somehow. The fight is only over whether the law forces it, or the market does.
**Walt:** So here's the charge, and you're the jury. The settled facts are settled, the piracy, the transformation, nothing there to deliberate. But the real question, whether uncompensated training is fair use or a harm the law must price, is yours to weigh. You've heard both cases. Now you decide.
