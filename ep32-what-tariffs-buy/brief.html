Episode 32 · The research

Research brief — Tariffs: They Raise What You Pay. Is What They Buy Worth It?

The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video. Every figure and quotation here is in the fact-check log, under the claim number given in brackets.

The question

Tariffs raise what you pay. Is what they buy worth it? Not "are tariffs good or bad", and not anyone's politics: the episode follows one instrument, the tariff, from the price of a washing machine to the factory that makes it, and asks what the country got for the cost. It will make nobody entirely happy, and that is the honest result. On who pays, the record leans clearly one way: Americans pay most of it. On whether it was worth it (did it bring factories home, did other countries open their markets to American goods, did it start a tariff war), the record has real answers on both sides, and the verdict depends on what you value. That second half is the heart of the episode.

Why now

In February the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump that the emergency-powers law (IEEPA) "does not authorize the President to impose tariffs" (opinion) [A1–A2]. That left an estimated $166 billion in collected duties, across 53 million import filings, to be paid back. Customs built a tool called CAPE to do it, and its own Federal Register notice says each refund goes directly to the importer of record's account (CBP, FR 2026-20228, 2026-10-05) [A6–A7]. In its latest sworn court declaration, filed on 2026-10-06, the agency says that as of 2 October about $136.6 billion had been accepted and about $126 billion "certified by the agency, and sent to the U.S. Department of Treasury … for disbursement" [A14] — its verb, never "paid". The same declaration says a third phase opened that morning, only for importers who sued and whose entries were already finally liquidated [A17]; the agency never calls it final [A18]. Meanwhile, more than two dozen consumer class actions against retailers allege that shoppers, not importers, paid those tariffs through higher prices and should share the money [A47–A50]. These are allegations: no class has been certified and no motion decided [A46]. Furniture makers are already booking the refunds in their accounts [D2-17, D2-18]. So the question "who actually pays a tariff?" is, right now, a live fight with a cheque attached. And it isn't over: the replacement tariffs on most imports (Section 301 "forced labor", 10–12.5%) were argued at the Court of International Trade on 2026-09-30, and the court has ordered further briefing due 2026-10-16, so no ruling can come before then [A33].

Splitthe script names the case on air, Learning Resources v. Trump, in ONE beat framed as

impact: the Supreme Court struck down most of the tariffs, the money is going back to the companies that imported the goods, and it is not going to American households. Spoken shape: "In February, in a case called Learning Resources v. Trump, the Supreme Court struck down most of these tariffs. About a hundred and sixty-six billion dollars is going back — to the companies that imported the goods. Not to you." The phases, the appeal and the Section 301 docket go on screen or in the description, never in the spoken track. Name the court, the case and the refund; frame the instrument, never a president or a party. The case name never goes in the title or thumbnail.

Settled (not the debate)

  • The law names one payer. The importer of record makes entry and deposits the duty when the goods come in; the refund goes only to that importer's account (or its designated party) — "no overpayments or payments to an erroneous party" [A7, A10, A11]. A shopper has no claim on the government.
  • The Court ruled on one law, not on tariffs. The holding is that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs; the Court did not decide the other tariff laws (Sections 232, 301, 122) [A1, A3]. The tariffs on washers and on upholstered furniture are Section 232 tariffs: they stand, and they are not refunded [D2-15, D2-16].
  • Most of the cost stayed in the US. Three independent estimates put about 90% of the 2025 tariff burden at the border on US firms and consumers together, not on foreign sellers (NY Fed, Feb 2026: 94% in the first eight months of 2025, 86% by November) [B17, B19]. One credible dissent puts foreign sellers' share at 40–50%, with a published critique of its method [B20–B21].
  • Prices rose, but late, and unevenly. Retailers absorbed costs and sold down inventory first [B12]. By early 2026 the Fed Board found tariffs passed through dollar for dollar into relative prices after about seven months [B5–B6]. In the anchor categories: washers and dryers are up 8.5% since January 2025 (10.2% from December 2024); major appliances as a whole +2.3%; furniture +2.0%; all three are still below their 2022 peaks [D2-22].
  • The household cost is a model, and the models agree on the size. About $1,100 a year under current law (Yale Budget Lab, 24 Aug 2026: $1,077 before shoppers switch products, $905 after, 2025 dollars), and it did not fall when one tariff lapsed, because another replaced it [B25, B28]. A different concept, tax collected per household, gives $820 for 2026 (Tax Foundation) [B30]. It bites the poorest tenth about three times as hard, as a share of income [B29].
  • The revenue is real. Customs duties were $194.9 billion in fiscal 2025, against $77.0 billion the year before [C1]. About three-quarters of the monthly take survived the ruling, because other tariffs replaced the struck-down ones [C4]; the refunds then turned net customs receipts negative for three months in 2026 [C5].
  • US exports rose about 15% in 2026, to almost everyone. Deal partners, countries with no deal (Mexico +17%), and a country whose deal is void (Malaysia +24%) alike; about $90bn of the $215bn rise is gold, crude and fuel oil [D1-19–D1-20]. No deal can claim the rise.

Contested (the real fight)

Side A — "Worth paying for": the cost is real, and it bought things money alone could not.

  • Other countries cut their tariffs on American goods, in law. The EU scrapped its duties on US industrial goods (Regulation 2026/1455, in force from 1 July 2026) [C8]. Britain opened duty-free quotas for US beef and 1.4bn litres of US ethanol from 30 June 2025, and about 82% of the ethanol quota had been claimed by 2 October [D1-1–D1-2]. Switzerland cut tariffs on US fish and farm goods and opened duty-free beef, bison and poultry quotas, in force from 14 November 2025 [D1-12]. Cambodia put 0% on new US goods from 8 August 2025 [D1-6a]. Japan now accepts US-built cars without re-testing, from 16 February 2026 [D1-10]. Vietnam cut duties on US ethanol, corn, nuts and chicken under the tariff threat [D1-4]. These are gazetted, dated cuts, not promises.
  • The feared tariff war mostly did not happen. Of the partners checked, only two imposed counter-tariffs: China (which escalated to 125%, then stood down to an extra 10%) and Canada [D1-25, D1-26]. The EU built a €93bn package and never applied it [C11]; India and Brazil prepared retaliation and, as far as found, applied nothing; Mexico threatened and held off; Indonesia says it "chose diplomacy rather than retaliation" [D1-28–D1-31].
  • Factory output is up, most in the protected industries. Manufacturing output is up 2.8% on 2024 and back to its 2019 level [D2-01]; raw steel +8.9% and iron and steel products +4.5% since January 2025 [D2-03]; pharmaceutical output +7.9% and jobs +3.3% [D2-07].
  • The washer tariff built plants that are still running. The 2018 washer tariff brought Samsung's plant in South Carolina, now past 8 million washers with its US-based suppliers up from 10% to 72%, and LG's in Tennessee, about 740 people making 6,000 washers and 1,000 dryers a day [C29, D2-10]. GE Appliances is moving front-load washers from China to Louisville (production 2027) and dryers from Mexico; its CEO calls the tariffs "an accelerant" that "makes the payback … much, much greater" [D2-11–D2-12]. Whirlpool, which makes about 80% of its US-sold appliances in the US from 96% US steel, says the appliance tariff helps "support a level playing field for domestic producers" [D2-14].
  • It raises serious money, and the dependence is documented. $194.9 billion in fiscal 2025, about 4.4 times the federal fuel taxes behind the Highway Trust Fund [C1, D1, D3]. The US is 100% import-reliant for 12 critical minerals [C35], makes about 10% of the chips it needs [C36], and about 53% of patented drugs sold here are made abroad (FDA figures, cited in the proclamation) [C37].

Side B — "Not at this price": the bill lands at home, and what it bought is thinner than announced.

  • You pay most of it. About 90 cents of each tariff dollar stayed with US firms and shoppers [B17, B19]; the Fed Board finds it passed on dollar for dollar after about seven months [B5]; Harvard's price trackers found shoppers bearing "roughly 43 percent" of the border cost by October 2025, the rest "mostly by U.S. firms", and rising [B14]. That is about $1,000 a year for the average household, depending on the model — roughly what a household spends in a whole year on furniture and major appliances together [B25, B30, D7]. The poorest tenth feel it about three times as hard [B29]. Washers and dryers are up 8.5% since January 2025 [D2-22]; last time, the washer tariff pushed them up 12.4% in five months [D2-21].
  • Most of the "deals" are promises. Indonesia's 0% on 99% of US goods starts only at entry into force, which waits on its parliament [D1-8]; India's is a framework not concluded by October [D1-13]; Thailand is still negotiating [D1-15]; Argentina's is signed, with no sign yet that it is in force [D1-14]; Korea's car-cap pledge is the US government's account, with no Korean rule found [D1-11a–b]. Malaysia signed, never ratified, and its minister called the deal "null and void" [D1-7]. Japan's rice pledge cut no tariff [D1-9]. Some cuts that are in force are small: Britain's US beef quota is barely used [D1-3]. And the exports rose to everyone, so the deals cannot be credited with them [D1-20].
  • The jobs did not come, and the anchor industries shrank. Factory jobs are 21,000 below January 2025 [C19]. Household-appliance output is down 6.7% and its jobs 2.9% since January 2025; furniture output is down 3.9% and its jobs by 10,000 [D2-08–D2-09]. Aluminium output is down 5.5% [D2-04]; the chip boom rests on the CHIPS Act, and chip jobs fell [D2-06]. Whirlpool's own filings say tariffs raised its costs, and its North American margin fell from 5.9% to 2.7%, which it puts down to "lower volume, tariff cost and inflation" [D2-14]. The goods deficit is no smaller than 2024's [D2-26], and announced reshoring was flat, 244,000 jobs in both 2024 and 2025 [D2-27].
  • Last time, a job cost over $815,000. The 2018 washer tariff cost buyers about $1.5bn a year, raised only $82m in revenue, and works out at over $815,000 per reported job [C28]. Across 2018-19, jobs gained from protection (+0.3%) were outweighed by higher input costs (−1.1%) and retaliation (−0.7%) — a comparison across industries [C23]; a second study found protected sectors' jobs "neither raised nor lowered" [C24].
  • Where retaliation came, it landed on farms, and one fight is live. Farm exports to China are forecast at $12bn this fiscal year, down from $16.2bn [C16]; taxpayers put up $12bn in "bridge" payments [C15]. With Canada, each side's tariff has been answered by the other's: US tariffs from 22 August 2026, Canada's counter-tariffs on C$27.6bn of US goods from 8 September, US import bans from 29 September [C12, D1-26].

The reframe

"Who pays a tariff?" has two true answers, and the shouting runs them together.

  • The law's payer is the importer. It deposits the duty when the goods come in, and it alone gets the refund [A7, A10]. That answer is simple and already decided.
  • The economic payer is a split — between the importer, the retailer, the shopper and, to a disputed degree, the foreign seller — and the split moves over time. Most of it stayed in the US from the start; the shopper's share grew month by month as prices caught up [B5, B14, B17].

The refund makes the gap visible: the money goes back to whoever wrote the cheque at the border, not to whoever bore the cost at the till. One furniture maker booked $51 million of tariff refunds in a quarter [D2-18]; another credits "refunds and pricing actions net of tariff costs" for a 240-basis-point rise in its gross margin [D2-17]. Neither is accused of anything: that is how the law sends the money. Second, "tariffs" is not one thing: the Court struck one legal route, and a broad tariff used for revenue or leverage and a narrow tariff on washers, steel or drugs have to be judged separately.

Both sides quietly agree

The shopper is never the legal payer and has no claim on the refund. And most of the cost stayed in America: even the study most favourable to tariffs leaves about half of it at home [B20]. So the honest fight is not "do Americans pay?" — they pay most of it. It is whether what that money bought (revenue, partner tariff cuts in law, a tariff war mostly avoided, more steel and the washer plants) is worth about a thousand dollars a year per household, when the jobs did not follow and most of the deals are still on paper.

Verdict framing

No winner is declared. On who pays, the episode says plainly where the record leans. On whether it was worth it, the viewer gets a dial — what would you pay about $1,000 a year for? — with four settings, each with something real behind it and a documented failure:

  1. For the revenue. It raised $194.9bn in a year [C1]. Failure on record: when the legal base fell, refunds turned net customs receipts negative for three months [C5].
  2. For the leverage. The EU, Britain, Switzerland and Cambodia cut tariffs on US goods in law, Japan dropped its car re-testing, and only China and Canada hit back [C8, D1-1, D1-6a, D1-10, D1-12, D1-25–D1-31]. Failure on record: Indonesia, India, Thailand, Argentina and Korea are signed or announced only, Malaysia's is "null and void", the export rise went to everyone, and Canada is in a live second round [D1-7, D1-8, D1-11b, D1-13–D1-15, D1-20, D1-26].
  3. For the factories. Factory output is up 2.8% on 2024, steel and pharma are up, the 2018 washer plants still run, and GE Appliances is bringing washers home [D2-01, D2-03, D2-07, D2-10, D2-11]; the dependence on minerals, chips and drugs is documented [C35–C37]. Failure on record: factory jobs are down 21,000; appliance and furniture output and jobs are down; aluminium is down; chips are the CHIPS Act's story; the 2018 washer jobs cost over $815,000 each [C19, C28, D2-04, D2-06, D2-08–D2-09].
  4. For nothing: keep the money. About $1,000 a year back in the household [B25]; the 2018-19 record found no net job gain [C23–C24]. Failure on record: without the washer tariff, the Samsung and LG plants would not have come [C29, D2-10], and the EU, UK, Swiss and Cambodian cuts came in deals struck under the tariffs [C8, D1-1, D1-6a, D1-12, D1-18].

Make it matter

The anchor is one thing in the viewer's house: the washing machine (with its twin, the dryer), and furniture as the second beat. It carries both halves of the question — what you pay, and what it bought. The journey runs in this order:

  1. The price tag. Washers and dryers cost about 8 to 10% more than when the tariffs began (laundry equipment +8.5% since January 2025, +10.2% from December 2024) [D2-22]. Last time a washer tariff came in, in 2018, the same index jumped 12.4% in five months [D2-21]. The honest frame: laundry is where it shows; appliances as a whole (+2.3%) and furniture (+2.0%) are up modestly and still below their 2022 highs [D2-22]. The household total: about $1,000 a year, roughly what a household spends in a year on furniture and major appliances together [B25, D7].
  2. The tariff on it. Since 23 June 2025, washers, dryers, dishwashers, fridges and ranges carry the steel tariff; since 6 April 2026 it is 25% of the machine's full value [D2-15]. Upholstered wooden furniture, kitchen cabinets and vanities have carried 25% since 14 October 2025 [D2-16]. The Fed Board's $20-item picture shows why a 25% tariff shows up as a smaller percentage on the shelf, and why that does not mean you paid less of it [B1–B2].
  3. Who gets the money back (the one court beat). Learning Resources v. Trump: the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs, and about $166 billion is going back to the importers, not to households [A1, A6, A7]. The concrete picture is furniture makers booking refunds: one $51 million in a quarter, another a 240-basis-point margin lift from refunds and pricing [D2-17, D2-18]. Shoppers' suits saying "we paid that" are allegations [A46]. (The washer and furniture tariffs themselves are not refunded: they are a different law [D2-15, D2-16].)
  4. What it bought — the heart. Start with the washer: the 2018 tariff brought Samsung's and LG's plants, still running, at over $815,000 per reported job [C28, D2-10]; GE Appliances is moving washers home, calling the tariffs "an accelerant" [D2-11]; Whirlpool says it levels the field, and that the tariff costs cut its margins [D2-14]; and today US appliance output and jobs are down [D2-08]. Then zoom out to the three questions the viewer asks: did factories come home (output up, jobs down) [D2-01, C19]; did other countries open up to US goods (some in law, most on paper) [C8, D1-1–D1-15]; did it start a tariff war (only China and Canada hit back) [D1-25–D1-32]. End on the dial.

The cold open states the question in plain words in its first fifteen seconds. No invented statistics in the clash; the camps the viewer will recognise are "a tax on us" and "the price of a stronger hand and factories at home". A personal washer-and-dryer story, if one airs, belongs to the host's own close, in his words; no cast member tells it.

Script traps

  • The one court beat. On air: the case name, the ruling, "about $166 billion", "to the companies that imported the goods, not to you". Everything else about the refund — phases, appeals, the Section 301 docket, the CAPE tool — is screen or description only.
  • The refund verb. The agency says "certified … and sent to the U.S. Department of Treasury … for disbursement" — never "paid" or "refunded" for its totals. Screen: "$126bn certified and sent to Treasury, of $136.6bn accepted (CBP declaration, filed Oct 6, 2026)" [A14]. Never "$136bn refunded" (that is claims accepted). Safe Treasury anchor: "more than $100 billion had gone back to importers by the end of August" (all customs refunds) [A15].
  • The refund figures include interest; the $166bn does not. Never subtract one from the other to show "what's left" [A21].
  • Phase 3 is a third phase, for importers who sued, not a final one [A17–A18]. The government is appealing the refunds to importers who did not sue [A22]. Never "every importer will get its money".
  • The Section 301 case cannot be decided before 16 October [A33]. Re-check the docket on publish day: a ruling changes "current law".
  • The refund is not the washer tariff. Refunds are of the struck-down emergency tariffs; the washer and furniture tariffs are Section 232 and stand [D2-15, D2-16].
  • Furniture refunds: two different 240s. La-Z-Boy's +240bp is gross margin, from "refunds and pricing actions net of tariff costs"; RH's +240bp is a segment margin, with refunds worth 590bp inside it. Never merge them; say "booked tariff refunds" and "pricing actions"; never say a company "kept" money [D2-17, D2-18].
  • "Much more expensive" fits LAUNDRY only. Washers and dryers +8.5% (+10.2% from December 2024); major appliances +2.3%; furniture +2.0%; all below their 2022 peaks. Never say appliance prices "soared" as a category, never "record highs"; say "up since the tariffs began" [D2-22].
  • Electronics is a trap. Phones and computers were exempt from the emergency tariff; phones fell 17% and TVs 3%; computers rose 11% for a reason not tied to tariffs [D2-19, D2-23, D2-24]. Never "electronics rose because of tariffs". Coffee's +22% is not attributed to tariffs [D2-25].
  • The appliance tariff changed form. From 23 June 2025, 50% of the steel content; from 6 April 2026, 25% of the full value, where steel is at least 15% of the weight. Never "a 50% tariff on washers" [D2-15]. The furniture tariff is upholstered wooden furniture, kitchen cabinets and vanities only, not "furniture" [D2-16].
  • Output ≠ jobs. Factory output +2.8% on 2024 and factory jobs −21,000: say both [D2-01, C19]. Use the 2024 average as the base for autos (+2.2%), never January 2025's dip (+12.7%) [D2-05]. Quote percentage changes, never index levels.
  • Chips are the CHIPS Act's story: TSMC Arizona was producing in late 2024 on a $6.6bn grant; chip jobs fell. Never credit chip output to tariffs [D2-06].
  • "Announced" ≠ "building" ≠ "producing". GE's washers start 2027, its dryers late 2027; GM's moved models 2027; Lilly's Houston plant broke ground in September; TSMC's $100bn is an "intention". Only the 2018 washer plants are producing [D2-10–D2-12, D2-28–D2-30].
  • Most companies do not credit tariffs. The only on-record credits are the GE Appliances CEO's "accelerant" (to CNN) and Whirlpool's "level playing field" (its 10-K). Quote them exactly; never "companies say tariffs brought them home" [D2-11, D2-14].
  • "Committed" ≠ "cut". In force with a legal act: EU, UK, Switzerland, Cambodia, Japan (cars, a non-tariff barrier), Vietnam (open to all). Signed, framework or announced: Indonesia, India, Thailand, Argentina, Korea, Vietnam's deal, Taiwan, the Philippines. Never "dozens of countries dropped their tariffs". Malaysia is never on the list of countries that cut [D1-1–D1-17].
  • A US fact sheet is a claim about the partner. "100 percent" (Cambodia) is USTR's line; the sub-decree excludes used goods [D1-6b]. Indonesia's "99%" starts only at entry into force [D1-8].
  • Japan did not open its rice market: a bigger US share of an existing tariff-free quota, and the $8bn is an intention. Total US rice exports fell this year [D1-9, D1-24]. The $550bn memorandum is not binding; about $4.6bn of loans is the money visible [C9–C10].
  • Vietnam's cut was open to every WTO member without a trade deal, made two days before 2 April 2025: "under the tariff threat", never "for America only" [D1-4].
  • Exports rose to everyone. Any line tying a deal to an export rise says "correlation, not cause". Never use Switzerland's tripling: it is gold, and it came before the deal [D1-20–D1-21].
  • UK ethanol: "the duty-free quota is being used", never "exports to Britain rose" [D1-2, D1-22]. UK beef: "barely used", no tonnage [D1-3].
  • The legal floor moved. Most deals traded partner cuts for relief from the tariffs struck down in February; India's lets either side "modify its commitments" [D1-13, D1-18].
  • Retaliation. "Of the partners we checked, only China and Canada imposed counter-tariffs; India, Brazil and Mexico prepared or threatened them." China: say both the 125% and the stand-down to 10%. Canada: "each side's tariff was answered by the other's" [D1-25–D1-32, C12].
  • "60 economies", counting the EU as one — never "60 countries"; 86 countries if the EU's members are counted separately [A30–A31]. The 301 rates are an all-in ceiling for the EU, Taiwan, Japan, Korea and Switzerland [A28]. "99.4% of imports" is a law firm's figure: attribute or omit [A32].
  • Never "consumers paid 26%". The 26% is a price response (a 10% tariff → about 2.6% on consumer goods prices) and the authors say it fits full pass-through [B1–B2]. Never put 24% or 26% beside 90% or 100% without the $10/$20 picture: different denominators.
  • "US firms and consumers" ≠ "consumers". The 90% is the US-versus-foreign split at the border [B17]. Say "Americans — companies and shoppers together — paid about 90 cents of each tariff dollar". The White House's attack on that paper airs, if at all, as an attack, not a rebuttal [B18].
  • The dissent travels with its method: Freund's 40–50% is weighted by import size and may count quality downgrades as price cuts [B20–B21]. Haver's 57% is a what-if; leave it off or call it that [B22]. Goldman's split is secondary: screen only, "as reported" [B23].
  • Price level vs inflation rate. Spoken: "under one percent on prices overall, more on goods"; screen: Fed Board +0.8% core PCE price level to Feb 2026 (0.4% in its own check) and Minneapolis Fed 0.2–0.4 points of July 2026 core inflation — never one "0.2–0.8" range [B6–B8].
  • Household cost. Spoken, rounded: "about a thousand dollars a year for the average household, depending on the model." Screen, exact: Yale $1,077 before / $905 after shoppers switch (current law, 24 Aug 2026, 2025 dollars); Tax Foundation $820 in 2026 (tax collected) [B25, B30]. Both are models, not measurements. Never air April's $760–1,500 spread as today [B27].
  • The comparators.
  • "About $1,000 a year ≈ what a household spends in a whole year on furniture and major appliances together" ($648 + $385 = $1,033, BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey 2024, "consumer units") [D6–D7]. Footnote the units and that it is an average over every household, most of which buys no washer in a given year. The clothing comparator (about half a year's clothing, [D5]) is retired with the clothing anchor.
  • "Fiscal 2025 customs, $194.9bn ≈ 4.4 times the federal fuel taxes behind the Highway Trust Fund, $44.3bn" [C1, D1, D3]. Label it "federal fuel taxes (Highway Trust Fund)" [D2].
  • ⛔ Never set revenue per household (about $1,400) beside the household-cost figures: one is money collected, the other a price effect [C39].
  • Revenue is fiscal-year, net of refunds. $194.9bn is fiscal 2025, before the refunds; never "$286bn in 2025" (calendar 2025 was $264bn) [C1–C2]. No fiscal-2026 total exists until mid-October.
  • The EU cut is real but conditional: two-thirds of US industrial goods already entered duty-free; it sunsets in 2029 and can be suspended [C8]. Never "Europe opened its markets", and never "nothing was delivered". The $750bn/$600bn figures are intentions [C7].
  • Jobs and construction: present both directions or neither [C19–C22]. Flaaen-Pierce is a relative, cross-industry estimate [C23]. Washer jobs are "reported", and say "over $800,000 per job" [C28].
  • The goods deficit did not shrink against 2024; 2025 was swollen by stockpiling [D2-26]. The Reshoring Initiative counts announcements, from an advocacy group, and 2025 equalled 2024 [D2-27].
  • Farm aid is a taxpayer cost, not lost sales, and USDA frames it as covering market disruption and input costs [C15]. Farm exports to China: $12bn forecast, never "$9bn" [C17].
  • National-security figures come from the agencies that imposed the tariffs; pharma's 53% is FDA's figure, cited in the proclamation [C37].
  • Class actions are allegations. Costco admits raising some prices; the dispute is whether it owes shoppers part of the refund [A43]. Never say a retailer "kept" money. No state has sued a retailer for shoppers; states sued the government [A52]. Parcel carriers billing shoppers the duty directly is alleged in suits: screen or description only, if at all [A53].
  • "The ruling was only about the emergency-powers law"; Learning Resources' own case was dismissed for lack of jurisdiction — never "Learning Resources won its refund" [A3, A5].
  • 30-for-30 cut nothing: two lists worth about $30bn each in 2024 trade, for possible future cuts [A24–A27].
  • Steel: utilisation "high 70s to about 80%", never "81%" or "never 80%" [C30].

⛔ Off air

  • "$13bn paid" or "$13bn accepted" — no source; contradicted by every declaration [A16].
  • "$31–44bn outstanding" — no source; not computable like for like [A21].
  • "Mexico never retaliated" — no current source [C13, D1-30].
  • 8,700 steel jobs at about $650,000 each [C27].
  • Tax Foundation "$900" per household [B31].
  • Penn Wharton's "$132.5bn net new revenue" [C6].
