1. Why now — what was hunted, and what was rejected

  • Chosen: the refund. The Supreme Court held on 2026-02-20 in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump that the emergency-powers law (IEEPA) does not authorize tariffs; the customs agency is returning an estimated $166 billion of those duties, by law to the importer of record; and more than two dozen consumer class actions allege that shoppers paid through prices. "Who pays a tariff?" is, this week, a live court fight with a cheque attached.
  • Corrected during the hand pass (rows A12–A18): the customs agency's sworn declarations never say "paid". Their words are "certified by the agency, and sent to the U.S. Department of Treasury … for disbursement" (latest, filed 2026-10-06: about $136.6bn accepted, about $126bn certified and sent, as of 2026-10-02). What opened on 2026-10-06 is a third phase, only for importers who sued, never a "final" one. The trade court cannot rule on the replacement (Section 301 "forced labor") tariffs before further briefing due 2026-10-16.
  • Rejected: the pending Section 301 ruling as the spine (strongest reject; a ruling would replace this peg), Canada's import ban (leverage, an earlier episode's storyline), a procurement memo against Canadian goods, the 2027 USMCA review notice, a polysilicon stockpiling rule, the US–China "30-for-30" lists (a stated intention, not an instrument), and a Mexico "deadline" story that turned out to be a year old.

Fairness audit

  • Equal rigour, then the record leans where it leans. Both teams' best evidence went through the same hand pass. The pro side's case gained in the check: the engine had said leverage delivered nothing binding, and the record shows an EU regulation adopted in law (C8) and first Japan loans signed (C10). The con side's case gained too: the household cost did not fall after the Section 122 surcharge lapsed (B28), and the "26%" that looked like consumers getting off lightly turned out to be consistent with full pass-through (B1–B2). Neither side was trimmed or padded to look even.
  • Where it leans, said plainly: on who bears the cost, the record leans one way — most of it stayed in the US (about 90% at the border by three independent estimates; one credible dissent near half, with a published critique). On whether that cost bought enough, the record does not settle it: it is a judgment about revenue, leverage and security against prices, and that is the viewer's call.
  • The top-up passes cut both ways. D1 and D2 were run because the "worth it" half was thin. The pro side gained real, dated facts: partner tariff cuts in law (EU, UK, Switzerland, Cambodia, Japan's car testing), a tariff war that mostly did not happen, factory output up, and the 2018 washer plants still running. It also lost claims it is often credited with: most Asian "99%/100%" deals are not in force, the export rise went to almost everyone, and appliance and furniture output and jobs fell. The pitch's own framing ("much more expensive" appliances, electronics) was corrected too (D2-22, D2-23). Nothing was trimmed or padded to look even.
  • The instrument, never a person or party. Every claim is about a tariff, a statute, a court or an agency. The one quotation from a named official (B18) is a public attack on a study, logged so it is not mistaken for a rebuttal; it is not a reason to air it.
  • Allegations stay allegations. No class is certified and no motion is decided (A46); no retailer is said to have "kept" money; Costco's own filing is quoted for what it admits and what it denies.
