Research brief — Deepfakes and Elections: Label It, Ban It, or Answer It?

The full research behind the episode. Design type: the verdict is what survives both teams plus the one dial the viewer sets. Researched 2026-10-03: peg hunt, then a deep-research fan-out in design mode, then three hand passes that opened every relied-on primary (court orders, statutes, FCC and FEC records, the studies, the platforms' own pages). Every number below is in the fact-check log with its source.

⛔ Frame (ruled 2026-09-30 and 2026-10-01): the process and why the viewer should care, never who gets elected. Examples balanced across parties. Which party's ads use AI more stays OFF air; nothing on air may say or imply both parties use AI ads equally ("both parties' races have been hit" is fine). Foreign influence operations are a different episode. Never re-render a fake of a real candidate.

The question

A fake video of a candidate lands in your feed. Should it be illegal, just labelled, or left to counter-speech?

Why now

On 16 September 2026 a federal judge in Montana blocked the state's 2025 election-deepfake law — for the people who sued — finding it likely discriminates by viewpoint because it "treats identically deceptive media differently based solely on whether its message is weaponized to damage or deployed to elevate a candidate." The law let a labelled fake run; the court said a government-mandated label "functions as an outright alteration of the speech itself", and named counter-speech, tort law, criminal liability for election interference and education as the less restrictive alternatives (Accountability in State Government v. Knudsen, order). Twelve days earlier the Wesleyan Media Project counted at least 164 AI-made or AI-enhanced political ads this cycle, just under $80 million, and 69% of the ads (78% of the spending) carried no AI disclaimer — an undercount by its own account (Wesleyan). The ban is losing in court, and the label is losing in practice — five weeks before the vote.

Settled (not the debate)

  • Most states have something; almost none of it is a pure ban. NCSL counts 31 states regulating election deepfakes: 28 by disclosure, Minnesota and Texas with windowed bans, Maryland year-round. But several "label" laws are really bans a label can cure: Michigan (90 days before an election), California (cut from 120 to 30 days on 19 Sept 2026), Montana (60 days). Texas covers video only, 30 days out.
  • Every federal court that has weighed one of these laws against free speech has blocked it — California's (Aug 2025, for the named plaintiffs), Hawaii's (Jan 2026, the whole statute; Hawaii did not appeal), Montana's (Sept 2026, for the plaintiffs). In each, the label offered as a safe harbour did not save the law.
  • Minnesota's ban was not "upheld". The 8th Circuit (Feb 2026) refused an early injunction on standing and delay: one challenger's videos were labelled parody, so the law didn't cover them; the other waited sixteen months. No ruling on the merits.
  • Even lies about voting aren't automatically bannable. The 8th Circuit (20 Aug 2026) blocked part of a Minnesota law against false statements meant to stop people voting, as applied to one group's claims about felon voting: there is no "historic and traditional categor[y]" of unprotected speech for false statements about voting; a law has to look like fraud — someone relies on the lie and is harmed — or pass strict scrutiny. The Supreme Court's 2018 line that states "may prohibit messages intended to mislead voters about voting requirements and procedures" is a footnote, and the 8th Circuit read it narrowly.
  • No conviction under a state election-deepfake law has been found. What exists: civil suits, an Oregon investigation, a Texas complaint to a county sheriff, cease-and-desist letters.
  • The federal referees are off the field. The FEC has had no quorum since 1 May 2025 (two of six seats filled); its 2024 action was an interpretive rule that existing fraud law applies whatever the technology. The FCC's 2024 proposal to require AI disclosure in broadcast political ads was never adopted.
  • The one clear US case was caught by laws that aren't about AI. January 2024, New Hampshire: 9,581 calls in a cloned presidential voice told primary voters to stay home, two days before the vote. The FCC fined the political consultant behind it $6 million under the caller-ID spoofing law; the phone company that carried it settled for $1 million; three voters won $7,500 each in federal court. A jury acquitted him of all 22 state criminal charges. New Hampshire passed its election-deepfake law afterwards.
  • Labels nudge belief, barely touch sharing. 7,579 people: a plain "AI-generated" label cut belief by 0.14 standard deviations and had no significant effect on sharing; "Manipulated" (−0.27) and "False" (−0.23) did more. Telling people deepfakes exist made them doubt real videos too.
  • There's no evidence a deepfake changed a 2024 result — and no study built to measure one. Of 78 election deepfakes in WIRED's worldwide tracker, 39 had no deceptive intent (Kapoor & Narayanan). The Turing Institute's reports found "no evidence" one "measurably altered an election result", largely because of "insufficient data" on voter behaviour.
  • Most voters meet the platforms' labels, not the law's. Meta, Google/YouTube and TikTok require AI disclosure on political ads or realistic altered content; Meta blocks new political ads in the final week; TikTok bans paid political ads.

Contested (the real fight)

Team Gold — "Draw a line: ban deceptive fakes near the vote": a face and a voice do damage a footnote can't undo, and a fake 48 hours out can't be answered before the polls close — New Hampshire's calls went out two days before the vote. Labels are already being ignored: 69% of known AI ads carried none, and a Wesleyan researcher's cross-check found labelling no better in states that require it (29%) than in states that don't (32%, small sample). The narrow designs answer the courts: Maryland's 2026 law requires intent, harm to a voter and exempts satire, and makes the state elections administrator publish corrections.

Team Green — "Answer it, don't ban it": every court to weigh these laws on the merits has found they pick sides by viewpoint, and the label meant to save them is compelled speech — in California's case, a satire disclaimer that "would kill the joke." Satirists are who keep getting swept in. No state has convicted anyone, so the bans chill speech without catching anything. The worst real case was caught by spoofing law, a phone network's traceback and a civil suit. And warnings have a cost: people start calling real video fake, which helps any liar who wants to dismiss true footage.

The reframe

It's argued as three rival designs. In practice nearly every US law is a hybrid — a ban inside a window, cured by a label — and none has been enforced to a conviction. The fight comes down to two things: who decides what counts as deceptive, and how fast a correction reaches the same voters. Every case decided in court so far involved satirists. The one clear harm — a fake telling people not to vote — was caught by old laws, and after this summer's 8th Circuit ruling even a law aimed only at voting lies needs to look like fraud to survive. The label most voters actually see is a platform's, not a statute's.

Both sides quietly agree

A deliberate lie about when, where or how to vote that someone relies on is the narrowest target — the courts point to fraud-like laws, Maryland builds harm into its ban, and the 8th Circuit says reliance and harm are what make false speech punishable. Obvious satire should be safe. And a fast, official correction to the same audience is part of the answer — Maryland writes it into law, and the courts list counter-speech first among the alternatives. The real disagreement is whether the law should reach candidate impersonation beyond voting logistics at all.

Verdict framing (design: what survives both, plus your dial)

The design that survives both teams: (1) a narrow, fraud-shaped law against false information about when, where and how to vote — intent, reliance, harm — in any medium; (2) disclosure on PAID AI ads, with the duty on the sponsor; (3) a duty on election officials to correct fast, to the same audience; (4) everything else — mockery, satire, attack — left to counter-speech.

The dial you set: when a fake of a candidate shows up, what should the law do? ① Ban it near the vote (Texas, Maryland, Minnesota) — failure: courts keep blocking these, and no conviction yet. ② Ban it unless it's labelled (Michigan, California, Montana) — failure: the label didn't save Montana's or California's law, and most ads skip it anyway. ③ Require a label only (most of the 31 states; the platforms) — failure: a plain "AI-generated" tag barely changes sharing. ④ Answer it with counter-speech and the laws we already have (what the courts name; how New Hampshire was handled) — failure: the criminal case collapsed, and a correction can lose the race against a fake in the last 48 hours.

Build notes

  • ⛔ Party guardrail: the Texas complaint, the New York complaint, the Michigan and Arizona suits name candidates — use the PROCESS only ("a candidate filed a criminal complaint about an opponent's AI ads"). Balanced examples available: a Vermont creator who faked one candidate from each party; a New York House race where each side's AI video targeted the other (both scout-grade — re-verify before use).
  • ⛔ Montana scope: the injunction protects the plaintiffs only; Montana's law still applies to everyone else.
  • ⛔ The robocall's exact text contains party words — paraphrase it ("a voice like the President's told primary voters to save their vote for November"), never read it, never re-create the voice.
  • ⛔ Say "the political consultant behind it", not his name. "Acquitted" (criminal) and "fined" (FCC, civil) are both true; collection of the fine is unverified — never "he paid".

  • Cold-open candidate: the New Hampshire call (a phone rings, a familiar voice says stay home) → the turn: no deepfake law caught it → the question.
