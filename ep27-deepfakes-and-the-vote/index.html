Deepfakes and Elections: Label It, Ban It, or Answer It?

The question: A fake video of a candidate lands in your feed: should it be illegal, just labelled, or left to counter-speech?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

A cloned voice told voters to stay home, and no AI law caught it. Should a fake be banned, labelled, or answered?

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in October 2026, the midterms were weeks away. In September a federal judge had blocked Montana's law on AI fakes of candidates, for the people who sued, and a count found at least 164 AI-made political ads that cycle, 69% without an AI disclaimer.

The fight

Green arguesAnswer it

The worst fake on record was caught by the phone network, a caller-ID law and three voters in court. Every federal court that has weighed one of these laws against free speech has blocked it, and warnings make people doubt real video. Use the laws we have, and make election offices correct voting lies fast.

Gold arguesDraw a line

The New Hampshire calls went out two days before the vote, and a later fine gives no one their vote back. The laws courts struck were broad ones; nobody has struck a narrow design like Maryland's. Ban lies about when, where and how to vote, built like fraud, with satire exempt and a duty to correct before the polls close.

What both sides quietly agree on

Thirty-one states have a law, most AI ads skip the label, and every federal court that has weighed one of these laws against free speech has blocked it. The episode found no conviction under them, and the one clear harm was caught by old laws. Both teams would make election offices correct a lie about voting, fast, to the same people.

What the shouting gets wrong

The real question is who decides what counts as deceptive, and how fast the truth reaches the same voters. The design that survived both teams is narrow: a fraud-shaped law against lies about voting, disclosure on paid AI ads, fast corrections, and everything else left to an answer. How hard the law should lean on a fake of a candidate was left to you.

The verdict

Andre This one doesn't resolve, and neither team wins it. Three things from me: what's settled, what survived, and what's yours.

Andre Both teams accepted the same record. Thirty-one states have a law. Most ads skip the label. Every federal court that has weighed one of these laws against free speech has blocked it, the label offered as a way out never saved one, and no one has been convicted under them. The one clear harm was caught by old laws.

Andre What survived from green. The label meant to save these laws was itself held to be compelled speech. Satirists are who keep getting swept in. Warnings make people doubt real video. And the worst case on record was answered by laws that weren't about AI at all.

Andre What survived from gold. A fake two days out can't be answered before the polls close. Labels are skipped even where they're required. The national referees are out of action. And the laws that lost were broad ones. A narrow, fraud-shaped design hasn't been struck.

Andre What fell. Green's comfort: the old laws caught New Hampshire after the damage, and the criminal case collapsed. Gold's fear: the narrowest line she drew, a law against voting lies, was partly blocked in August, and none of these bans has ever produced a conviction.

Andre So here's the design that survives both teams. A narrow law against lies about when, where and how to vote, built like fraud: intent, reliance, harm. Disclosure on paid AI ads, with the duty on whoever pays. A duty on election offices to correct, fast, to the same people. And everything else, the mockery, the attacks, left to an answer.

Andre That leaves one dial neither team can set for you. When a fake of a candidate shows up, how hard should the law lean? Ban it outright, like Texas and Maryland. Ban it unless it's labelled, like Michigan and California. Require a label only, like most of the thirty-one states and the platforms. Or answer it with the laws we already have, the way New Hampshire was handled.

Andre So the question we're handing you isn't AI or not AI. It's this: when a fake lands in your feed, how hard should the law lean? That one's yours.

What our fact-check caught

7 of the 32 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidEvery federal court that has reached one of these laws has blocked it.

    The record saysMinnesota's federal courts declined to block its law, on procedural grounds. The claim holds for the courts that weighed the laws against free speech: California, Hawaii and Montana.

  2. Draft saidA court upheld Minnesota's deepfake ban.

    The record saysAn appeals court only refused to block it early, over delay. Nobody has ruled on whether the law itself stands.

  3. Draft saidA federal judge blocked Montana's law.

    The record saysOnly for the people who sued. It still applies to everyone else, and the ruling is preliminary.

  4. Draft saidHawaii paid $118,000 in legal fees after its law was struck.

    The record saysHawaii agreed to pay about $118,000, pending approval of the money by its legislature.

  5. Draft saidAn AP analysis found AI ads were labelled no more often in states that require it.

    The record saysThe comparison was a Wesleyan researcher's check, quoted by the AP, on a small sample.
