Deepfakes and Elections: Label It, Ban It, or Answer It?

A fake video of a candidate lands in your feed: should it be illegal, just labelled, or left to counter-speech?

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in October 2026, the midterms were weeks away. In September a federal judge had blocked Montana's law on AI fakes of candidates, for the people who sued, and a count found at least 164 AI-made political ads that cycle, 69% without an AI disclaimer.

Settled

What both sides accept

Thirty-one states have a law, most AI ads skip the label, and every federal court that has weighed one of these laws against free speech has blocked it. The episode found no conviction under them, and the one clear harm was caught by old laws. Both teams would make election offices correct a lie about voting, fast, to the same people.

What the shouting gets wrong

The real question is who decides what counts as deceptive, and how fast the truth reaches the same voters. The design that survived both teams is narrow: a fraud-shaped law against lies about voting, disclosure on paid AI ads, fast corrections, and everything else left to an answer. How hard the law should lean on a fake of a candidate was left to you.

The verdict

What survives both teams
A narrow law against lies about when, where and how to vote, built like fraud: intent, reliance, harm. Disclosure on paid AI ads, with the duty on whoever pays. A duty on election offices to correct, fast, to the same people. And everything else, the mockery, the attacks, left to an answer.
What didn't hold up
Green's comfort: the old laws caught New Hampshire after the damage, and the criminal case collapsed. Gold's fear: the narrowest line she drew, a law against voting lies, was partly blocked in August, and none of these bans has ever produced a conviction.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: When a fake of a candidate shows up, how hard should the law lean?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Ban it outright
    What it buys
    It takes on the hard case: the judge kept Gold's points that a fake two days out can't be answered before the polls close, and that labels are skipped even where they're required. The laws that lost were broad ones; nobody has struck a law built the way Maryland's is. Maryland's, from this year, needs intent and harm to a voter, exempts satire, carries up to $5,000 or five years, and makes the state's elections administrator publish a correction. Texas's covers video only, in the last 30 days.
    What it costs
    What fell: the narrowest line Gold drew, a law against lies about voting, was partly blocked by an appeals court in August, as applied to one group's claims about who can vote. We found no conviction under any of these laws. Every federal court that has weighed one of these laws against free speech has blocked it.
    Not measured
    Whether a law built like Maryland's holds up: it hasn't been tested in court.

    On the record: row 8 of the fact-check log, row 7 of the fact-check log, row 14 of the fact-check log, row 17 of the fact-check log, row 32 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected)

  2. 2Ban it unless it's labelled
    What it buys
    A labelled fake can still run, and the ban only applies in a window before the vote: Michigan's runs 90 days, and California's used to run 120 days and became 30 on September 19. A plain AI-generated tag made people a little less likely to believe a fake.
    What it costs
    The label offered as a way out never saved one. Montana's law banned AI fakes of candidates in the 60 days before a vote unless labelled; in September a federal judge blocked it for the people who sued, in a preliminary ruling, and said a government-mandated label functions as an outright alteration of the speech itself. California's law was blocked last year for the people who sued, and satirists are who keep getting swept in.

    On the record: row 6 of the fact-check log, row 1 of the fact-check log, row 2 of the fact-check log, row 10 of the fact-check log, row 27 of the fact-check log

  3. 3Require a label only
    What it buys
    No ban, just disclosure, which is what most of the 31 states and the platforms do: Meta, Google, YouTube and TikTok require AI disclosure on political ads or realistic altered video. A plain AI-generated tag made people a little less likely to believe a fake, and labels that said manipulated, or false, did more.
    What it costs
    Labels are skipped even where they're required: 69% of the AI ads counted this cycle carried no AI disclaimer, and in a Wesleyan researcher's small-sample check, about 29% were labelled in states with an AI law and about 32% in states without one. A plain tag made no measurable difference to whether people would share a fake.
    Not measured
    Whether a label changes votes: the study measured what people said they'd believe and share about images, not how they voted.

    On the record: row 5 of the fact-check log, row 31 of the fact-check log, row 3 of the fact-check log, row 4 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 27 of the fact-check log

  4. 4Answer it with the laws we already have
    What it buys
    The worst case on record was answered by laws that weren't about AI at all: the phone industry traced the calls, the FCC fined the consultant $6 million under the law against faking caller ID (whether it was collected is unknown), the carrier settled for $1 million, and three voters won $7,500 each in federal court. The mockery and the attacks are left to an answer, and the judge kept Green's point that warnings make people doubt real video.
    What it costs
    What fell: the old laws caught New Hampshire after the damage, and the criminal case collapsed, with a jury acquitting the consultant on all 22 counts. A fake two days out can't be answered before the polls close. And the national referees are out of action: the FEC has had no quorum since May 2025, and the FCC's 2024 proposal for AI disclosure in broadcast political ads was never adopted.
    Not measured
    Which failure happens more, a law that gets struck or sweeps in a joke, or a correction that arrives after the vote: nobody has measured it.

    On the record: row 18 of the fact-check log, row 19 of the fact-check log, row 23 of the fact-check log, row 22 of the fact-check log, row 25 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pick: The one clear harm on record was caught by old laws. And there's no evidence a deepfake has ever swung an election, but nobody has been able to measure it.

What our fact-check caught

7 of the 32 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidEvery federal court that has reached one of these laws has blocked it.

    The record saysMinnesota's federal courts declined to block its law, on procedural grounds. The claim holds for the courts that weighed the laws against free speech: California, Hawaii and Montana.

  2. Draft saidA court upheld Minnesota's deepfake ban.

    The record saysAn appeals court only refused to block it early, over delay. Nobody has ruled on whether the law itself stands.

  3. Draft saidA federal judge blocked Montana's law.

    The record saysOnly for the people who sued. It still applies to everyone else, and the ruling is preliminary.

  4. Draft saidHawaii paid $118,000 in legal fees after its law was struck.

    The record saysHawaii agreed to pay about $118,000, pending approval of the money by its legislature.

  5. Draft saidAn AP analysis found AI ads were labelled no more often in states that require it.

    The record saysThe comparison was a Wesleyan researcher's check, quoted by the AP, on a small sample.
