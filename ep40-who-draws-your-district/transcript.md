## The research · Who drew your line, what a commission is, and how this cycle started
**Erin:** In most states, the people who drew it are your state's legislators: the same majority that will run in those districts, or beside them. In ten states a commission has the main say over congressional maps, and commission means very different things.
**Cole:** Different how?
**Erin:** California's has citizens with the final say. Several others are chosen by legislative leaders. Ohio's is a backup: the governor, the auditor and the secretary of state, plus four people appointed by legislative leaders. New York's only advises; the legislature can override it.
**Cole:** And if a map's unfair, I can sue?
**Erin:** Not in federal court. In twenty nineteen, the Supreme Court ruled five to four that partisan gerrymandering claims are beyond the reach of the federal courts, while saying that doesn't condone excessive gerrymandering. A state court can still apply its own constitution. And Congress has passed no rule.
**Cole:** So maps get drawn once a decade, after the census?
**Erin:** That's the usual rhythm. Since the summer of twenty twenty-five, ten states have changed their congressional maps in the middle of the decade, at rates the National Conference of State Legislatures says haven't been seen since the eighteen hundreds.
**Cole:** Who started it?
**Erin:** Texas moved first. On July seventh, twenty twenty-five, the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division wrote to Texas that four of its districts were unconstitutional coalition districts, a claim about race. Two days later, the governor added redistricting to a special session, citing the department's concerns. Texas's new map was signed at the end of August.
**Cole:** And then?
**Erin:** California answered. Its plan carried a legislative finding that it was in response to redistricting in Texas, and its voters approved it that November. Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio and others followed. Indiana's and Virginia's stated reasons also pointed at other states.
**Erin:** So the party in power usually holds the pen, the federal courts won't hear the complaint, and this cycle one state's move set off the next. Hold that.

## The research · Ledger two: the maps that stayed put, and what courts found

## The research · The stop: Missouri's two days, Virginia, and Utah's revocable pen
**Erin:** Missouri shows you. The legislature passed a new map in September twenty twenty-five. Opponents filed a referendum petition on December ninth: more than three hundred thousand signatures, two days before the map was due to take effect.
**Cole:** Two days?
**Erin:** Two days is the whole case. The Missouri Supreme Court ruled that because the petition came first, the new map never took effect. It's not the law, and has never been the law. So the twenty twenty-two districts were the ones for this November.
**Cole:** But the August primary ran on the new map?
**Erin:** It did. The secretary of state went ahead with the twenty twenty-five map for the primary. By the time the courts finished, the U.S. Supreme Court noted that ballots using the twenty twenty-two map had already been printed and mailed. So a Missouri voter picked a nominee in August in one district, and voted in November in a district with the same number and the old lines.
**Cole:** Wait. Same district number, different streets?
**Erin:** Same number, twenty twenty-two's lines. And the new map went on the ballot as Proposition A. On November third, Missouri voters decided it.
**Cole:** Is Missouri the only one?
**Erin:** Virginia's voters approved an amendment in April letting their legislature redraw. In May, the Virginia Supreme Court voided it, four to three. Not because the map was unfair. The amendment needed an election between the legislature's two votes, and early voting in that election had already begun before the first one.
**Cole:** So the calendar decided both.
**Erin:** In those two, yes. Other stops had other grounds. In Indiana and South Carolina, a vote in the legislature. In Texas and Alabama, the Supreme Court leaned first on the merits, with the calendar alongside.
**Cole:** And can a state lock the pen in advance?
**Erin:** Utah tried. In twenty eighteen its voters created an independent commission, by about seven thousand votes. In twenty twenty, the legislature repealed it and passed a law that dropped its own duty to vote on the commission's maps. A court voided that in twenty twenty-five, restored the voters' standards, and adopted a map the plaintiffs had drawn. Colorado voted this month on a lock of that kind: any off-cycle redraw would need its commission and its supreme court. And Maryland voted on its own rules for congressional maps.
**Erin:** So where a map was stopped, a date often did it, and every pen turned out to be revocable. Hold that.
