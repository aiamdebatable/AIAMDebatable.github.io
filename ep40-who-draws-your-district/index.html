Who Should Draw Your Voting District?

Who should draw your district: the party in power, or someone else?

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in November 2026, ten states had changed their congressional maps in the middle of the decade since the summer of 2025, most drawn by the party in power. In Missouri, a voter could pick a nominee in August on a new map, then vote in November on the 2022 lines.

Settled

What both sides accept

The federal courts won't hear a complaint that a map favours a party. This cycle's redraws lean one way: they were aimed at up to about 15 seats toward Republican candidates and about 6 toward Democratic ones, as targets on paper, not results. And every map has to be settled before the ballots go out.

What the shouting gets wrong

This cycle's redraws lean toward Republicans, while the maps that stayed put tilt both ways, and the court findings still standing are three against Republican-drawn maps and two against Democratic-drawn ones. Whoever holds the pen, the map that stands is the one nobody stopped in time: in Missouri, it took a petition filed two days before the map took effect. So the open dial has two settings, who holds the pen, and who can stop a map and how late.

Your dial: Who holds the pen, and who can stop a map, and how late?

The judge named one dial with two settings: who holds the pen, and who can stop a map. You'd pick one of each. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

Who holds the pen

  1. 1The legislature you can fire
    What it buys
    The pen stays with people voters can fire. Lawmakers answer to voters, and some listened: Indiana's Senate voted down a map its House had passed, 31 to 19. When California's legislature drew a map, its voters judged it and said yes, about 64%.
    What it costs
    The drawer chooses the voters, and where a majority held the pen, it used it, in both directions: courts found it in Maryland and New York, and in Pennsylvania, North Carolina and Ohio. Green's own debater conceded on air: the count this cycle leans one way, and her rule didn't stop it.

    On the record: row 46 of the fact-check log, row 36 of the fact-check log, row 61 of the fact-check log, row 154 of the fact-check log, row 156 of the fact-check log

  2. 2A commission, and which kind
    What it buys
    Takes the pen from the people who run in the districts. The evidence that commissions make races closer is real, but modest and contested: one study finds that moving to a commission raises a state's competitive share from about a quarter to about three-eighths, on the authors' measure. The kind matters: the same study finds commissions where parties keep some control, as in Ohio, do worse than one like Michigan's.
    What it costs
    A commission is only as independent as the people on it, and only as permanent as its voters allow. Somebody picks the commissioners, and that somebody is a pen too. California's voters set theirs aside, even though about 9 in 10 told pollsters they prefer a nonpartisan commission. Another study, of the 2010 maps, found no gain, and no study we found shows commissions deliver seats in proportion to votes.
    Not measured
    How lasting the gains are: the strongest study covers two rounds of maps and calls its own sample small.

    On the record: row 97 of the fact-check log, row 98 of the fact-check log, row 100 of the fact-check log, row 104 of the fact-check log, row 113 of the fact-check log

  3. 3A court
    What it buys
    A court can hold a legislature to rules the voters wrote. In Utah, a court voided the law the legislature used to repeal the commission its voters had created, restored the voters' standards, rejected the legislature's map and adopted another.
    What it costs
    A court map puts the pen in judges' hands, and nobody elected that choice: in Utah the court picked a map one side submitted. And court findings often don't last: of 13 findings since 2018 that a map was drawn to favour a party, only 5 still stand, and most of the rest fell when courts said these claims weren't theirs to judge.

    On the record: row 33 of the fact-check log, row 142 of the fact-check log, row 143 of the fact-check log, row 181 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 182 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected)

Who can stop a map, and how late

  1. 1Voters by petition before it takes effect
    What it buys
    In Missouri, opponents filed a referendum petition with more than 300,000 signatures two days before the new map was due to take effect. Because the petition came first, the state's supreme court ruled the map never took effect, so this November ran on the 2022 districts, and voters decided the map themselves as Proposition A.
    What it costs
    The veto has to arrive in time. Missouri's only worked because a petition beat the clock by two days, and the August primary still ran on the new map: a voter picked a nominee in one district, then voted in November in a district with the same number and the old lines. In most states, voters never got a say on the map.

    On the record: row 69 of the fact-check log, row 70 of the fact-check log, row 77 of the fact-check log, row 50 of the fact-check log, row 75 of the fact-check log

  2. 2A state court applying your state's rules
    What it buys
    Since 2019, a state court is the only one that can hear a complaint that a map favours a party, and it applies your state's own constitution. Findings still standing cover maps from both sides: Maryland's and New York's, and Pennsylvania's, Ohio's and Utah's.
    What it costs
    It rules on your state's rules, not on fairness. Virginia's Supreme Court voided an amendment its voters had approved, four to three, not because the map was unfair but because of when an election starts. And a state's own top court can close the door: North Carolina's later held these claims aren't for courts to judge, and overruled its own finding.
    Not measured
    Whether your state's constitution gives a court anything to apply: for Maryland, Illinois and Massachusetts, the research couldn't establish it, so no finding there doesn't mean a map is fair.

    On the record: row 2 of the fact-check log, row 39 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 167 of the fact-check log, row 180 of the fact-check log, row 182 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected)

  3. 3A lock written in advance
    What it buys
    Rules set before anyone's tempted beat rules written mid-fight; both teams accepted that. Colorado voted this month on a lock of that kind: any off-cycle redraw would need its commission and its supreme court. The Gold team's version adds a referendum within reach before a map takes effect, so the calendar works for voters, not against them.
    What it costs
    Every pen turned out to be revocable. Utah's voters created an independent commission in 2018 by about 7,000 votes; in 2020 the legislature repealed it, and it came back only through a lawsuit. California's commission lasted only until its voters set it aside.

    On the record: row 81 of the fact-check log, row 140 of the fact-check log, row 141 of the fact-check log, row 142 of the fact-check log, row 36 of the fact-check log

  4. 4Nobody until the next census
    What it buys
    No late challenge can change the map once voting starts, so nobody picks a nominee in August on one map and votes in November on another, as Missouri's voters did.
    What it costs
    The map that stands is the one nobody stopped in time, and the federal courts won't hear a partisan complaint. This cycle, ten states changed their maps in the middle of the decade, aimed at up to about 15 seats toward Republican candidates and about 6 toward Democratic ones: more seats than the House majority's edge.
    Not measured
    What the redrawn maps will actually deliver: the seat figures are targets on paper, not results, and the show made no forecast.

    On the record: row 1 of the fact-check log, row 55 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 60 of the fact-check log, row 62 of the fact-check log, row 50 of the fact-check log
