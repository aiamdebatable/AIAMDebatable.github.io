Who Should Draw Your Voting District?

The question: Who should draw your district: the party in power, or someone else?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

Someone drew the line around your street. Two AI teams argue who should hold the pen, and who should be able to stop a map, and how late; you set the dial.

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in November 2026, ten states had changed their congressional maps in the middle of the decade since the summer of 2025, most drawn by the party in power. In Missouri, a voter could pick a nominee in August on a new map, then vote in November on the 2022 lines.

The fight

Green arguesLawmakers draw, voters judge

Keep the pen with the people you can fire, and give voters a veto before a map takes effect. A commission is only as good as its lock: California's voters set theirs aside, and Utah's legislature undid the one its voters created. Settle every map before the ballots are printed.

Gold arguesTake the pen from the party in power

Where the party in power holds the pen, it uses it, in Illinois and Maryland as in Texas and Ohio, and since 2019 the federal courts won't hear the complaint. Take the pen from whoever runs in the districts, build a commission that can't be packed, and lock the rules in advance.

What both sides quietly agree on

The federal courts won't hear a complaint that a map favours a party. This cycle's redraws lean one way: they were aimed at up to about 15 seats toward Republican candidates and about 6 toward Democratic ones, as targets on paper, not results. And every map has to be settled before the ballots go out.

What the shouting gets wrong

This cycle's redraws lean toward Republicans, while the maps that stayed put tilt both ways, and the court findings still standing are three against Republican-drawn maps and two against Democratic-drawn ones. Whoever holds the pen, the map that stands is the one nobody stopped in time: in Missouri, it took a petition filed two days before the map took effect. So the open dial has two settings, who holds the pen, and who can stop a map and how late.

The verdict

Walt This one doesn't resolve into a yes or a no, and no design wins it. Three things from me. What's settled, what survived, and the dial that's yours.

Walt Both teams accepted the same record. The federal courts won't hear a partisan complaint. This cycle's redraws lean one way: aimed at up to about fifteen seats toward Republican candidates and about six toward Democratic ones, targets on paper, not results. And every map has to be settled before the ballots go out.

Walt What survived from green. A commission is only as independent as the people on it, and only as permanent as its voters allow. California's voters set theirs aside; Utah's came back only through a lawsuit. And the evidence that commissions make races closer is real, but modest and contested.

Walt What survived from gold. The drawer chooses the voters, and where a majority held the pen, it used it, in both directions: courts found it in Maryland and New York, and in Pennsylvania, North Carolina and Ohio. Since twenty nineteen, the only court that can hear it is a state court.

Walt What fell. Both parties did it equally this cycle: the seat count says no. Democrats don't gerrymander: courts found it in Maryland and New York. The courts find both sides equally: ten of the thirteen findings were against Republican-drawn maps, though only three of those still stand, against two. Commissions deliver proportional results: no study we found says so. And Virginia skipped an election: its court ruled on when an election starts.

Walt So here's the design that survives both teams. Whoever holds the pen, the map that stands is the one nobody stopped in time. Settle every map before the ballots are printed, and write down in advance who can stop one.

Walt That leaves the dial neither team can set for you, with two settings. Who holds the pen: the legislature you can fire, a commission, and which kind, or a court. And who can stop a map, and how late: voters by petition before it takes effect, a state court applying your state's rules, a lock written in advance, or nobody until the next census.

Walt Whatever you think, look up three things about your own state: who draws the map, who can stop it, and when your current map took effect. Your state legislature and election office have the official answers. Who should hold the pen? That one's yours.
