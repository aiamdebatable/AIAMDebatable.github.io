The research · Who drew your line, what a commission is, and how this cycle started

ErinIn most states, the people who drew it are your state's legislators: the same majority that will run in those districts, or beside them. In ten states a commission has the main say over congressional maps, and commission means very different things.

ColeDifferent how?

ErinCalifornia's has citizens with the final say. Several others are chosen by legislative leaders. Ohio's is a backup: the governor, the auditor and the secretary of state, plus four people appointed by legislative leaders. New York's only advises; the legislature can override it.

ColeAnd if a map's unfair, I can sue?

ErinNot in federal court. In twenty nineteen, the Supreme Court ruled five to four that partisan gerrymandering claims are beyond the reach of the federal courts, while saying that doesn't condone excessive gerrymandering. A state court can still apply its own constitution. And Congress has passed no rule.

ColeSo maps get drawn once a decade, after the census?

ErinThat's the usual rhythm. Since the summer of twenty twenty-five, ten states have changed their congressional maps in the middle of the decade, at rates the National Conference of State Legislatures says haven't been seen since the eighteen hundreds.

ColeWho started it?

ErinTexas moved first. On July seventh, twenty twenty-five, the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division wrote to Texas that four of its districts were unconstitutional coalition districts, a claim about race. Two days later, the governor added redistricting to a special session, citing the department's concerns. Texas's new map was signed at the end of August.

ColeAnd then?

ErinCalifornia answered. Its plan carried a legislative finding that it was in response to redistricting in Texas, and its voters approved it that November. Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio and others followed. Indiana's and Virginia's stated reasons also pointed at other states.

ErinSo the party in power usually holds the pen, the federal courts won't hear the complaint, and this cycle one state's move set off the next. Hold that.

The research · Ledger two: the maps that stayed put, and what courts found

ErinNow the other ledger.

ColeThe maps already in place?

ErinTake twenty twenty-four, from the Clerk of the House's own numbers. In Illinois, Democratic candidates won about fifty-three percent of the two-party House vote, and fourteen of seventeen seats. In Maryland, about sixty-five percent of the vote, and seven of eight seats. And in Texas and Ohio, on the maps they used before this cycle, Republican candidates won about two-thirds of the seats on about fifty-nine and fifty-seven percent of the vote.

ColeSo those are gerrymanders too?

ErinA gap alone doesn't prove it. The party that wins the vote usually gets a seat bonus in single-member districts, and where people live packs voters together. One study puts geography and the rules at about eight seats of bias nationally, against about two from gerrymandering. The strongest evidence is a court finding that a map was drawn to favour a party.

ColeHave courts found that?

ErinIn the record we could find, thirteen times since twenty eighteen. Ten were against maps drawn by Republican-led legislatures, in places like Pennsylvania, North Carolina and Ohio. Three were against maps drawn by Democratic-led ones: Maryland, twice, and New York. But most of the ten didn't last. Three fell when the Supreme Court closed the federal courts to these cases, and four more were undone later. Count only the findings still standing, and it's three against Republican-drawn maps, two against Democratic-drawn ones.

ColeAnd Illinois, with the biggest gap?

ErinNo court finding we could find. But since twenty nineteen, no federal court would hear it, so no finding doesn't mean fair. It can mean nobody sued, or nobody could.

ColeDo commissions fix it?

ErinModestly, and it's contested. One study finds that moving to a commission raises a state's competitive share from about a quarter to about three-eighths, on the authors' measure. Another, of the twenty-ten maps, found no gain. No study we found shows commissions deliver seats in proportion to votes. And polls showing majorities for commissions are from twenty twenty-five, or paid for by advocates. In California, about nine in ten voters told pollsters they prefer a nonpartisan commission, and a majority still voted to set theirs aside.

ErinSo the maps that stayed put tilt both ways, courts have found it on both sides, and commissions help less than people think. Hold that.

The research · The stop: Missouri's two days, Virginia, and Utah's revocable pen

ErinMissouri shows you. The legislature passed a new map in September twenty twenty-five. Opponents filed a referendum petition on December ninth: more than three hundred thousand signatures, two days before the map was due to take effect.

ColeTwo days?

ErinTwo days is the whole case. The Missouri Supreme Court ruled that because the petition came first, the new map never took effect. It's not the law, and has never been the law. So the twenty twenty-two districts were the ones for this November.

ColeBut the August primary ran on the new map?

ErinIt did. The secretary of state went ahead with the twenty twenty-five map for the primary. By the time the courts finished, the U.S. Supreme Court noted that ballots using the twenty twenty-two map had already been printed and mailed. So a Missouri voter picked a nominee in August in one district, and voted in November in a district with the same number and the old lines.

ColeWait. Same district number, different streets?

ErinSame number, twenty twenty-two's lines. And the new map went on the ballot as Proposition A. On November third, Missouri voters decided it.

ColeIs Missouri the only one?

ErinVirginia's voters approved an amendment in April letting their legislature redraw. In May, the Virginia Supreme Court voided it, four to three. Not because the map was unfair. The amendment needed an election between the legislature's two votes, and early voting in that election had already begun before the first one.

ColeSo the calendar decided both.

ErinIn those two, yes. Other stops had other grounds. In Indiana and South Carolina, a vote in the legislature. In Texas and Alabama, the Supreme Court leaned first on the merits, with the calendar alongside.

ColeAnd can a state lock the pen in advance?

ErinUtah tried. In twenty eighteen its voters created an independent commission, by about seven thousand votes. In twenty twenty, the legislature repealed it and passed a law that dropped its own duty to vote on the commission's maps. A court voided that in twenty twenty-five, restored the voters' standards, and adopted a map the plaintiffs had drawn. Colorado voted this month on a lock of that kind: any off-cycle redraw would need its commission and its supreme court. And Maryland voted on its own rules for congressional maps.

ErinSo where a map was stopped, a date often did it, and every pen turned out to be revocable. Hold that.

LenaSo keep the pen with the people you can fire, give voters a veto before a map takes effect, and settle every map before the ballots are printed. Accountable, and stoppable in time.

The verdict

WaltThis one doesn't resolve into a yes or a no, and no design wins it. Three things from me. What's settled, what survived, and the dial that's yours.

WaltBoth teams accepted the same record. The federal courts won't hear a partisan complaint. This cycle's redraws lean one way: aimed at up to about fifteen seats toward Republican candidates and about six toward Democratic ones, targets on paper, not results. And every map has to be settled before the ballots go out.

WaltWhat survived from green. A commission is only as independent as the people on it, and only as permanent as its voters allow. California's voters set theirs aside; Utah's came back only through a lawsuit. And the evidence that commissions make races closer is real, but modest and contested.

WaltWhat survived from gold. The drawer chooses the voters, and where a majority held the pen, it used it, in both directions: courts found it in Maryland and New York, and in Pennsylvania, North Carolina and Ohio. Since twenty nineteen, the only court that can hear it is a state court.

WaltWhat fell. Both parties did it equally this cycle: the seat count says no. Democrats don't gerrymander: courts found it in Maryland and New York. The courts find both sides equally: ten of the thirteen findings were against Republican-drawn maps, though only three of those still stand, against two. Commissions deliver proportional results: no study we found says so. And Virginia skipped an election: its court ruled on when an election starts.

WaltSo here's the design that survives both teams. Whoever holds the pen, the map that stands is the one nobody stopped in time. Settle every map before the ballots are printed, and write down in advance who can stop one.

WaltThat leaves the dial neither team can set for you, with two settings. Who holds the pen: the legislature you can fire, a commission, and which kind, or a court. And who can stop a map, and how late: voters by petition before it takes effect, a state court applying your state's rules, a lock written in advance, or nobody until the next census.

WaltWhatever you think, look up three things about your own state: who draws the map, who can stop it, and when your current map took effect. Your state legislature and election office have the official answers. Who should hold the pen? That one's yours.
