## The pitch, in one line

**The fight is shouted as "who holds the pen", but in 2025–26 what decided which maps voters actually got
was who could stop a map, and whether they got there before the ballots were printed.** Legislatures drew
most of the new maps, and the count leans hard one way; a commission turned out to mean anything from
citizens to three statewide elected officials and four appointees of legislative leaders; a court picked a plaintiff's map. Then a petition filed two days
before a map took effect, an early vote that had already begun, and ballots already in the mail settled
more than any of that. So the real setting is **who holds the pen, and who can stop it, and when**.

## The viewer's moment

One street: **yours**. (Invented and unnamed; never a real address or person.)

Somebody drew a line around your street. It decides which House race is on your ballot, which neighbours
are in your district and which are not, and how close that race is likely to be. Most people have never
asked who drew it, or who could have stopped it.

- **In most states, the people who drew it are the state's legislators**, the same party majority that
  will run in those districts or alongside them. In ten states a commission holds primary responsibility
  for congressional maps, and "commission" covers very different bodies (log 63).
- **This decade, the line around your street may have been drawn twice.** Since summer 2025, NCSL counts
  ten states that changed their congressional maps mid-decade (log 55). One more (Virginia) tried and
  was stopped by its own Supreme Court (log 39–40).
- **And in one state, the same voter got both maps in one year.** In Missouri, the August primary ran on
  the legislature's 2025 map; the November general runs on the 2022 map, because a voter petition reached
  the state before the new map took effect, and the U.S. Supreme Court noted that ballots on the 2022 map
  "have been printed and mailed" (log 50, 75). The district number stayed; its shape changed (secondary,
  NCSL; log 50).

The episode follows one line, around one street, through the three places a map can be drawn and the
three ways it can be stopped, and ends on what the viewer can look up about their own.

## The question

**Who should draw your district: the party in power, or someone else?** And because the record says the
answer depends on it, the second half: **who should be able to stop the map, and how late?**

## Why now

**The structural peg (true on any posting date):** since summer 2025 states have changed congressional
maps mid-decade "at rates not seen since the 1800s" (NCSL, secondary; log 55), and the 2025–26 record now
contains every setting of the dial: legislatures (Texas, North Carolina, Florida, Tennessee, Louisiana),
a backup commission of elected officials and leaders' appointees (Ohio), voters suspending their own commission (California), a court
choosing a plaintiff's map (Utah), a voter petition (Missouri), and a state court voiding a voter-approved
amendment on timing (Virginia).

**The hard peg: three statewide votes on Tuesday 2026-11-03.**

  act of the General Assembly entitled 'House Bill No. 1 (2025 Second Extraordinary Session),' which

## Settled (not the debate)

**The federal floor.**
- Federal courts will not hear partisan-gerrymandering claims: *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019-06-27, 5–4)
  held they "present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts", while adding "Our
  conclusion does not condone excessive partisan gerrymandering" and pointing to the states and Congress
  (log 1).
- State courts may apply their own constitutions to congressional maps, within "the ordinary bounds of
  judicial review": *Moore v. Harper* (2023-06-27, 6–3) (log 2).
- Congress has enacted no ban. The Redistricting Reform Act (S. 2885 / H.R. 5449) sits in committee;
  H.R. 4889 has a discharge motion filed (log 3).
- *Louisiana v. Callais* (2026-04-29, 6–3) is in this episode only as the trigger for the May 2026 redraws
  (log 4).

**Utah, the pen that proved revocable both ways** (log 139–143; the court-pen beat):
- Voters chose a commission: **Proposition 4** passed in November 2018, creating the Utah Independent
  Redistricting Commission and standards for maps, **512,218 to 505,274 (50.3 %)**, a margin of 6,944 votes
  (Lieutenant Governor's canvass; the percentage is the show's arithmetic; log 139–140).
- The legislature undid it: on **2020-03-11** it voted to repeal Proposition 4 and enacted **S.B. 200**
  (signed 2020-03-28), which dropped the legislature's duty to vote up or down on the commission's maps and
  made the gerrymandering ban "binding only on the Commission" (log 141).
- A court restored it: after the Utah Supreme Court held the people's initiative right protected (2024 UT
  21), the district court ruled on **2025-08-25** that S.B. 200 was "unconstitutional and void" and enjoined
  it and the 2021 map; on **2025-11-10** it found the legislature's Map C "does not comply with Proposition 4"
  and adopted **Map 1** (log 142–143, 33). Map 1 governs 2026 (log 34).
- What it shows: a commission the voters created was undone by the legislature, and only litigation brought
  its rules back. Every pen here was revocable, in both directions.

**Virginia, the other clearest timing case.** In *Scott v. McDougle* (Record No. 260127, 2026-05-08, 4–3,
opinion by Justice Kelsey), the Supreme Court of Virginia held that the legislature's process "violated
Article XII, Section 1" and that this "incurably taints the resulting referendum vote and nullifies its
legal efficacy." The first legislative passage (2025-10-31) came after early voting had begun ("over 1.3
million votes had been cast in the general election"), so the November 2025 election did not count as an
"intervening" one. It is a ruling on **when an election starts**, not a finding that anything was
skipped (log 39–40). Voters had approved the amendment on 04-21-2026, 1,604,276 yes to 1,499,393 no (51.7 %,
the court-recited **unofficial** count; it was never certified) (log 38). The court called the existing map
"nonpartisan" (6–5) and the replacement's "expected districts divided 10-1"; both phrases are the court's
(log 40). The U.S. Supreme Court denied a stay on 05-15 (log 41).

**What a "commission" is** (NCSL, which "does not use the phrase 'independent commissions'"):
- **Primary responsibility, 10 states:** Arizona, California, Colorado, Hawaii, Idaho, Michigan, Montana,
  New Jersey, Virginia, Washington. Several are appointed by legislative leaders (log 63).
- **Backup, 3:** Connecticut, Indiana, Ohio. **Advisory**, including New York, which is "commission-first,

- California's Prop 50 was a legislative constitutional amendment; it put the Legislature's August 2025

- Utah's 2026 map was **adopted by a state district court from a plaintiffs' submission** ("The Court

## Contested (the real fight)

**GREEN — "Elected lawmakers should hold the pen; voters judge them at the ballot box."**
- **Legislators can be fired; commissioners can't.** A legislature that draws an unfair map answers for it
  at the next election. A commission member answers to nobody on the ballot.
- **"Independent" isn't neutral.** Ohio's "commission" is three statewide elected officials and four appointees of legislative leaders, who adopted a map
  whose published target leans toward the majority party (log 17–19); APSR finds commissions "where parties
  retain some control (Ohio)" less effective than Michigan's design (log 98); PNAS: "commission-drawn maps
  are not free of bias, as shown by pro-Democratic bias in Michigan and the pro-Republican biases found for
  Iowa" (log 103). Who picks the commissioners is itself a pen.
- **A court map puts the pen in judges' hands.** Utah's map was chosen by a trial court from the
  plaintiffs' own submissions (log 33); Virginia's in-force map was issued by its Supreme Court (log 40).
  Nobody elected those choices.
- **Unilateral disarmament, and voters themselves acted on it.** When maps are being redrawn for one side
  elsewhere, a state that keeps its commission gives seats away. California said so in its own text: its
  amendment carries "a legislative finding that it is in response to redistricting in Texas in 2025", and
  Texas had moved first (log 121–122); Indiana's and Virginia's stated grounds also name other states (log
  128–129). Voters were asked and said yes: California
  64.4 % for Prop 50 (log 36); Virginia 51.7 % unofficial for its amendment (log 38). In AP VoteCast, about
  9 in 10 California voters said lines should generally be drawn by a nonpartisan commission, "But a
  majority nevertheless backed Proposition 50" (log 113).

## The reframe

The shouting is about **who holds the pen**, and it is fought as if picking the right drawer were the whole
answer. The record splits it in two.

1. **The pen matters, and the 2025–26 count says so.** Where a legislature's majority drew, the published
   targets pointed toward that majority's party, and most of those maps lean Republican (log 61).
2. **But where a map was stopped, a date often did it.** Missouri's map was halted by a petition filed two
   days before its effective date; Virginia's amendment by early voting that had already begun; Missouri's
   2022 map held because ballots had been printed. Neither stop turned on whether a map was fair. (Other
   stops had other grounds: likely merits in Texas and Alabama, a chamber vote in Indiana and South Carolina.)
3. **Every pen proved revocable.** California's commission was set aside by its own voters; Ohio's
   "commission" is elected officials and leaders' appointees; Utah's voters chose a commission, its
   legislature undid it, and a court restored it; Virginia's voters said
   yes and the court said the process came too late. A commission without a lock on when it can be
   overridden is a preference, not a rule.
4. **Where the reframe does not hold:** in states with no in-state stop (North Carolina, Texas for
   partisan claims), the pen is the whole story. And geography, not the pen, sets much of the floor on
   competitiveness (PNAS: ~8 seats from geography and rules nationally vs ~2 from gerrymandering). So the
   episode says "the stop did more work in 2025–26", never "the pen doesn't matter".

## Both sides quietly agree

**Nobody in the record defends the gerrymander as a principle.** The Supreme Court majority that closed the
federal courts to partisan claims wrote that its conclusion "does not condone excessive partisan
gerrymandering"; four justices dissented (log 1). Majorities in both parties, in states
run by either party, say in the abstract they prefer a nonpartisan drawer (NBC 2025, adults, forced choice;
log 107), and California's voters said the same thing while voting to suspend their commission (log 113).
Each side defends its own map as a response to the other's.

**Every court in the record treated the ballot calendar as the hard line.** Missouri's Supreme Court put the
2022 map back for November; the U.S. Supreme Court, with no noted dissent, kept it there because ballots
were "printed and mailed"; Virginia's court measured the process against early voting already under way;
Texas and Alabama's stays cited the eve of an election. They disagree about almost everything else.
**A map needs a fixed point before ballots go out**, and Missouri's two-map election shows what happens
without one.

## Make it Matter — the line around your street

- **The cold open (premise before the object):** *"Who drew the line around your street, the one that
  decides which House race is on your ballot? This year, the answer was often the party in power. Today:
  should it be them, or someone else, and who should be able to stop them? Two teams argue it, the desk
  checks both, and you set the dial."* Feeling over figures; ≤ ~100 words.
- **The story, ep21-style: one line, followed.** A street on a cloud map → the legislature's chamber,
  where a majority passes a map → the commission room (and the question: who is on it?) → the courtroom,
  where a judge picks from submitted maps → the calendar on the wall: an effective date, a petition two
  days ahead, the first day of early voting, a stack of printed ballots → the dial.
- **The one voter with two maps** (invented and unnamed; never a real person): a Missouri voter who in
  August picked her party's House nominee in a district drawn in 2025, and in November finds the same
  district number on her ballot with the 2022 shape, and Proposition A on the same ballot. The ringmaster
  asks what the viewer is thinking: "Wait, the same district number, different streets?" — "Same number,
  2022's lines." *Hold that.*
- **The fear against the record**, by role, no candidates: "they're rigging the House" vs: maps drawn to
  shift up to about 15 seats toward one party and 6 toward the other, against a 3-seat majority edge, and
  one forecaster's average that expects the redrawn states to net out much smaller (log 58, 60, 62).
  "Commissions are the fix" vs: real but modest gains, contested, and the one "commission" that drew a
  2025 map is three statewide elected officials and four appointees of legislative leaders (log 17, 97–103).
- **The pictures:** a line drawn across one street; a scoreboard in the square ("drawn to shift: 15 / 6 —
  the majority's edge: 3"), with "targets, not results" printed under it; a calendar with Dec 9 and Dec 11
  circled; a ballot box beside a printing press; a cloud-map rise for the state-by-state count; the
  commission room with seven nameplates, three reading "elected" and four "appointed by party leaders"; the
  courtroom with a stack of submitted maps.
- **What the viewer can do with their own district** (information, never instruction; every line points to
  an official source):
  1. **Look up who draws your state's congressional map**: the legislature, a commission (and which kind),
     or, after litigation, a court. NCSL keeps the list; your state legislature's site has the law.
  2. **Look up who can stop it**: does your state let voters petition to refer a law before it takes
     effect? Does your state constitution set districting rules a state court can apply? Is there a
     rule about changing maps mid-decade?
  3. **Look up when your current map took effect, and until when it lasts.**
  4. **If your state has a measure about this on your ballot, read its official wording** at your state's
     election office. (⛔ The show names the three 11-03 measures only as news, never as advice.)

## Script traps

**Pre-vote (every line here reads as campaigning if posted before 11-03; for a post-vote slot, rewrite
with the results after the 11-04 re-hunt):**
- ⛔ **No position on Proposition A, Amendment 86 or Question 3, ever**, in script, description, Short or
  community post. Say so once, plainly, on air.
- **State yes/no in the official words only.** Missouri: "yes" approves the legislature's 2025 map; "no"
  keeps 2022's. The polarity is the reverse of what many voters assume; say it neutrally, never "a yes
  vote kills the map".
- ⛔ **Never "2028" for a Missouri yes.** No primary says which election it first affects (log 68). Say
  "the next congressional election after it takes effect".
- **"Voters are confused"**: only as the pollster's own words, about the measure ("indicates confusion
  among voters over this ballot measure"), with SLU/YouGov named and no numbers (log 111). ⛔ Never the
  horse-race figure (SLU/YouGov fielded Aug 13–24), pre- or post-vote.
- **Colorado:** Amendment 86 covers any off-cycle redraw, by either side; ⛔ never "aimed at Democrats" or
  "a conservative group's measure"; the sponsor is named only by a secondary, so omit it (log 87). It is
  "Amendment 86", not "Initiative 256", on air.
- **Maryland:** the court-substituted ballot wording, verbatim or in its own verbs ("clarifying …
  authorizing … providing"). ⛔ Never "removes" or "strips" (that is the dissent's framing; log 90). ⛔
  Never "it redraws" or "it lets Maryland redraw in 2028" (log 92).
- **The dial must not map onto a ballot.** Setting 3 ("a lock written in advance") cites Amendment 86 as an
  example of the kind; the judge never ranks setting 3.
- **No House forecast**, in any form, before or after the vote.
- Louisiana's 11-03 contest is an **open primary** with a 12-12 second round; Louisiana's House races are
  not decided on 11-03 (log 28).

**The count:**
- **Always "drawn to shift" / "targets on paper", never "seats moved", "gained" or "flipped".** Say
  "toward Republican candidates", never "Republicans redrew" as the subject.
- **The one tally:** "up to about 15 toward Republican candidates and about 6 toward Democratic ones",
  with "at the low ends, 12 and 5" available on screen. Name what each is. ⛔ Not NBC's June "16 vs 6"
  (still counted Missouri; log 57); not "13 vs 5, net 8" (an arithmetic error; log 56); not Cook's "around
  four" (unread; log 59).
- **The comparator is the House majority:** "3 seats more than everyone else combined, as of 10-19" (log
  62). Same unit (House seats). ⛔ Never set it against the targets as a prediction.
- ⛔ **No state ratio ("7 to 1") on air** (council 10-19): the enactments are Texas, North Carolina, Ohio, Florida,
  Tennessee, Louisiana in force, Missouri's blocked; California. Say the seat count instead. ⛔ Alabama is **not** "court-restored": it is Alabama's own 2023
  legislative plan, which the Supreme Court let back into use (log 30). Utah is **not** a Republican
  redraw (log 55).
- **Seats vs votes is the show's arithmetic**, labelled so on screen, with its caveats (log 144–151). ⛔ Not
  "Illinois 14–3 on about 44 %" (a viewer comment's figure): the Clerk's 2024 statistics give about 53 % of
  the two-party House vote. ⛔ Never "proof of a gerrymander". ⛔ Massachusetts only with "Republicans ran in
  2 of 9 districts", or not at all.
- **The sequence:** Texas first (2025-07-09), on the Justice Department's 2025-07-07 letter; California's
  first formal move 2025-08-18. ⛔ Never date Louisiana's move to February (log 134); ⛔ never list Alabama
  as a 2025–26 move (log 138); Ohio is "on its constitution's clock", never "chose to redraw" (log 124). No
  stated ground for North Carolina, Louisiana or South Carolina (log 127, 135, 137).
- **NCSL counts 10 states that changed maps**, not 11. Virginia is an attempt that was voided (log 55).
- Ohio's "governing party" is the majority party in a commission vote that both Democratic members joined;
  North Carolina's map came from a legislature whose governor is of the other party and has no veto. Say
  "the legislative majority's party" when precision matters.

**The court findings (log 152–182):**
- ⛔ **Both counts or neither**: "3 against 2 still standing" only with "13 findings in all, 10 against maps drawn
  by Republican-led legislatures"; "10 against 3" only with "most of the ten did not stand, three of them federal
  findings undone after *Rucho*". ⛔ Never "most of the ten came in the federal wave" (three did; log 182).
- **Units:** findings, not maps (13 findings on 12 maps in 8 states). "The findings we found", never "the only
  ones". Utah's place on the Republican-led side is APPROXIMATE until a roster is read (log 175).
- **A seat–vote gap is never called a gerrymander**; a court finding is named with its court and year.
- **Absences:** "no court finding we could find", bounded by case lists, never "no court has ever"; and never
  "no finding, so it's fair": since 2019 federal courts don't hear these claims (log 176–180).
- **New York:** the 2022 replacement was drawn by a court-appointed special master, certified by the Steuben
  County trial court; ⛔ never "the Court of Appeals drew it" or "the legislature redrew" (log 157). Nothing about
  the 2024 map (log 158).
- **Ohio:** ⛔ never "the court replaced Ohio's map": no court replaced the March 2 plan (log 161).
- **Maryland:** "found an extreme gerrymander" is the 2021 plan; the current map (2022) has never been ruled on.
  ⛔ Never say the current map was found (log 155, 178).
- **Judges by court or role**; no governor, legislator or expert is named from these opinions.

**The pen:**
- **Ohio:** "a backup commission" of three statewide elected officials and four appointees of legislative leaders (Ohio Const. Art. XI §1(A)), **Art. XIX §1(E)**, 7–0, lasting
  **until 2031** (2026, 2028, 2030). ⛔ Never "independent commission", never §1(B), never "12-3" as a
  result (log 15–17). The referendum-threat motive is attributed journalism (log 20).
- **California:** "set aside" or "suspended for congressional maps through 2030", ⛔ never "abolished";
  a legislative constitutional amendment, not a citizen initiative; 64.4 % (log 36).
- **Utah:** a court **adopting a plaintiffs' map**, not "a judge drew it" (log 33). Proposition 4: "50.3 %"
  or "about 7,000 votes" on air, 512,218 to 505,274 on screen (log 140). The court voided the 2020 repeal
  (S.B. 200) and found Map C "does not comply with Proposition 4" (log 142–143): say that, not "the court
  found the map unfair".
- **Tennessee:** an ordinary bill removed a **statutory** bar; ⛔ never "amended the constitution" (log 24).
- **New York:** "commission-first, the legislature can override"; NCSL files it as advisory (log 64).
- **NCSL doesn't use "independent commission".** If the script uses the phrase, it names which body.
- **Florida's plan name** marks it as the governor's office plan; that is an inference plus secondary
  press (log 21). Not needed on air; the pen is the Legislature.

**The stop and the timing:**
- **Virginia:** a 4–3 ruling on **when an election starts** (first passage came after early voting began).
  ⛔ Never "skipped an election". ⛔ Never "certified": 51.7 % is the court-recited unofficial count (log
  38–39). "Nonpartisan" (6–5) and "expected 10-1" are the court's words; attribute both.
- **Purcell:** the **Texas 12-04-2025 stay did not name *Purcell*** (it rested on the merits and the
  eve-of-election rule); **NY-11 was a stay of a state trial court's remedy order, with no reasons**.
  Missouri's 26A388 names *Purcell*. Alabama 2026 used the eve-of-election rule plus the merits under
  *Callais* (log 8, 32, 44, 93). ⛔ Never "the Court used Purcell to save Texas's map".
- **Texas:** the 04-27-2026 summary reversal reversed a preliminary injunction on a racial-gerrymander
  finding; ⛔ never "upheld the map as constitutional" (log 9).
- **Missouri:** the petition was filed **before** the effective date; that ordering is the whole legal
  point (log 70). The Secretary of State is a role, never a name. "Missouri put a congressional plan to
  referendum in 1922" only as the court citing that history; ⛔ never "first since 1922" (log 72).
- **Missouri's footnote 7** is about **state legislative** maps (exempt from referendum when commission-drawn),
  not congressional ones; say it exactly.
- **Callais/VRA:** only as the trigger for the May 2026 redraws. ⛔ No discussion of the VRA question.

**Commissions evidence:**
- **APSR's +13 points is a fractional, model-based competitive share**, "from 25% to 38% on average". ⛔
  Never "13 % more close races" or "13 more competitive districts". Say "from about a quarter to about
  three-eighths, on the authors' measure" (log 97).
- **APSR and PNAS come from the same research group**; ⛔ never present them as independent confirmation
  (log 99).
- **The Journal of Politics finding airs as a finding only**, no percentages, until its published text is
  read (log 100).
- ⛔ **Never "commissions produce proportional results"** (log 104).
- **New America is a think-tank synthesis**, not peer-reviewed; say so if quoted (log 102).

**Polling:**
- **Flag the sponsor and the year every time.** Common Cause/Noble is advocacy-commissioned (2025);
  NBC/SurveyMonkey is adults, a forced two-way choice, 2025; Change Research is self-published (2026), its
  wording paraphrased; The Center Square/Noble is opt-in with no margin of error, seen via a reprint.
- ⛔ **Not "a large majority of Americans want commissions"** as a neutral finding: no neutral 2026
  national poll was found (log 111).
- ⛔ **The Politico poll** (partisans backing their own side's map) was seen only through an opinion
  column: off air (log 108).
- **The honest finding:** stated preference vs actual votes (AP VoteCast: 9 in 10 vs a majority for Prop
  50; log 113).

**Naming:**
- **Process, not parties, as the subject.** Name states and instruments ("Texas's HB 4", "Ohio's
  commission plan"), never "X's map" for a politician.
- **Role by default; a name only for a dated official act** (a court's ruling, an opinion's author, a
  concurrence). No governor, legislator, commissioner or candidate is named.
