The pitch, in one line

The fight is shouted as "who holds the pen", but in 2025–26 what decided which maps voters actually got was who could stop a map, and whether they got there before the ballots were printed. Legislatures drew most of the new maps, and the count leans hard one way; a commission turned out to mean anything from citizens to three statewide elected officials and four appointees of legislative leaders; a court picked a plaintiff's map. Then a petition filed two days before a map took effect, an early vote that had already begun, and ballots already in the mail settled more than any of that. So the real setting is who holds the pen, and who can stop it, and when.

The viewer's moment

One street: yours. (Invented and unnamed; never a real address or person.)

Somebody drew a line around your street. It decides which House race is on your ballot, which neighbours are in your district and which are not, and how close that race is likely to be. Most people have never asked who drew it, or who could have stopped it.

  • In most states, the people who drew it are the state's legislators, the same party majority that will run in those districts or alongside them. In ten states a commission holds primary responsibility for congressional maps, and "commission" covers very different bodies (log 63).
  • This decade, the line around your street may have been drawn twice. Since summer 2025, NCSL counts ten states that changed their congressional maps mid-decade (log 55). One more (Virginia) tried and was stopped by its own Supreme Court (log 39–40).
  • And in one state, the same voter got both maps in one year. In Missouri, the August primary ran on the legislature's 2025 map; the November general runs on the 2022 map, because a voter petition reached the state before the new map took effect, and the U.S. Supreme Court noted that ballots on the 2022 map "have been printed and mailed" (log 50, 75). The district number stayed; its shape changed (secondary, NCSL; log 50).

The episode follows one line, around one street, through the three places a map can be drawn and the three ways it can be stopped, and ends on what the viewer can look up about their own.

The question

Who should draw your district: the party in power, or someone else? And because the record says the answer depends on it, the second half: who should be able to stop the map, and how late?

Why now

The structural peg (true on any posting date): since summer 2025 states have changed congressional maps mid-decade "at rates not seen since the 1800s" (NCSL, secondary; log 55), and the 2025–26 record now contains every setting of the dial: legislatures (Texas, North Carolina, Florida, Tennessee, Louisiana), a backup commission of elected officials and leaders' appointees (Ohio), voters suspending their own commission (California), a court choosing a plaintiff's map (Utah), a voter petition (Missouri), and a state court voiding a voter-approved amendment on timing (Virginia).

The hard peg: three statewide votes on Tuesday 2026-11-03.

  • Missouri, Proposition A. Official ballot title: "Do the people of the state of Missouri approve the act of the General Assembly entitled 'House Bill No. 1 (2025 Second Extraordinary Session),' which repeals Missouri's existing congressional plan, and replaces it with new congressional boundaries that keep more counties intact?" Settled (not the debate)

The federal floor.

  • Federal courts will not hear partisan-gerrymandering claims: Rucho v. Common Cause (2019-06-27, 5–4) held they "present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts", while adding "Our conclusion does not condone excessive partisan gerrymandering" and pointing to the states and Congress (log 1).
  • State courts may apply their own constitutions to congressional maps, within "the ordinary bounds of judicial review": Moore v. Harper (2023-06-27, 6–3) (log 2).
  • Congress has enacted no ban. The Redistricting Reform Act (S. 2885 / H.R. 5449) sits in committee; H.R. 4889 has a discharge motion filed (log 3).
  • Louisiana v. Callais (2026-04-29, 6–3) is in this episode only as the trigger for the May 2026 redraws (log 4).

Utah, the pen that proved revocable both ways (log 139–143; the court-pen beat):

  • Voters chose a commission: Proposition 4 passed in November 2018, creating the Utah Independent Redistricting Commission and standards for maps, 512,218 to 505,274 (50.3 %), a margin of 6,944 votes (Lieutenant Governor's canvass; the percentage is the show's arithmetic; log 139–140).
  • The legislature undid it: on 2020-03-11 it voted to repeal Proposition 4 and enacted S.B. 200 (signed 2020-03-28), which dropped the legislature's duty to vote up or down on the commission's maps and made the gerrymandering ban "binding only on the Commission" (log 141).
  • A court restored it: after the Utah Supreme Court held the people's initiative right protected (2024 UT 21), the district court ruled on 2025-08-25 that S.B. 200 was "unconstitutional and void" and enjoined it and the 2021 map; on 2025-11-10 it found the legislature's Map C "does not comply with Proposition 4" and adopted Map 1 (log 142–143, 33). Map 1 governs 2026 (log 34).
  • What it shows: a commission the voters created was undone by the legislature, and only litigation brought its rules back. Every pen here was revocable, in both directions.

Missouri, the clearest timing case.

  • HB 1 passed 2025-09-12; opponents filed 691 boxes holding more than 300,000 signatures on Dec 9, two days before HB 1's Dec 11 effective date; the Secretary of State certified the petition insufficient at about 4 p.m. on primary day, Aug 4 (log 77).

In one state's text, the voters' veto reaches the legislature's pen and not the commission's. This is the dial in one state's constitution.

Contested (the real fight)

GREEN — "Elected lawmakers should hold the pen; voters judge them at the ballot box."

  • Legislators can be fired; commissioners can't. A legislature that draws an unfair map answers for it at the next election. A commission member answers to nobody on the ballot.
  • "Independent" isn't neutral. Ohio's "commission" is three statewide elected officials and four appointees of legislative leaders, who adopted a map whose published target leans toward the majority party (log 17–19); APSR finds commissions "where parties retain some control (Ohio)" less effective than Michigan's design (log 98); PNAS: "commission-drawn maps are not free of bias, as shown by pro-Democratic bias in Michigan and the pro-Republican biases found for Iowa" (log 103). Who picks the commissioners is itself a pen.
  • A court map puts the pen in judges' hands. Utah's map was chosen by a trial court from the plaintiffs' own submissions (log 33); Virginia's in-force map was issued by its Supreme Court (log 40). Nobody elected those choices.
  • Unilateral disarmament, and voters themselves acted on it. When maps are being redrawn for one side elsewhere, a state that keeps its commission gives seats away. California said so in its own text: its amendment carries "a legislative finding that it is in response to redistricting in Texas in 2025", and Texas had moved first (log 121–122); Indiana's and Virginia's stated grounds also name other states (log 128–129). Voters were asked and said yes: California 64.4 % for Prop 50 (log 36); Virginia 51.7 % unofficial for its amendment (log 38). In AP VoteCast, about 9 in 10 California voters said lines should generally be drawn by a nonpartisan commission, "But a majority nevertheless backed Proposition 50" (log 113).
  • The commission evidence is thin. Gerrymandering nets about 2 seats nationally while geography and rules account for about 8 (PNAS, log 103); "Even in states that use independent commissions, barely one-in-ten districts are two-party competitive" (New America, log 102); a Journal of Politics study of 2010-cycle maps found commissions no more competitive (APPROXIMATE, finding only; log 100).

GOLD — "Someone other than the party in power should hold the pen."

  • The person drawing chooses their voters. A Supreme Court concurrence in the Texas case called the map's impetus "partisan advantage pure and simple" (Justice Alito, 2025-12-04; log 7). The federal courts will not hear that complaint (log 1).
  • Texas moved first, on a federal letter. The Governor added redistricting on 2025-07-09 "in light of … constitutional concerns raised by the U.S. Department of Justice", two days after the Civil Rights Division's letter; his June call had left it out (log 115, 121). The letter's own ground was race, not party (log 117), and the opinion reproducing it was stayed and reversed (log 119): Gold uses the sequence, not the panel's characterisations.
  • The count. Maps in force for November 2026 were drawn to shift up to about 15 seats toward Republican candidates and about 6 toward Democratic ones (log 60), in a House where the majority's edge over everyone else is 3 (log 62). Most of the enacted redraws lean one way (log 61).
  • Missouri voters nominated in August on one map and vote in November on another. Ballots on the 2022 map "have been printed and mailed" while the nominees were chosen on the 2025 map (log 50, 75). That is what a mid-decade fight looks like from a kitchen table.
  • Commissions can work when built right. APSR (2026): moving from a legislature to a commission raises a state's competitive share "from 25% to 38% on average", on the authors' fractional measure (log 97); Nelson (PS, 2023, 1982–2018 district data): commissions "2.25 times more likely to have competitive elections" (log 101).
  • Voters say so, across parties. NBC/SurveyMonkey (2025, adults, a forced choice): 82 % prefer nonpartisan commissions, including 71 % of Republicans in Republican-controlled states and 88 % of Democrats in Democratic-controlled states (log 107). Change Research (Aug 2026, self-published): 55 % support vs 20 % oppose commissions in every state (log 109).

The reframe

The shouting is about who holds the pen, and it is fought as if picking the right drawer were the whole answer. The record splits it in two.

  1. The pen matters, and the 2025–26 count says so. Where a legislature's majority drew, the published targets pointed toward that majority's party, and most of those maps lean Republican (log 61).
  2. But where a map was stopped, a date often did it. Missouri's map was halted by a petition filed two days before its effective date; Virginia's amendment by early voting that had already begun; Missouri's 2022 map held because ballots had been printed. Neither stop turned on whether a map was fair. (Other stops had other grounds: likely merits in Texas and Alabama, a chamber vote in Indiana and South Carolina.)
  3. Every pen proved revocable. California's commission was set aside by its own voters; Ohio's "commission" is elected officials and leaders' appointees; Utah's voters chose a commission, its legislature undid it, and a court restored it; Virginia's voters said yes and the court said the process came too late. A commission without a lock on when it can be overridden is a preference, not a rule.
  4. Where the reframe does not hold: in states with no in-state stop (North Carolina, Texas for partisan claims), the pen is the whole story. And geography, not the pen, sets much of the floor on competitiveness (PNAS: ~8 seats from geography and rules nationally vs ~2 from gerrymandering). So the episode says "the stop did more work in 2025–26", never "the pen doesn't matter".

Both sides quietly agree

Nobody in the record defends the gerrymander as a principle. The Supreme Court majority that closed the federal courts to partisan claims wrote that its conclusion "does not condone excessive partisan gerrymandering"; four justices dissented (log 1). Majorities in both parties, in states run by either party, say in the abstract they prefer a nonpartisan drawer (NBC 2025, adults, forced choice; log 107), and California's voters said the same thing while voting to suspend their commission (log 113). Each side defends its own map as a response to the other's.

Every court in the record treated the ballot calendar as the hard line. Missouri's Supreme Court put the 2022 map back for November; the U.S. Supreme Court, with no noted dissent, kept it there because ballots were "printed and mailed"; Virginia's court measured the process against early voting already under way; Texas and Alabama's stays cited the eve of an election. They disagree about almost everything else. A map needs a fixed point before ballots go out, and Missouri's two-map election shows what happens without one.
