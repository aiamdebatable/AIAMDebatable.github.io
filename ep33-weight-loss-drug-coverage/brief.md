# Research brief — Weight-Loss Drugs: Should Your Health Plan Pay, for Whom, at What Price?

_The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video._

**Episode type: DESIGN episode.** Green and gold do not argue yes or no. Each steelmans a coverage
**design**: a rule for who gets the drug paid for, on what terms, at what price. The verdict is the
design that survives both teams' objections, and the one dial the viewer still has to set. The judge
may **name** a dial the research earned; the judge never **sets** it.

**Grade:** episode research, 2026-10-06. Peg hunt → deep-research-v3 (41 agents) → a three-way hand
pass on the primary record (public payers / clinical and economics / employers and commercial plans) →
after Lucas reviewed the pitch, a top-up hand pass (D3) on the debate viewers actually have ("is it a
shortcut?", "are people abusing it?") and on the drug's uses beyond weight. Every figure below is
CONFIRMED in the fact-check log unless it is marked otherwise. Anything the hand passes could not
establish is in "Off air" at the end and is not used.

**Whose plan this is about.** The viewer this episode is built for gets health coverage through a job.
Every design below is a rule an **employer plan** can pick at its next renewal. Medicare's Bridge and the
federal employees' plan appear as one-line proof that big, real payers are already splitting this way;
they are not the story.

---

## The question

**Should your health plan pay for weight-loss drugs, and for whom, at what price?**

Almost no plan is choosing between "cover everyone" and "cover no one". An employer that still pays for
these drugs is choosing a **filter**, and the ones that stopped chose the bluntest filter there is. The
menu an employer plan actually has, each item a rule some real plan runs today:

1. **Risk tiers behind prior authorization:** BMI plus specific health conditions, checked before the
   first fill. Massachusetts' state-employee plan keeps coverage for BMI 27+ with an FDA-approved
   condition; Medicare's Bridge uses the same shape.
2. **A program first:** the drug only with documented participation in a lifestyle or behavioral
   program. A third of employers that cover the drugs (34%, up from 10%) require a dietitian, case
   manager, therapist or lifestyle program (KFF 2025); the federal employees' plan makes it the rule for
   every carrier in 2027.
3. **A price cap:** cover it, but cap what the member pays per month (Evernorth's $200 option; a
   vendor's offer, no outcome record).
4. **A subsidy outside the plan:** take it off the drug list and pay toward a cash price at the counter
   ($449 Zepbound KwikPen through GoodRx Employer Direct or Lilly Employer Connect; vendor announcements).
5. **No coverage for weight loss:** 14% of the large employers Business Group on Health surveyed have
   eliminated it or plan to in 2027, and coverage for obesity among them fell from 72% to 60%,
   2025 → 2026.

The two filters with real institutions and real arguments behind them are the two designs:

**GREEN — "Pay for the people at risk, at a bargained price."** The plan decides by medical need: body-mass
index plus specific health conditions, written as tiers and checked by prior authorization. It pays a
negotiated price, the member pays a small flat copay, and there is no extra hurdle beyond the
prescriber's word that the drug comes with diet and exercise. For an employer, Massachusetts'
state-employee plan (BMI 27+ with an FDA-approved condition) is this shape; at national scale,
**Medicare's GLP-1 Bridge**, England's NICE and Canada's Drug Agency use it. Goal: spend where the drug
buys the most health, and keep people on it.

**GOLD — "Lifestyle first, then the drug": pay for the program, not just the prescription.** The plan
covers the drug for its approved use rather than a narrower risk tier, but only while the member takes
part in a certified behavioral or lifestyle program, before and during treatment, and the plan pays for
the program too. For an employer, this is the condition a third of covering employers already attach; at
national scale, the **federal employees' plan (FEHB) for 2027** makes it the rule for every carrier
(OPM's documents set the program requirement and add no risk tiers; each carrier's own criteria will
only show in its 2027 brochure). Goal: pay for the whole treatment the drug's label describes (both
labels pair the drug with a reduced-calorie diet and more physical activity), not the prescription
alone.

**What does not blend:** what earns you the drug, **your medical need, or finishing a program first**.
Green's gate is a doctor's form about your health; gold's gate is a program's certificate that the
treatment around the drug is under way.

## Why now

This fall, the plan you get through work is deciding which of those rules it runs, and the biggest
payers in the country have already split the same drug in public.

- **Large employers are narrowing or leaving.** The Business Group on Health found that **14%** of
  employers "have already eliminated coverage for GLP-1s for weight loss or plan to do so in 2027", and
  that coverage for obesity fell from **72% in 2025 to 60% in 2026** (127 employers covering 8.7 million
  people in the US)
  ([BGH](https://www.businessgrouphealth.org/topics/blog/how-are-employers-managing-glp-1-coverage-amid-rising-demand-and-costs);
  [BGH survey release](https://www.businessgrouphealth.org/resources/2027-employer-healthcare-strategy-survey)).
  For 2027, 17% plan direct-to-employer arrangements and 16% will point workers to direct-to-consumer
  channels (BGH executive summary).
- **The ones still paying are attaching conditions.** About a third (34%) of firms covering the drugs
  for weight loss require a dietitian, case manager, therapist or lifestyle program, up from 10% a year
  earlier ([KFF EHBS 2025](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/2025-employer-health-benefits-survey/)).
- **The price an employer gets is moving.** **CVS Caremark**, a pharmacy-benefit manager, put Zepbound
  back on its template commercial formularies as an additional preferred option on 2026-10-01, crediting
  negotiation with "drug manufacturer partners". It disclosed no price, and each employer still decides
  whether to cover the drugs at all ([CVS Health, 2026-05-28](https://www.cvshealth.com/news/company-news/cvs-caremark-delivers-affordability-and-access-to-glp-1-weight-management-medications-with-expanded-coverage-options.html)).
- **Proof at national scale, one line each.** The **Medicare GLP-1 Bridge** (2026-07-01 → 2027-12-31)
  pays by risk tier at a bargained $245 net price, with a **$50 copay**; Medicare open enrollment runs
  October 15 to December 7
  ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coverage/prescription-drug-coverage/medicare-glp-1-bridge);
  [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/open-enrollment)). **The federal employees'
  plan** keeps covering the drugs in 2027 but only with documented participation in a certified
  intensive behavioral therapy program, and OPM names GLP-1 spending among the drivers of a **10.9%** rise
  in what enrollees pay toward premiums; open season runs November 9 to December 14
  ([OPM, Open Season Highlights 2027](https://www.opm.gov/healthcare-insurance/open-season/agency-benefits-officers/housing-for-os-materials/2026-federal-benefits-open-season-highlights-for-plan-year-2027.pdf);
  [OPM 2027 Call Letter](https://www.opm.gov/healthcare-insurance/carriers/fehb/2026/2026-07.pdf)).

That is the episode's question being answered in public, three ways: **for whom** (risk tiers:
Massachusetts' state-employee plan, Medicare's Bridge), **on what conditions** (a program: a third of
covering employers, the federal plan), **at what price** (the bargained deals that make coverage
affordable, or their absence, which makes employers leave).

**Script (structural):** "This year, the plan you get through work had to pick a rule for these drugs.
Some pay for the people at highest risk. A third of the ones still paying want you in a program first.
About one in seven big employers say they've stopped or will. And Medicare and the federal workers' plan
made the same split, in public." Name the programmes and survey sources; no personalities.
**Re-peg triggers:** KFF's 2026 Employer Health Benefits Survey (not out as of 2026-10-06) ·
2027-01-01 (Medicare's negotiated semaglutide price takes effect; the federal plan's requirement
starts) · any CMS action on what follows the Bridge.

## Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

**The drugs**
- **They work while you take them.** After 36 weeks on tirzepatide (20.9% average loss), people kept on
  it lost a further 5.5% over the next year; people switched to placebo regained 14.0%
  ([SURMOUNT-4, JAMA 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10714284/)). In the full STEP 1
  trial, semaglutide gave 14.9% loss vs 2.4% on placebo at 68 weeks
  ([STEP 1 extension paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9542252/)).
- **Stop, and in the trials most of it comes back.** In the STEP 1 extension, two-thirds of the loss
  returned within a year of stopping and the heart and metabolic gains reverted (same source). A 2026
  meta-analysis projects cardiometabolic markers back at baseline within about 1.4 years of stopping
  ([BMJ 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085304)).
- **In people with established heart disease and no diabetes, semaglutide cut major heart events from
  8.0% to 6.5%**, and that benefit did not track how much weight people lost
  ([SELECT, NEJM 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37952131/);
  [Lancet 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736(25)01375-3)).
- **Real-world staying power is rising.** For the weight-loss-labelled drugs, the share still on them a
  year later rose from 33.2% (2021 starters) to 62.6% (early-2024 starters), which Prime ties mostly to
  the end of shortages ([Prime Therapeutics](https://www.primetherapeutics.com/documents/d/primetherapeutics/evt_ext_261001-a_amcppstr_trendsrealworldpersistence_r4-1)).
  In a 125,474-person cohort, 64.8% of people without diabetes stopped within a year
  ([JAMA Network Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11786232/)).
- **Cost is the top reason people stop**: 47.6% of stoppers in one health system cited cost or
  insurance ([Obesity 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41039650/)).

**The money**
- **It does not pay for itself.** CBO: Medicare coverage would cost $38.8B in drugs over 2026–2034
  against $3.4B in savings elsewhere, about 9 cents back per dollar, and "CBO is not aware of any
  direct evidence" that treating obesity with these drugs reduces other medical spending
  ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/60441)). ICER's lifetime model gets more back (about 37–41
  cents per extra drug dollar, our arithmetic) and still finds it costs more than it saves.
- **It is still good value per year of health gained.** ICER's council rated both drugs high long-term
  value at Q1 2025 net prices, which sit below ICER's value-based price ranges
  ([ICER, Dec 2025](https://icer.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/ICER_Obesity_Final-Report_For-Publication_121625-1.pdf)).
- **And unaffordable for everyone eligible.** Fewer than 1% of the 92 million eligible US adults could be
  treated within five years before crossing ICER's $880M-a-year affordability test, and that holds even
  at $245 a month (same source).
- **Price is the hinge.** EBRI's simulation: covering the drugs raises employer premiums 5.3–13.8% at
  real-world net prices ($617–766 a month), and 1–3.9% at a hypothetical $200
  ([EBRI, 2025](https://www.ebri.org/content/glp-1-coverage-and-its-impact-on-employment-based-health-plan-premiums--a-simulation-based-analysis)).
  In real employer plans, coverage added $9.23 per plan member per month at 2025 prices
  ([Shahzad et al., JAMA Health Forum 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13309865/)).
- **There are three prices, never one.** List (Wegovy roughly $1,350 a month now, per a price tracker and
  the White House's 2025 figure, approximate; $675 from 2027-01-01, Novo's announcement),
  net (what insurers pay after rebates; ICER used $6,829 a year for semaglutide, Q1 2025), and cash
  ($349 Wegovy from Novo; $299–$449 Zepbound from Lilly, conditional). Novo says its list cut does not
  change self-pay prices; Mercer expects it to change employers' net cost little
  ([Novo](https://www.novonordisk.com/news-and-media/news-and-ir-materials/news-details.html?id=916504);
  [Mercer](https://www.mercer.com/en-us/insights/us-health-news/novo-nordisks-glp-1-list-price-cut-what-to-watch-next/)).

**The rules**
- **Medicare's exclusion of weight-loss drugs is statute**; the Bridge works around it as a demonstration
  ([42 U.S.C. 1395w-102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1395w-102);
  [42 U.S.C. 1396r-8](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1396r-8)).
- **Self-funded employer plans are outside state insurance mandates** (ERISA), and the ACA's
  essential-benefits rule reaches only individual and small-group insurance; the 2027 federal
  marketplace rule adds nothing on these drugs
  ([29 U.S.C. 1144](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/1144);
  [42 U.S.C. 300gg-6](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/300gg-6);
  [Fed. Reg. 2026-10050](https://www.federalregister.gov/d/2026-10050)). So for most viewers with job-based
  coverage, the setting is chosen by the employer, not the state.
- **A pharmacy-benefit manager's formulary is a template.** CVS Caremark's own words: "Plan sponsors …
  retain discretion to customize coverage for their members."

**Dropping coverage does not stop use.** About one in four GLP-1 users (27%) had insurance but paid the
full price themselves, and 56% found the drugs hard to afford
([KFF poll, Nov 2025](https://www.kff.org/public-opinion/kff-health-tracking-poll-prescription-drug-costs-views-on-trump-administration-actions-and-glp-1-use/)).

## Contested (the real fight)

**GREEN — "Pay for the people at risk, at a bargained price."** The drug's best evidence is in the people
green targets: SELECT's heart benefit was in people with established heart disease, and it did not depend
on how much weight they lost, so a plan buying health should buy it where the risk is. Price is what
makes coverage affordable (EBRI: 5.3–13.8% on premiums at today's net price, 1–3.9% at $200), and the
buyers that limit who qualifies are the ones with a price on the table: Medicare's $245 came with risk
tiers, NICE's approval rests on a commercial arrangement with the maker, and Canada's agency said
coverage would need a price about 67% lower. Once people qualify, green keeps
them on it with a small flat copay (the Bridge's is $50), because the trials say stopping throws most of the benefit away and the top
reason people stop is cost (47.6%). Green does not ignore lifestyle: the Bridge requires the prescriber to
certify that the drug comes with diet and exercise. It just doesn't make the patient prove it a second
time.

**GOLD — "Lifestyle first, then the drug": pay for the program, not just the prescription.** Both drugs'
labels pair them with a reduced-calorie diet and more physical activity. Even Medicare's Bridge pays only
when the drug comes "in combination with current and ongoing lifestyle modification", and the trial that
paired the drug with real therapy (STEP 3, 30 counselling visits) got 16.0% loss. A signature on a form is not a
program. OPM wrote that "attestation statements alone" should not bypass the requirement, and gold's
design pays for the therapy as well as the drug. Lifestyle programmes are worth buying on their own: in
the Diabetes Prevention Program one cut new diabetes by 58%, beating a drug, and the Task Force rates them
grade B with harms "small to none". Gold filters on a program under way, not on a risk tier, so nobody is
shut out for being one BMI point short of a line.
And gold is honest about the money: CBO finds about 9 cents back per dollar, ICER says fewer than 1% of
eligible people can be treated within its affordability test even at $245, and OPM names GLP-1 spending
among the costs behind a 10.9% rise in what federal workers pay. If a plan is going to pay roughly what a
worker pays toward a year of family coverage for one person's drug, it is fair to ask that the money buy
the whole treatment the label describes, program included. Gold adds a fair point from the record: after
stopping, weight comes back more slowly after behavioural programmes than after drugs (BMJ 2026).

**The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:**

- *Gold at green:* **the Bridge has no outcome record**: it is three months old, CMS has published no
  uptake, and it ends 2027-12-31 with nothing scheduled after. **Green's price is not the viewer's
  price**: $245 is a Medicare deal; the net price insurers paid in early 2025 was about $569 a month
  (ICER's figure, from SSR Health), and Caremark's new deal discloses no price at all. **The rules leave
  people out**: KFF estimates fewer than half of those meeting the clinical criteria are eligible once
  the other exclusions apply (3.8 million of 9.7 million), and the $50 gets no low-income help. **Green's lifestyle condition is paper**: an attestation, which is exactly
  what OPM refused to accept.
- *Green at gold:* **no study anywhere shows the requirement works**: not one GLP-1 study tests a
  coverage condition. **The nearest record cuts the other way**: insurer-required diets before bariatric
  surgery predicted dropout and weight gain, and a trade-press account of a second study found no
  clinical benefit. **STEP 3 tested drug plus therapy, not therapy as a gate**: everyone in it got both.
  **A gate rations by who can attend**, and cost and insurance hurdles are already the top reason people
  stop. **A program gate cannot screen out a "shortcut"**: in STEP 1 and STEP 3 both arms got the same
  programme, and the drug is what changed the result (12–27 in 100 reached a 10% loss without it, 69–75
  with it); never phrase this as anyone's effort or willpower. **SURMOUNT-4 did not test behavioural
  therapy at all**, and still found 89.5% of people who stayed on the drug kept most of their loss.

**Where the record leans, said plainly.** On the filter, the record leans **green**: risk tiers aim at
the people with trial outcomes behind them, and the program requirement has no outcome record while the
nearest analogue counts against it. On the price, the record leans **gold**: green's design works at a
bargained price that most viewers' plans cannot get, and neither design pays for itself. Equal length on
"documented failures" would misrepresent this: gold's requirement is untested, and green's own program
is too new to have results.

## The debate people actually have: is it a shortcut?

Before any design, this is the question viewers ask: **"Why should my plan pay for something a lot of
people could handle with diet and exercise? Is this a shortcut? Are people abusing it?"** It is gold's gut
objection, and it gets asked on air in those words. Hand pass D3 tested it against the record. Here is
what the record says, and where it lands.

**Diet and exercise work, and they are worth paying for.** In the Diabetes Prevention Program a lifestyle
programme (a 7% weight-loss goal, 150 minutes of activity a week) cut new diabetes by **58%**, and beat a
drug, metformin (31%); ten years on, with weight partly regained, the reduction was 34%. The US
Preventive Services Task Force tells clinicians to offer intensive behavioural programmes to adults with
BMI 30+ (grade B), with harms "small to none".

**But they rarely reach, or hold, the losses the drug trials count.** A large, intensive lifestyle trial, Look AHEAD (5,145 adults with type 2 diabetes; group sessions, then
years of individual counselling; an individual goal of 10%), got **37.7%** of people to a 10% loss at one year. At eight years **26.9%** had 10% off, against
17.2% in a group that got education classes only; of those who reached 10% in year one, 39.3% still had it
in year eight. In the Task Force's evidence review, behavioural programmes beat controls by about 2.4 kg at 12–18
months (67 studies). A
29-study meta-analysis found more than 80% of lost weight regained by five years (the average person still
kept more than 3 kg off). A national survey found 17.3% of adults who were ever overweight report keeping
10% off for a year (self-reported).

**In the drug trials, both arms got the same programme.** This is the fact that answers the "shortcut"
question. The placebo groups were not left to sit still; they got the same diet-and-exercise support as
the drug groups:
- **STEP 1** (lifestyle support in both arms): **12.0%** on placebo reached a 10% loss, against **69.1%** on
  semaglutide (68 weeks).
- **STEP 3** (30 counselling visits and an initial low-calorie diet in both arms): **27.0%** on placebo
  reached 10%, against **75.3%** on the drug.
- **SURMOUNT-1** (tirzepatide): 50–57% on the two top doses lost 20% or more, against 3% on placebo.

So 12 to 27 in 100 of the people doing the programme without the drug reached a 10% loss, and 69 to 75 in
100 doing the same programme with it. The line for the script: **"The drug is not a way around diet and
exercise. In the trials, both groups did the same programme; the drug is what changed the result."**
("Both arms made the same effort" is also accurate, but the word "effort" stays off air: say "the same
programme".)

**Why weight comes back is largely biology.** A 2018 review (Hall and Kahan) estimates that for every
kilogram lost, appetite rises by about 100 calories a day above where it started, while the body burns
about 20–30 fewer; hormonal changes that raise appetite persist. The 2025 Lancet commission on obesity
names the "personal responsibility" frame as a source of bias, and most of the public already agrees:
**65%** in a Pew survey say willpower alone is usually not enough to lose weight and keep it off.

**Two caveats that cut the other way, and they travel with the above.**
- Look AHEAD's lifestyle programme **did not reduce heart attacks, strokes or cardiovascular deaths**; the
  trial was stopped early for futility. That is not proof the drug would have: SELECT (Settled) is a
  different population (established heart disease, no diabetes). Never set the two side by side as one
  comparison.
- **The drug works only while it is taken** (Settled: SURMOUNT-4; the STEP 1 extension). On the drug, the
  loss holds: SELECT's four-year figure is −10.2% against −1.5% on placebo.

**The disease label, both halves.** The American Medical Association has called obesity a disease since
2013. The 2025 Lancet commission draws a line: **clinical** obesity (excess fat already affecting organs
or daily function) is an illness and should get equitable access to treatment; **preclinical** obesity
(excess fat, organs still working) is a risk state that gets counselling and monitoring. Both are true;
say both. The commission's split is, in effect, a medical-need filter, which is green's shape.

**"Are people abusing it?" — said precisely.**
- A 2026 study of health records found prescriptions to adults with **no documented FDA-approved reason**
  rose **15-fold** from 2021 to 2025 (0.1% → 1.5% of such adults; about 1.1 million people in all), and
  **35.1%** of them had a normal BMI. That prescribing skews to people in better-off areas and with
  private insurance. Its limits are part of the fact: the records cannot say why a drug was prescribed,
  missing documentation is not proof there was no reason, compounded products were not captured, and the
  study **cannot show who paid**.
- A secret-shopper study (in JAMA, as reported by AJMC; APPROXIMATE, attribute it) found most online
  sellers prescribed, often within a day, and only about half asked about eating disorders; the shopper
  met the eligibility criteria.
- FDA has received hundreds of adverse-event reports tied to **compounded** versions (990 semaglutide,
  more than 730 tirzepatide, to May 31, 2026), says these are likely undercounted, and warns of
  counterfeit Ozempic in the US.
- None of this is evidence about members whose plan pays under its criteria. And **no evidence was found
  of people gaining weight to qualify** for coverage (Off air).

**What the public thinks.** In KFF's 2024 poll, **61%** said Medicare should cover these drugs for weight
loss, and views stayed largely unchanged after people heard the arguments for and against, including that
premiums could rise. In Pew's 2024 survey, 53% of those who had heard of the drugs called them a good
option for people with obesity or a weight-related condition; 12% for people without. No poll was found on
whether **employer** plans should cover them.

**Where the record lands, said plainly (no false balance).** The "shortcut" intuition does not survive the
trials. Diet and exercise are worth paying for, and both designs already require them, but for most people
they do not reach or hold a 10% loss, and in the trials the drug made the difference for people doing the
same programme. The cost worry is real (Settled: the drug does not pay for itself), and a narrower misuse
worry is real (prescriptions with no documented reason, thin online screening, an unsafe compounded
supply), but neither is evidence about members whose plan pays under its criteria. This does not decide
the design fight on its own: it removes the "shortcut" reason for a program gate, and leaves gold's real
reasons (the label's pairing, STEP 3, the money) standing to be tested.

## The drug's other uses — and why the diagnosis decides coverage

The same two molecules are approved for more than weight, and they are being tested for more still. Only
FDA-approved uses and trial evidence are used, each labelled; anecdotes are not evidence.

| use | semaglutide (Wegovy / Ozempic / Rybelsus) | tirzepatide (Zepbound / Mounjaro) | status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type 2 diabetes | Ozempic 2017; Rybelsus 2019 | Mounjaro 2022 | APPROVED |
| Chronic weight management | Wegovy 2021; Wegovy tablets Dec 2025 | Zepbound 2023 | APPROVED |
| Heart-attack and stroke risk, established heart disease plus overweight, no diabetes needed | Wegovy, March 2024 | none | APPROVED (semaglutide only) |
| Heart-event risk in type 2 diabetes | Ozempic 2020; Rybelsus (date approximate) | Mounjaro (date approximate) | APPROVED |
| Kidney disease in type 2 diabetes | Ozempic, January 2025 | none | APPROVED (semaglutide only) |
| Moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea with obesity | none | Zepbound, December 2024 | APPROVED (tirzepatide only) |
| MASH liver disease with moderate-to-advanced scarring | Wegovy, August 2025 | none | APPROVED under accelerated approval, pending a confirmatory trial |
| Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) | STEP-HFpEF trials; label says an effect "has not been established" | SUMMIT; US application withdrawn 2025 (as Lilly reported) | TRIAL EVIDENCE, NOT APPROVED |
| Knee osteoarthritis pain | STEP 9 | none found | TRIAL EVIDENCE, NOT APPROVED |
| Alcohol use disorder | three small trials (48, 50, 108 people) | none checked | EARLY TRIALS |
| Rheumatoid arthritis; fibromyalgia | one chart review; one records study | (same, class-wide) | EARLY / OBSERVATIONAL, no randomised trial |

**Heart failure, the strongest unapproved case.** In STEP-HFpEF (529 people with this kind of heart failure
and BMI 30+, no diabetes), semaglutide improved a patient-reported symptom score by **7.8 points** more
than placebo and the six-minute walk by **20.3 metres** (21.5 m against 1.2 m); a companion trial in people
with diabetes found +7.3 points and +14.3 m. Pooled across four trials, worsening heart-failure events
were 2.8% against 4.7%, with no significant effect on cardiovascular death alone. In SUMMIT (tirzepatide,
731 people), cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure occurred in **9.9%** against **15.3%** on
placebo over about two years. Even so, neither drug is approved for it: FDA added SELECT's heart-failure
numbers to Wegovy's label with the note that an effect "has not been established", and Lilly said it
withdrew its US application after FDA asked for another trial. No threshold for what walking distance is
clinically meaningful was verified; do not call 20 metres "small" or "large".

**Joints and inflammation.** In STEP 9 (knee osteoarthritis, BMI 30+, 407 people), knee pain scores fell
41.7 points against 27.5 on placebo: trial evidence, not an approved use. Inflammation is a lab marker,
not an outcome: CRP fell 43.5% against 7.3% in STEP-HFpEF. Rheumatoid arthritis and fibromyalgia rest on
one chart review and one records study each; say "early, not tested in a randomised trial".

**Why it matters for coverage: the same pen is covered or not depending on why it is written.** Medicare's
statute excludes drugs "when used for" weight loss; it excludes a use, not a molecule. So today the same
GLP-1 goes through a Medicare member's regular Part D plan when it is prescribed for diabetes, sleep apnea,
MASH or (Wegovy) heart-risk reduction, and through the $50 Bridge only when it is written for weight. An
employer plan can draw the same line in plain words: Massachusetts' state-employee plan says "Medicines
used only for weight loss will no longer be covered", while coverage may be approved when the doctor shows
the medicine is "being used to treat that condition". Heart failure, arthritis and fibromyalgia are not FDA
indications, so a person on the drug for those benefits is, for coverage purposes, on it for weight (the
Bridge uses BMI 30+ with this kind of heart failure as one of its weight tiers) or not covered. Whether
commercial plans pay for off-label uses was not established (Off air).

## The reframe

The shouting asks "cover it or not?" The record says four things that question misses.

1. **Everybody is already rationing.** Some employer plans ration by risk tier, a third of covering
   employers by a program, others by price, and the ones who "dropped" it ration by wallet; Medicare and
   the federal plan made the same split in public. "Cover it or not" is not the choice anyone is
   making. The choice is the filter.
2. **"Does it pay for itself?" is the wrong test.** CBO and ICER agree it does not, and ICER still calls it
   good value per year of health gained. The honest question is what it buys and who pays, not whether
   it is free.
3. **Dropping coverage moves the bill; it does not delete it.** One in four users with insurance already
   pays full price, about one in ten weekly Wegovy prescriptions went through cash channels in October
   2025, and 17% of large employers plan direct-to-employer arrangements for these drugs in 2027. The
   real variable is **price × how long people stay on it**, and every design is a bet on both.
4. **The same pen is covered or not depending on why it is written.** Coverage rules exclude a *use*, not
   a molecule: Medicare's statute excludes drugs "when used for" weight loss, and Massachusetts'
   state-employee plan dropped medicines "used only for weight loss" while keeping them for diagnosed
   conditions. The same drug is approved for diabetes, heart-attack and stroke risk, kidney disease, sleep
   apnea and a liver disease, and has trial evidence (not approval) in heart failure and knee arthritis.
   So "should the plan pay for weight-loss drugs?" is really "should the plan pay when the reason on the
   prescription is weight?" — and the line between weight and the illnesses it drives is exactly what
   green's risk tiers try to draw.

---

## Both sides quietly agree

**Both designs already require diet and exercise alongside the drug.** The Bridge makes the prescriber
certify "current and ongoing lifestyle modification"; the federal plan makes a program provider certify
participation. Medicare.gov even says "Your provider must certify that you're using the GLP-1 drug as
part of a lifestyle program." The fight is not over whether lifestyle matters. **It is a signature
versus a certificate**, and nobody has tested whether the certificate changes anything.

And both are betting on the same thing: **that the people they pay for stay on the drug.** Green's flat
copay and gold's program are two different theories of persistence, and the trials say the money spent
on someone who stops in the first year mostly buys weight that comes back.

## Verdict framing

"The design that survives both teams' objections, and the dial you still have to set." Candidates, each
to be **earned in the debate** and dropped if it is not:

- **Survives:** **pay by risk, at a bargained price, with the program covered and offered, not
  required.** Green's filter survives because it is the one with outcomes behind it. Gold's program
  survives as a benefit (the federal plan already must cover up to 12–22 sessions) but not as a gate,
  because the gate has no record. Gold's price discipline survives: coverage at today's net price costs everyone's
  premium, so the price must be bargained, not assumed.
- **Fell:** "It's a shortcut around diet and exercise" (in the trials both arms got the same programme;
  the drug changed the result). "It pays for itself" (CBO and ICER both say no). "A program requirement
  makes the drug work better" (untested; the nearest record cuts against it). "Medicare's $245 price is available to your
  plan" (it is a Medicare deal). "Dropping coverage saves the money" (the bill moves to the user).
- **The one dial the viewer sets — the judge names it, the judge does not set it: WHERE THE LINE SITS.**
  How far down the risk ladder your employer's plan should pay, knowing each rung costs everyone's
  premium. Any employer plan can write any of these lines into its prior authorization; the named
  settings are real-world proof points, each with a real institution and a documented weakness:
  1. **BMI 40 and four conditions** — NHS England's first cohort. Weakness: at least 220,000 of
     3.4 million eligible funded in three years; up to 12 years to reach the rest.
  2. **BMI 35, or lower with specific conditions** — Medicare's Bridge; NICE (35 plus one condition).
     Weakness: an end date with nothing after it; fewer than half of those meeting the clinical criteria
     are eligible once the other exclusions apply (KFF: 3.8 million of 9.7 million).
  3. **BMI 27 with an FDA-approved condition** — Massachusetts' state-employee plan since July 2026.
     Weakness: it took a 10–7 vote, and obesity-only prescriptions lost coverage.
  4. **Anyone it is approved for** — the federal employees' plan (behind its program requirement).
     Weakness: OPM names GLP-1 spending among the drivers of a 10.9% rise in what enrollees pay.
  5. **No one, for weight loss** — North Carolina's state plan since 2024. Weakness: one in four users
     already pays full price anyway, and NC's own Medicaid reversed a drop within ten weeks.

  The research earned the price tag on each rung: EBRI's 5.3–13.8% vs 1–3.9%, and ICER's "under 1%". It
  did **not** earn a right answer. That is the viewer's call.

## Make it Matter — the viewer's own decision this fall

The episode is about a choice the viewer's own job-based plan makes at its next renewal, and what the
viewer finds out at open enrollment. It is not an abstract policy, and it is not about Medicare.

- **The story, ep21-style: one worker, one email, one drug, three possible answers.** A worker (unnamed,
  invented, never a real or identifiable person) opens the open-enrollment email from HR. The drug is the
  same in every version; the answer depends on which rule the employer picked:
  1. **"Covered if your BMI and health conditions qualify; your doctor sends a prior authorization."**
     Green's shape. Real instance: Massachusetts' state-employee plan (BMI 27+ with an FDA-approved
     condition); at national scale, Medicare's Bridge.
  2. **"Covered once you're enrolled in our lifestyle program, and while you stay in it. The sessions are
     covered too."** Gold's shape. Real instance: a third of employers that cover the drugs; at national
     scale, the federal employees' plan from 2027.
  3. **"No longer covered for weight loss. Here's a discount program."** Real instance: 14% of BGH's large
     employers; the outside-the-plan subsidy at a $449 cash price (vendor announcements).
  The episode walks the worker through each answer: who gets in, what it costs them, what it costs
  everyone's premium, and what the record says about each. The "is it a shortcut?" question is the
  worker's coworker's question, asked once and answered with the trial arms; the worker's effort or
  willpower is never the subject.
- **What to check in your own plan** (coverage, never medical advice): whether the drug list excludes
  weight-loss use; what conditions sit on it (BMI and conditions, a program); whether a "discount
  program" outside the plan counts toward your deductible (nobody has published that for the new employer
  deals); and whether the reason on the prescription (diabetes, sleep apnea, heart disease) puts it under
  a different rule (see "The drug's other uses").
- **The picture:** one drug, three prices this month: roughly $1,350 list (approximate), $349–449 cash,
  and what plans pay after rebates (undisclosed; ICER's early-2025 estimate is about $570 a month). The
  comparator is the viewer's own premium contribution (below).
- **Medicare and federal viewers, one line:** the Bridge runs through 2027 and is not a plan you pick
  (Medicare open enrollment Oct 15 – Dec 7; CMS has not said what happens if you switch Part D plan), and
  every federal carrier requires a certified program from 2027 (open season Nov 9 – Dec 14; read your
  carrier's brochure).
