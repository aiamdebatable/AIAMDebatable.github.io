The research · What the drug does, and what it costs a plan

ColeTwo halves. First the body, then the bill.

LenaDoes it work?

ColeWhile you take it, yes. In the main semaglutide trial, people lost about fifteen percent of their weight over sixteen months, against about two percent on placebo.

LenaAnd if you stop?

ColeIn the trials, most of it comes back. In one tirzepatide trial, people kept on the drug lost more over the next year, while people switched to placebo regained about fourteen percent. In a semaglutide follow-up, two-thirds of the loss returned within a year of stopping, and the heart and metabolic gains went with it.

LenaIs it only about weight?

ColeNo, and this matters for the fight. In people with heart disease and no diabetes, semaglutide cut major heart events from eight percent to six and a half. And that benefit didn't track how much weight people lost.

LenaDo people stay on it?

ColeMore than they did. For people starting in early twenty twenty-four, about six in ten were still on it a year later. For twenty twenty-one starters, about one in three. And when people stop, the top reason in one health system was cost or insurance. Nearly half.

LenaOkay. The bill. What does a plan actually pay?

ColeThere are three prices, never one. The list price, roughly thirteen hundred and fifty dollars a month for Wegovy. The cash price, roughly three hundred and fifty to four hundred and fifty. And the net price plans pay after rebates, which nobody publishes. The best estimate, from early twenty twenty-five, is about five hundred and seventy dollars a month.

LenaGive me something I can picture.

ColeOne person's drug, at that net price, costs about what the average worker pays toward a year of family health coverage. About sixty-eight hundred dollars a year, each.

LenaSo does it pay for itself? Fewer heart attacks, less diabetes later?

ColeNot on the record. The Congressional Budget Office scored Medicare coverage at about nine cents back on every dollar, and said it knows of no direct evidence these drugs cut other medical spending. ICER's lifetime model gets more back, and still finds the drugs cost more than they save.

LenaThen why would anyone cover it?

ColeBecause costing more isn't the same as bad value. ICER's council rated both drugs high long-term value at early twenty twenty-five prices. It just can't be given to everyone: fewer than one in a hundred eligible adults could be treated within five years under ICER's affordability test.

The research · The question people ask: is it a shortcut?

ColeThis one gets argued at work, so let's ask it straight.

LenaOkay. Is this a shortcut? Why should my plan pay for something a lot of people could handle with diet and exercise? Are people abusing it?

ColeThree questions. First: do diet and exercise work? Yes, and they're worth paying for. In the Diabetes Prevention Program, a lifestyle program cut new diabetes by fifty-eight percent. It beat a drug. And the Task Force tells doctors to offer these programs to adults with a BMI of thirty or more.

LenaSo why not just that?

ColeBecause they rarely reach, or hold, the losses the drug trials count. A large, intensive lifestyle trial called Look AHEAD, in adults with type two diabetes, got about four in ten people to a ten percent loss at one year. After eight years, about one in four still had it.

LenaOne in four isn't nothing.

ColeIt isn't. It's real, and it's worth having. But across twenty-nine long-term studies, more than eighty percent of lost weight was back by five years. And a review explains part of why: for every kilo lost, appetite rises by about a hundred calories a day. Weight coming back is largely biology.

LenaThen the real test. Does the drug replace diet and exercise?

ColeThe trials answer that directly. The placebo group wasn't left alone. Both groups got the same diet-and-exercise program. In one trial, about one in eight on placebo reached a ten percent loss. About seven in ten on the drug.

LenaSame program, both groups?

ColeSame program. In another trial, with thirty counselling visits in both groups, about a quarter reached it without the drug, three in four with it. The drug isn't a way around diet and exercise. Both groups did the same program, and the drug is what changed the result.

LenaAnd the last one. People taking it who don't need it?

ColeSay it precisely. One health-records study found prescriptions to adults with no documented approved reason rose fifteen-fold from twenty twenty-one to twenty twenty-five. About a third of them had a normal weight.

LenaSo people are gaming it.

ColeThe study can't say that. Missing paperwork isn't proof there was no reason, and it can't show who paid. We also found no evidence of people gaining weight to qualify for coverage. The safety worry is elsewhere: the FDA has hundreds of adverse-event reports tied to compounded copies, and it has warned about counterfeits.

LenaSo where does the shortcut question land?

ColePlainly: diet and exercise work, and both designs on this floor already require them. But for most people they don't reach or hold the losses the trials count, and in the trials, the drug made the difference for people doing the same program.

ColeThe cost worry is real, and so is the worry about prescriptions with no documented reason. But neither one is evidence about members whose plan pays under its own rules.

The research · Same drug, different reason, and each rule's own record

ColeStart with what's approved, and keep it labelled.

LenaApproved for what?

ColeThe same two molecules. Semaglutide is approved for type two diabetes, for weight, to cut the risk of heart attack and stroke in people with heart disease, and for kidney disease in diabetes. Tirzepatide is approved for diabetes, for weight, and for moderate-to-severe sleep apnea with obesity.

LenaAnd the liver one I keep hearing about?

ColeWegovy, for a liver disease called MASH, with scarring. Approved, pending a confirmatory trial.

LenaHeart failure?

ColeTrial evidence, not approved. In one trial in people with one kind of heart failure, semaglutide improved a symptom score by about eight points more than placebo, and people walked about twenty metres further in six minutes. FDA says an effect on heart failure has not been established.

LenaAnd tirzepatide?

ColeIn its heart-failure trial, cardiovascular death or a worsening heart-failure event came to about ten in a hundred, against fifteen on placebo. It isn't approved for that either. Lilly says it withdrew its application after FDA asked for another trial.

LenaJoints?

ColeOne trial in knee arthritis cut pain more than placebo. Trial evidence, not approved. Beyond that, claims about joints and inflammation are early, and haven't been tested in a randomised trial.

LenaSo why does any of this matter for coverage?

ColeBecause coverage rules exclude a use, not a molecule. Medicare's law excludes drugs when used for weight loss. So the same drug goes through a member's regular drug plan when it's written for diabetes, sleep apnea or heart risk, and through the Bridge only when it's written for weight.

LenaAnd at work?

ColeSame line, in plain words. Massachusetts told its state workers that medicines used only for weight loss will no longer be covered. But coverage may be approved when the doctor shows the medicine is being used to treat a condition.

LenaSo the same drug is covered or not, depending on why it's written.

ColeExactly. Now the record on the rules themselves. Here it gets thin.

LenaHow thin?

ColeThe program requirement first. No study anywhere tests whether a coverage condition makes these drugs work better or saves money. The closest record is weight-loss surgery, where insurer-required diets predicted people dropping out, and weight gain before the operation.

LenaAnd the risk line?

ColeToo new to have results. Medicare's Bridge started in July, CMS hasn't published uptake, and nothing is scheduled after December twenty twenty-seven. It leaves people out, too: KFF estimates fewer than half of those meeting the clinical criteria are eligible.

LenaSo two rules, and neither has a record of its own yet. Hold that.

The verdict

WaltThis one doesn't resolve, and no design wins it. Three things from me. What's settled, what survived, and the dial that's yours.

WaltBoth teams accepted the same record. The drug works while you take it, and in the trials most of it comes back when you stop. It doesn't pay for itself. And it isn't a shortcut: in the trials, both groups did the same program, and the drug changed the result.

WaltWhat survived from green. The filter. Risk tiers aim at the people with trial outcomes behind them, while the program requirement has no record of its own. And the small copay, because cost is the top reason people stop.

WaltWhat survived from gold. The price. At today's net price, coverage costs every member's premium, and green's bargained price is a Medicare deal, not your plan's. And the program, as a benefit: a signature isn't a program.

WaltWhat fell. It's a shortcut. It pays for itself. A program requirement makes the drug work better: untested. And dropping coverage saves the money: about one in four users had insurance and still paid full price.

WaltSo here's the design that survives both teams. Pay by risk, at a bargained price, with the program covered and offered, but not required. Green's line, gold's price discipline, and gold's program as a benefit, not a gate.

WaltThat leaves one dial neither team can set for you: where the line sits. A BMI of forty with four conditions, like England's first rollout, which reaches a small share of those eligible in its first three years. Thirty-five, or lower with conditions, like Medicare's Bridge, which ends in twenty twenty-seven. Twenty-seven with an approved condition, like Massachusetts, which dropped weight alone.

WaltAnyone it's approved for, like the federal plan, where these drugs are among the costs behind higher premiums. Or no one, like North Carolina's state plan, which leaves the whole bill to the people still taking it. So how far down that line should your plan pay? That one's yours.
