The question

Should your health plan pay for weight-loss drugs, and for whom, at what price?

Almost no plan is choosing between "cover everyone" and "cover no one". An employer that still pays for these drugs is choosing a filter, and the ones that stopped chose the bluntest filter there is. The menu an employer plan actually has, each item a rule some real plan runs today:

  1. Risk tiers behind prior authorization: BMI plus specific health conditions, checked before the first fill. Massachusetts' state-employee plan keeps coverage for BMI 27+ with an FDA-approved condition; Medicare's Bridge uses the same shape.
  2. A program first: the drug only with documented participation in a lifestyle or behavioral program. A third of employers that cover the drugs (34%, up from 10%) require a dietitian, case manager, therapist or lifestyle program (KFF 2025); the federal employees' plan makes it the rule for every carrier in 2027.
  3. A price cap: cover it, but cap what the member pays per month (Evernorth's $200 option; a vendor's offer, no outcome record).
  4. A subsidy outside the plan: take it off the drug list and pay toward a cash price at the counter ($449 Zepbound KwikPen through GoodRx Employer Direct or Lilly Employer Connect; vendor announcements).
  5. No coverage for weight loss: 14% of the large employers Business Group on Health surveyed have eliminated it or plan to in 2027, and coverage for obesity among them fell from 72% to 60%, 2025 → 2026.

The two filters with real institutions and real arguments behind them are the two designs:

GREEN — "Pay for the people at risk, at a bargained price." The plan decides by medical need: body-mass index plus specific health conditions, written as tiers and checked by prior authorization. It pays a negotiated price, the member pays a small flat copay, and there is no extra hurdle beyond the prescriber's word that the drug comes with diet and exercise. For an employer, Massachusetts' state-employee plan (BMI 27+ with an FDA-approved condition) is this shape; at national scale, Medicare's GLP-1 Bridge, England's NICE and Canada's Drug Agency use it. Goal: spend where the drug buys the most health, and keep people on it.

GOLD — "Lifestyle first, then the drug": pay for the program, not just the prescription. The plan covers the drug for its approved use rather than a narrower risk tier, but only while the member takes part in a certified behavioral or lifestyle program, before and during treatment, and the plan pays for the program too. For an employer, this is the condition a third of covering employers already attach; at national scale, the federal employees' plan (FEHB) for 2027 makes it the rule for every carrier (OPM's documents set the program requirement and add no risk tiers; each carrier's own criteria will only show in its 2027 brochure). Goal: pay for the whole treatment the drug's label describes (both labels pair the drug with a reduced-calorie diet and more physical activity), not the prescription alone.

What does not blendwhat earns you the drug, your medical need, or finishing a program first.

Green's gate is a doctor's form about your health; gold's gate is a program's certificate that the treatment around the drug is under way.

Why now

This fall, the plan you get through work is deciding which of those rules it runs, and the biggest payers in the country have already split the same drug in public.

  • Large employers are narrowing or leaving. The Business Group on Health found that 14% of employers "have already eliminated coverage for GLP-1s for weight loss or plan to do so in 2027", and that coverage for obesity fell from 72% in 2025 to 60% in 2026 (127 employers covering 8.7 million people in the US) (BGH; BGH survey release). For 2027, 17% plan direct-to-employer arrangements and 16% will point workers to direct-to-consumer channels (BGH executive summary).
  • The ones still paying are attaching conditions. About a third (34%) of firms covering the drugs for weight loss require a dietitian, case manager, therapist or lifestyle program, up from 10% a year earlier (KFF EHBS 2025).
  • The price an employer gets is moving. CVS Caremark, a pharmacy-benefit manager, put Zepbound back on its template commercial formularies as an additional preferred option on 2026-10-01, crediting negotiation with "drug manufacturer partners". It disclosed no price, and each employer still decides whether to cover the drugs at all (CVS Health, 2026-05-28).
  • Proof at national scale, one line each. The Medicare GLP-1 Bridge (2026-07-01 → 2027-12-31) pays by risk tier at a bargained $245 net price, with a $50 copay; Medicare open enrollment runs October 15 to December 7 (CMS; Medicare.gov). The federal employees' plan keeps covering the drugs in 2027 but only with documented participation in a certified intensive behavioral therapy program, and OPM names GLP-1 spending among the drivers of a 10.9% rise in what enrollees pay toward premiums; open season runs November 9 to December 14 (OPM, Open Season Highlights 2027; OPM 2027 Call Letter).

That is the episode's question being answered in public, three ways: for whom (risk tiers: Massachusetts' state-employee plan, Medicare's Bridge), on what conditions (a program: a third of covering employers, the federal plan), at what price (the bargained deals that make coverage affordable, or their absence, which makes employers leave).

Script (structural): "This year, the plan you get through work had to pick a rule for these drugs. Some pay for the people at highest risk. A third of the ones still paying want you in a program first. About one in seven big employers say they've stopped or will. And Medicare and the federal workers' plan made the same split, in public." Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

The drugs

  • They work while you take them. After 36 weeks on tirzepatide (20.9% average loss), people kept on it lost a further 5.5% over the next year; people switched to placebo regained 14.0% (SURMOUNT-4, JAMA 2024). In the full STEP 1 trial, semaglutide gave 14.9% loss vs 2.4% on placebo at 68 weeks (STEP 1 extension paper).
  • Stop, and in the trials most of it comes back. In the STEP 1 extension, two-thirds of the loss returned within a year of stopping and the heart and metabolic gains reverted (same source). A 2026 meta-analysis projects cardiometabolic markers back at baseline within about 1.4 years of stopping (BMJ 2026).
  • In people with established heart disease and no diabetes, semaglutide cut major heart events from 8.0% to 6.5%, and that benefit did not track how much weight people lost (SELECT, NEJM 2023; Lancet 202501375-3)).
  • Real-world staying power is rising. For the weight-loss-labelled drugs, the share still on them a year later rose from 33.2% (2021 starters) to 62.6% (early-2024 starters), which Prime ties mostly to the end of shortages (Prime Therapeutics). In a 125,474-person cohort, 64.8% of people without diabetes stopped within a year (JAMA Network Open).
  • Cost is the top reason people stop: 47.6% of stoppers in one health system cited cost or insurance (Obesity 2025).

The money

  • It does not pay for itself. CBO: Medicare coverage would cost $38.8B in drugs over 2026–2034 against $3.4B in savings elsewhere, about 9 cents back per dollar, and "CBO is not aware of any direct evidence" that treating obesity with these drugs reduces other medical spending (CBO). ICER's lifetime model gets more back (about 37–41 cents per extra drug dollar, our arithmetic) and still finds it costs more than it saves.
  • It is still good value per year of health gained. ICER's council rated both drugs high long-term value at Q1 2025 net prices, which sit below ICER's value-based price ranges (ICER, Dec 2025).
  • And unaffordable for everyone eligible. Fewer than 1% of the 92 million eligible US adults could be treated within five years before crossing ICER's $880M-a-year affordability test, and that holds even at $245 a month (same source).
  • Price is the hinge. EBRI's simulation: covering the drugs raises employer premiums 5.3–13.8% at real-world net prices ($617–766 a month), and 1–3.9% at a hypothetical $200 (EBRI, 2025). In real employer plans, coverage added $9.23 per plan member per month at 2025 prices (Shahzad et al., JAMA Health Forum 2026).
  • There are three prices, never one. List (Wegovy roughly $1,350 a month now, per a price tracker and the White House's 2025 figure, approximate; $675 from 2027-01-01, Novo's announcement), net (what insurers pay after rebates; ICER used $6,829 a year for semaglutide, Q1 2025), and cash ($349 Wegovy from Novo; $299–$449 Zepbound from Lilly, conditional). Novo says its list cut does not change self-pay prices; Mercer expects it to change employers' net cost little (Novo; Mercer).

The rules

  • Medicare's exclusion of weight-loss drugs is statute; the Bridge works around it as a demonstration (42 U.S.C. 1395w-102; 42 U.S.C. 1396r-8).
  • Self-funded employer plans are outside state insurance mandates (ERISA), and the ACA's essential-benefits rule reaches only individual and small-group insurance; the 2027 federal marketplace rule adds nothing on these drugs (29 U.S.C. 1144; 42 U.S.C. 300gg-6; Fed. Reg. 2026-10050). So for most viewers with job-based coverage, the setting is chosen by the employer, not the state.
  • A pharmacy-benefit manager's formulary is a template. CVS Caremark's own words: "Plan sponsors … retain discretion to customize coverage for their members."

Dropping coverage does not stop use. About one in four GLP-1 users (27%) had insurance but paid the full price themselves, and 56% found the drugs hard to afford (KFF poll, Nov 2025).

Contested (the real fight)

GREEN — "Pay for the people at risk, at a bargained price." The drug's best evidence is in the people green targets: SELECT's heart benefit was in people with established heart disease, and it did not depend on how much weight they lost, so a plan buying health should buy it where the risk is. Price is what makes coverage affordable (EBRI: 5.3–13.8% on premiums at today's net price, 1–3.9% at $200), and the buyers that limit who qualifies are the ones with a price on the table: Medicare's $245 came with risk tiers, NICE's approval rests on a commercial arrangement with the maker, and Canada's agency said coverage would need a price about 67% lower. Once people qualify, green keeps them on it with a small flat copay (the Bridge's is $50), because the trials say stopping throws most of the benefit away and the top reason people stop is cost (47.6%). Green does not ignore lifestyle: the Bridge requires the prescriber to certify that the drug comes with diet and exercise. It just doesn't make the patient prove it a second time.

GOLD — "Lifestyle first, then the drug": pay for the program, not just the prescription. Both drugs' labels pair them with a reduced-calorie diet and more physical activity. Even Medicare's Bridge pays only when the drug comes "in combination with current and ongoing lifestyle modification", and the trial that paired the drug with real therapy (STEP 3, 30 counselling visits) got 16.0% loss. A signature on a form is not a program. OPM wrote that "attestation statements alone" should not bypass the requirement, and gold's design pays for the therapy as well as the drug. Lifestyle programmes are worth buying on their own: in the Diabetes Prevention Program one cut new diabetes by 58%, beating a drug, and the Task Force rates them grade B with harms "small to none". Gold filters on a program under way, not on a risk tier, so nobody is shut out for being one BMI point short of a line. And gold is honest about the money: CBO finds about 9 cents back per dollar, ICER says fewer than 1% of eligible people can be treated within its affordability test even at $245, and OPM names GLP-1 spending among the costs behind a 10.9% rise in what federal workers pay. If a plan is going to pay roughly what a worker pays toward a year of family coverage for one person's drug, it is fair to ask that the money buy the whole treatment the label describes, program included. Gold adds a fair point from the record: after stopping, weight comes back more slowly after behavioural programmes than after drugs (BMJ 2026).

The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:

  • Gold at green: the Bridge has no outcome record: it is three months old, CMS has published no uptake, and it ends 2027-12-31 with nothing scheduled after. Green's price is not the viewer's price: $245 is a Medicare deal; the net price insurers paid in early 2025 was about $569 a month (ICER's figure, from SSR Health), and Caremark's new deal discloses no price at all. The rules leave people out: KFF estimates fewer than half of those meeting the clinical criteria are eligible once the other exclusions apply (3.8 million of 9.7 million), and the $50 gets no low-income help. Green's lifestyle condition is paper: an attestation, which is exactly what OPM refused to accept.
  • Green at gold: no study anywhere shows the requirement works: not one GLP-1 study tests a coverage condition. The nearest record cuts the other way: insurer-required diets before bariatric surgery predicted dropout and weight gain, and a trade-press account of a second study found no clinical benefit. STEP 3 tested drug plus therapy, not therapy as a gate: everyone in it got both. A gate rations by who can attend, and cost and insurance hurdles are already the top reason people stop. A program gate cannot screen out a "shortcut": in STEP 1 and STEP 3 both arms got the same programme, and the drug is what changed the result (12–27 in 100 reached a 10% loss without it, 69–75 with it); never phrase this as anyone's effort or willpower. SURMOUNT-4 did not test behavioural therapy at all, and still found 89.5% of people who stayed on the drug kept most of their loss.

Where the record leans, said plainly. On the filter, the record leans green: risk tiers aim at the people with trial outcomes behind them, and the program requirement has no outcome record while the nearest analogue counts against it. On the price, the record leans gold: green's design works at a bargained price that most viewers' plans cannot get, and neither design pays for itself. Equal length on "documented failures" would misrepresent this: gold's requirement is untested, and green's own program is too new to have results.

The debate people actually have: is it a shortcut?

Before any design, this is the question viewers ask: "Why should my plan pay for something a lot of people could handle with diet and exercise? Is this a shortcut? Are people abusing it?" It is gold's gut objection, and it gets asked on air in those words. Hand pass D3 tested it against the record. Here is what the record says, and where it lands.

Diet and exercise work, and they are worth paying for. In the Diabetes Prevention Program a lifestyle programme (a 7% weight-loss goal, 150 minutes of activity a week) cut new diabetes by 58%, and beat a drug, metformin (31%); ten years on, with weight partly regained, the reduction was 34%. The US Preventive Services Task Force tells clinicians to offer intensive behavioural programmes to adults with BMI 30+ (grade B), with harms "small to none".

But they rarely reach, or hold, the losses the drug trials count. A large, intensive lifestyle trial, Look AHEAD (5,145 adults with type 2 diabetes; group sessions, then years of individual counselling; an individual goal of 10%), got 37.7% of people to a 10% loss at one year. At eight years 26.9% had 10% off, against 17.2% in a group that got education classes only; of those who reached 10% in year one, 39.3% still had it in year eight. In the Task Force's evidence review, behavioural programmes beat controls by about 2.4 kg at 12–18 months (67 studies). A 29-study meta-analysis found more than 80% of lost weight regained by five years (the average person still kept more than 3 kg off). A national survey found 17.3% of adults who were ever overweight report keeping 10% off for a year (self-reported).

In the drug trials, both arms got the same programme. This is the fact that answers the "shortcut" question. The placebo groups were not left to sit still; they got the same diet-and-exercise support as the drug groups:

  • STEP 1 (lifestyle support in both arms): 12.0% on placebo reached a 10% loss, against 69.1% on semaglutide (68 weeks).
  • STEP 3 (30 counselling visits and an initial low-calorie diet in both arms): 27.0% on placebo reached 10%, against 75.3% on the drug.
  • SURMOUNT-1 (tirzepatide): 50–57% on the two top doses lost 20% or more, against 3% on placebo.

So 12 to 27 in 100 of the people doing the programme without the drug reached a 10% loss, and 69 to 75 in 100 doing the same programme with it. The line for the script: "The drug is not a way around diet and exercise. In the trials, both groups did the same programme; the drug is what changed the result." ("Both arms made the same effort" is also accurate, but the word "effort" stays off air: say "the same programme".)

Why weight comes back is largely biology. A 2018 review (Hall and Kahan) estimates that for every kilogram lost, appetite rises by about 100 calories a day above where it started, while the body burns about 20–30 fewer; hormonal changes that raise appetite persist. The 2025 Lancet commission on obesity names the "personal responsibility" frame as a source of bias, and most of the public already agrees: 65% in a Pew survey say willpower alone is usually not enough to lose weight and keep it off.

Two caveats that cut the other way, and they travel with the above.

  • Look AHEAD's lifestyle programme did not reduce heart attacks, strokes or cardiovascular deaths; the trial was stopped early for futility. That is not proof the drug would have: SELECT (Settled) is a different population (established heart disease, no diabetes). Never set the two side by side as one comparison.
  • The drug works only while it is taken (Settled: SURMOUNT-4; the STEP 1 extension). On the drug, the loss holds: SELECT's four-year figure is −10.2% against −1.5% on placebo.

The disease label, both halves. The American Medical Association has called obesity a disease since

  1. The 2025 Lancet commission draws a line: clinical obesity (excess fat already affecting organs

or daily function) is an illness and should get equitable access to treatment; preclinical obesity (excess fat, organs still working) is a risk state that gets counselling and monitoring. Both are true; say both. The commission's split is, in effect, a medical-need filter, which is green's shape.

"Are people abusing it?" — said precisely.

  • A 2026 study of health records found prescriptions to adults with no documented FDA-approved reason rose 15-fold from 2021 to 2025 (0.1% → 1.5% of such adults; about 1.1 million people in all), and 35.1% of them had a normal BMI. That prescribing skews to people in better-off areas and with private insurance. Its limits are part of the fact: the records cannot say why a drug was prescribed, missing documentation is not proof there was no reason, compounded products were not captured, and the study cannot show who paid.
  • A secret-shopper study (in JAMA, as reported by AJMC; APPROXIMATE, attribute it) found most online sellers prescribed, often within a day, and only about half asked about eating disorders; the shopper met the eligibility criteria.
  • FDA has received hundreds of adverse-event reports tied to compounded versions (990 semaglutide, more than 730 tirzepatide, to May 31, 2026), says these are likely undercounted, and warns of counterfeit Ozempic in the US.
  • None of this is evidence about members whose plan pays under its criteria. And no evidence was found of people gaining weight to qualify for coverage (Off air).

What the public thinks. In KFF's 2024 poll, 61% said Medicare should cover these drugs for weight loss, and views stayed largely unchanged after people heard the arguments for and against, including that premiums could rise. In Pew's 2024 survey, 53% of those who had heard of the drugs called them a good option for people with obesity or a weight-related condition; 12% for people without. No poll was found on whether employer plans should cover them.

Where the record lands, said plainly (no false balance). The "shortcut" intuition does not survive the trials. Diet and exercise are worth paying for, and both designs already require them, but for most people they do not reach or hold a 10% loss, and in the trials the drug made the difference for people doing the same programme. The cost worry is real (Settled: the drug does not pay for itself), and a narrower misuse worry is real (prescriptions with no documented reason, thin online screening, an unsafe compounded supply), but neither is evidence about members whose plan pays under its criteria. This does not decide the design fight on its own: it removes the "shortcut" reason for a program gate, and leaves gold's real reasons (the label's pairing, STEP 3, the money) standing to be tested.

Heart failure, the strongest unapproved case. In STEP-HFpEF (529 people with this kind of heart failure and BMI 30+, no diabetes), semaglutide improved a patient-reported symptom score by 7.8 points more than placebo and the six-minute walk by 20.3 metres (21.5 m against 1.2 m); a companion trial in people with diabetes found +7.3 points and +14.3 m. Pooled across four trials, worsening heart-failure events were 2.8% against 4.7%, with no significant effect on cardiovascular death alone. In SUMMIT (tirzepatide, 731 people), cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure occurred in 9.9% against 15.3% on placebo over about two years. Even so, neither drug is approved for it: FDA added SELECT's heart-failure numbers to Wegovy's label with the note that an effect "has not been established", and Lilly said it withdrew its US application after FDA asked for another trial. No threshold for what walking distance is clinically meaningful was verified; do not call 20 metres "small" or "large".

Joints and inflammation. In STEP 9 (knee osteoarthritis, BMI 30+, 407 people), knee pain scores fell 41.7 points against 27.5 on placebo: trial evidence, not an approved use. Inflammation is a lab marker, not an outcome: CRP fell 43.5% against 7.3% in STEP-HFpEF. Rheumatoid arthritis and fibromyalgia rest on one chart review and one records study each; say "early, not tested in a randomised trial".

Why it matters for coverage: the same pen is covered or not depending on why it is written. Medicare's statute excludes drugs "when used for" weight loss; it excludes a use, not a molecule. So today the same GLP-1 goes through a Medicare member's regular Part D plan when it is prescribed for diabetes, sleep apnea, MASH or (Wegovy) heart-risk reduction, and through the $50 Bridge only when it is written for weight. An employer plan can draw the same line in plain words: Massachusetts' state-employee plan says "Medicines used only for weight loss will no longer be covered", while coverage may be approved when the doctor shows the medicine is "being used to treat that condition". Heart failure, arthritis and fibromyalgia are not FDA indications, so a person on the drug for those benefits is, for coverage purposes, on it for weight (the Bridge uses BMI 30+ with this kind of heart failure as one of its weight tiers) or not covered. Whether commercial plans pay for off-label uses was not established (Off air).

The reframe

The shouting asks "cover it or not?" The record says four things that question misses.

  1. Everybody is already rationing. Some employer plans ration by risk tier, a third of covering employers by a program, others by price, and the ones who "dropped" it ration by wallet; Medicare and the federal plan made the same split in public. "Cover it or not" is not the choice anyone is making. The choice is the filter.
  2. "Does it pay for itself?" is the wrong test. CBO and ICER agree it does not, and ICER still calls it good value per year of health gained. The honest question is what it buys and who pays, not whether it is free.
  3. Dropping coverage moves the bill; it does not delete it. One in four users with insurance already pays full price, about one in ten weekly Wegovy prescriptions went through cash channels in October 2025, and 17% of large employers plan direct-to-employer arrangements for these drugs in 2027. The real variable is price × how long people stay on it, and every design is a bet on both.
  4. The same pen is covered or not depending on why it is written. Coverage rules exclude a use, not a molecule: Medicare's statute excludes drugs "when used for" weight loss, and Massachusetts' state-employee plan dropped medicines "used only for weight loss" while keeping them for diagnosed conditions. The same drug is approved for diabetes, heart-attack and stroke risk, kidney disease, sleep apnea and a liver disease, and has trial evidence (not approval) in heart failure and knee arthritis. So "should the plan pay for weight-loss drugs?" is really "should the plan pay when the reason on the prescription is weight?" — and the line between weight and the illnesses it drives is exactly what green's risk tiers try to draw.

Both sides quietly agree

Both designs already require diet and exercise alongside the drug. The Bridge makes the prescriber certify "current and ongoing lifestyle modification"; the federal plan makes a program provider certify participation. Medicare.gov even says "Your provider must certify that you're using the GLP-1 drug as part of a lifestyle program." The fight is not over whether lifestyle matters. It is a signature versus a certificate, and nobody has tested whether the certificate changes anything.

And both are betting on the same thing: that the people they pay for stay on the drug. Green's flat copay and gold's program are two different theories of persistence, and the trials say the money spent on someone who stops in the first year mostly buys weight that comes back.

Verdict framing

"The design that survives both teams' objections, and the dial you still have to set." Candidates, each to be earned in the debate and dropped if it is not:

  • Survives: pay by risk, at a bargained price, with the program covered and offered, not required. Green's filter survives because it is the one with outcomes behind it. Gold's program survives as a benefit (the federal plan already must cover up to 12–22 sessions) but not as a gate, because the gate has no record. Gold's price discipline survives: coverage at today's net price costs everyone's premium, so the price must be bargained, not assumed.
  • Fell: "It's a shortcut around diet and exercise" (in the trials both arms got the same programme; the drug changed the result). "It pays for itself" (CBO and ICER both say no). "A program requirement makes the drug work better" (untested; the nearest record cuts against it). "Medicare's $245 price is available to your plan" (it is a Medicare deal). "Dropping coverage saves the money" (the bill moves to the user).
  • The one dial the viewer sets — the judge names it, the judge does not set it: WHERE THE LINE SITS. How far down the risk ladder your employer's plan should pay, knowing each rung costs everyone's premium. Any employer plan can write any of these lines into its prior authorization; the named settings are real-world proof points, each with a real institution and a documented weakness: 1. BMI 40 and four conditions — NHS England's first cohort. Weakness: at least 220,000 of 3.4 million eligible funded in three years; up to 12 years to reach the rest. 2. BMI 35, or lower with specific conditions — Medicare's Bridge; NICE (35 plus one condition). Weakness: an end date with nothing after it; fewer than half of those meeting the clinical criteria are eligible once the other exclusions apply (KFF: 3.8 million of 9.7 million). 3. BMI 27 with an FDA-approved condition — Massachusetts' state-employee plan since July 2026. Weakness: it took a 10–7 vote, and obesity-only prescriptions lost coverage. 4. Anyone it is approved for — the federal employees' plan (behind its program requirement). Weakness: OPM names GLP-1 spending among the drivers of a 10.9% rise in what enrollees pay. 5. No one, for weight loss — North Carolina's state plan since 2024. Weakness: one in four users already pays full price anyway, and NC's own Medicaid reversed a drop within ten weeks.

The research earned the price tag on each rung: EBRI's 5.3–13.8% vs 1–3.9%, and ICER's "under 1%". It did not earn a right answer. That is the viewer's call.

Make it Matter — the viewer's own decision this fall

The episode is about a choice the viewer's own job-based plan makes at its next renewal, and what the viewer finds out at open enrollment. It is not an abstract policy, and it is not about Medicare.

  • The story, ep21-style: one worker, one email, one drug, three possible answers. A worker (unnamed, invented, never a real or identifiable person) opens the open-enrollment email from HR. The drug is the same in every version; the answer depends on which rule the employer picked: 1. "Covered if your BMI and health conditions qualify; your doctor sends a prior authorization." Green's shape. Real instance: Massachusetts' state-employee plan (BMI 27+ with an FDA-approved condition); at national scale, Medicare's Bridge. 2. "Covered once you're enrolled in our lifestyle program, and while you stay in it. The sessions are covered too." Gold's shape. Real instance: a third of employers that cover the drugs; at national scale, the federal employees' plan from 2027. 3. "No longer covered for weight loss. Here's a discount program." Real instance: 14% of BGH's large employers; the outside-the-plan subsidy at a $449 cash price (vendor announcements). The episode walks the worker through each answer: who gets in, what it costs them, what it costs everyone's premium, and what the record says about each. The "is it a shortcut?" question is the worker's coworker's question, asked once and answered with the trial arms; the worker's effort or willpower is never the subject.
  • What to check in your own plan (coverage, never medical advice): whether the drug list excludes weight-loss use; what conditions sit on it (BMI and conditions, a program); whether a "discount program" outside the plan counts toward your deductible (nobody has published that for the new employer deals); and whether the reason on the prescription (diabetes, sleep apnea, heart disease) puts it under a different rule (see "The drug's other uses").
  • The picture: one drug, three prices this month: roughly $1,350 list (approximate), $349–449 cash, and what plans pay after rebates (undisclosed; ICER's early-2025 estimate is about $570 a month). The comparator is the viewer's own premium contribution (below).
  • Medicare and federal viewers, one line: the Bridge runs through 2027 and is not a plan you pick (Medicare open enrollment Oct 15 – Dec 7; CMS has not said what happens if you switch Part D plan), and every federal carrier requires a certified program from 2027 (open season Nov 9 – Dec 14; read your carrier's brochure).

Script traps

From the pitch review and hand pass D3 (2026-10-06)

  • Never "effort". Gold is "lifestyle first, then the drug" / "pay for the program". Never "your effort", never "who tries", never a line that implies people with obesity lack effort or willpower. The record says the opposite (both trial arms got the same programme; Pew's 65%; the Lancet commission on the "personal responsibility" frame).
  • Centre the job-based plan. Each design is a rule an employer plan picks. Medicare and the federal plan are one-line proof, never the frame of a scene.
  • Never "abuse". Say "no documented reason" (the study's own limit: missing documentation is not proof of no reason).
  • Never "your premiums pay for cosmetic use". The no-documented-reason study is associated with private insurance but cannot show who paid.
  • Never say people gain weight to qualify. No evidence was found; if raised, say exactly that.
  • Heart failure is not an approved use for either drug: Wegovy's label says an effect "has not been established"; Lilly said it withdrew its US application (attribute it). Say "trial evidence, not approved". Same for knee arthritis. Alcohol use, rheumatoid arthritis and fibromyalgia are "early".
  • Never set Look AHEAD against SELECT as one comparison ("lifestyle didn't cut heart attacks, the drug did"): different populations (type 2 diabetes vs established heart disease without diabetes).
  • Look AHEAD's people all had type 2 diabetes. Say so when its figures are used.
  • Don't state a weight-loss threshold that "moves health" as a fact. No row verifies one; say "the 10% and 15% marks the trials count".
  • The 17.3% "kept 10% off" is self-reported; trial figures are measured. On screen, label it.
  • MASH approval is accelerated: "approved, pending a confirmatory trial".
  • Rybelsus and Mounjaro heart-risk dates are approximate (FDA letters not retrievable): show "approved" without a date unless the dates are accepted at review.
  • Online-seller figures are APPROXIMATE (JAMA via AJMC): attribute, direction only ("most sites prescribed, often within a day"), no percentage on air.
  • The disease label, both halves: AMA (2013) calls obesity a disease; the Lancet commission calls preclinical obesity a risk state, not an illness. Never only one.
  • Polls: KFF's 61% is about Medicare, in 2024; no poll on employer coverage was found. Pew's 53% is among people who had heard of the drugs.
  • No medical advice, nothing on whether anyone should take it. Never suggest a viewer could qualify for, start, stop or switch a drug, or use one for heart failure, arthritis, fibromyalgia or alcohol.
  • No named or identifiable private individuals. People the team knows inform the script's instincts, not its facts; the worker in the story is invented and unnamed.

Figures that must travel with their caveat

  • The Bridge is not "Medicare covers weight-loss drugs." It is a temporary CMS demonstration outside Part D; the statute still excludes weight-loss drugs. Say "a temporary Medicare program".
  • Never say "no successor". Say "nothing is scheduled after December 31, 2027". If BALANCE comes, its designed copay is $50 or $125 depending on plan type, not a flat $50.
  • $245 is what the maker nets, not what anyone pays at the counter. The enrollee pays $50. Never say "Medicare pays the pharmacy $195".
  • Bridge drugs are Wegovy, Zepbound KwikPen and Foundayo, not Ozempic or Mounjaro.
  • Heart disease does not exclude from the Bridge; diabetes, moderate-to-severe sleep apnea and MASH with fibrosis do.
  • The Bridge has a lifestyle condition too, by attestation. Never present it as "no strings".
  • 2027 Medicare semaglutide price: $274 per 30-day equivalent = $276.78 per Ozempic pen = $385.63 for four Wegovy pens, Part D-covered uses only. Do not say which price Medicare "pays" in 2027 (unresolved).
  • FEHB: 12–22 is sessions plans must cover, not a hurdle. The requirement is documented, provider-certified participation. "In-person or virtual" both count; do not say "apps don't count"; do not say current users are or are not grandfathered.
  • 10.9% is the rise in the enrollee's share of premiums (total premium: 9.3%), and OPM gives no GLP-1 share of it. Say "one of the costs OPM names", never "GLP-1s drove a 10.9% rise". Never "third straight double-digit year". Never "ballooning adoption" as OPM's words.
  • BGH 14%: quote BGH's own words ("already eliminated coverage… or plan to do so in 2027"), source it to BGH, never add "up from 10%". 72% → 60% is 2025 → 2026, large employers only. Use 8.7 million (US), not 11 million (global). "Not a single employer will add coverage" is a reported spoken quote; BGH's written line is that non-covering employers are "unlikely to add coverage in the future".
  • Never put BGH (72→60%), Mercer (49%) and IFEBP (36%) on one trend line: three surveys, three universes.
  • Caremark: a template, not a mandate; no price, no "10–15% savings", no "$25 copay". The $25 is a manufacturer coupon with caps that does nothing for someone whose plan excludes the drug.
  • The outside-the-plan subsidy rests on vendor announcements and one employer. Do not imply the spending counts toward a deductible or how it is taxed. $449 is also the self-pay price for the maintenance doses if refilled within 45 days; miss a refill and LillyDirect's price is $499–$699.
  • Trial regain vs real-world regain: keep "in the trials". A real-world cohort found +0.5% a year after stopping, because 19.6% restarted and 35.2% switched treatment. Do not say "losing coverage causes regain" as a measured fact.
  • STEP 1 extension also withdrew the lifestyle programme, and net loss at 120 weeks was still 5.6%: "two-thirds regained", never "all". SURMOUNT-4 randomised only people who tolerated 36 weeks of the drug (113 of 783 stopped first): its 89.5% is a best case.
  • SELECT is people with established heart disease and no diabetes; that use already goes through Part D, outside the Bridge. Side-effect stopping was 16.6% vs 8.2%. Novo-funded.
  • Persistence: never air Prime's 8.1% three-year figure as today's rate. Spoken: "for people starting in early 2024, about six in ten were still on it a year later; for 2021 starters, about one in three."
  • ICER vs CBO is a horizon-and-persistence difference, not a contradiction. ICER warns its persistence "may be higher than in real-world settings". Spoken: "worth the money per year of healthy life, but not self-funding, and unaffordable to give everyone eligible."
  • "Under 1%" is of 92 million eligible US adults, at ICER's $880M-a-year threshold; manufacturers call the threshold "artificial". Say "ICER's affordability test", not "the budget".
  • $9.23 is per plan member per month (about $111 a year), every member, user or not, one insurer's plans, priced at ICER's net estimate. Cite Shahzad et al., not "Chernew".
  • EBRI is a simulation, not observed premiums.
  • 27%: "about one in four GLP-1 users had insurance but paid the full price themselves", never "a quarter of insured users".
  • Conditions: the honest line is "no study has tested whether these conditions make the drug work better or save money; what we know is that hurdles cut how many people get it." The Connecticut ~90% adherence is a vendor's uncontrolled report: not evidence that gates work.
  • Who is left out: the Boston gap (Black 49%, Hispanic 47% less likely) is one clinic, a three-month window, and narrowed after Medicaid began covering. The US income signal on stopping is for people with diabetes; the obesity-only income signal is Danish and area-level (APPROXIMATE, not on air as a figure).
  • Massachusetts is a partial drop: diabetes and BMI 27+ with an FDA-approved condition stay covered. "22,000 members lost coverage" overstates it.
  • NC: never merge the State Health Plan (still out) with NC Medicaid (dropped, then reinstated 2025-12-12). The $170M is a projection. No vote tally.
  • Starbucks and Cigna are secondary-only: if named, "reported", with the month (October 2026; July 2026 for Cigna's own staff).
  • ACA and state rules reach only some viewers. Colorado's 2027 law makes carriers offer drug coverage as a buy-up, not include it. Do not name Maryland. Courts "have so far dismissed" exclusion challenges (from a legal summary; the opinion was not opened).
  • Canada's figures are Canadian dollars; its recommendation is final (July 2025), not a draft. NHS England starts at BMI 40 with four conditions.
  • Cash prices move: re-check LillyDirect and NovoCare before render.
