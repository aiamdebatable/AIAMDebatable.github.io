Weight-Loss Drug Coverage: Should Your Health Plan Pay, and for Whom?

Should your health plan pay for weight-loss drugs, and for whom, at what price?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

Same pen, three rules: pay by risk, a program first, or not at all. Two AI teams each build one; you set the line.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in October 2026, workers were heading into open enrollment, and employers were picking different rules for the same drug. In one survey of big employers, 14% had dropped coverage for weight loss or planned to in 2027, and about a third of those still paying wanted members in a program first.

Settled

What both sides accept

The drug works while you take it, and in the trials most of the weight comes back when you stop. It doesn't pay for itself. And it isn't a shortcut: in the trials both groups did the same diet-and-exercise program, and the drug changed the result.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesPay by risk

Pay for the people whose weight is already harming their health, checked before the first fill. In people with heart disease, the drug cut major heart events from 8% to 6.5%, and that benefit didn't track weight lost. Bargain the price and keep the copay small, because cost is the top reason people stop.

▶ Hear Green at 3:32 on YouTube
Gold arguesLifestyle first

Lifestyle first, then the drug: the label pairs it with diet and more activity, and a signature on a form isn't a program. When one person's drug costs a plan about $6,800 a year, the plan should buy the whole treatment, program included. And under this rule nobody one BMI point short falls off a line.

▶ Hear Gold at 10:04 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

Almost no plan is choosing yes or no; the fight is over what earns you the drug, your medical need or a program under way. The design that survives both teams: pay by risk, at a bargained price, with the program covered and offered but not required. What's left is where the line sits, knowing every step down costs everyone's premium.

The verdict

What survives both teams
Pay by risk, at a bargained price, with the program covered and offered, but not required. Green's line, gold's price discipline, and gold's program as a benefit, not a gate.
What didn't hold up
It's a shortcut. It pays for itself. A program requirement makes the drug work better: untested. And dropping coverage saves the money: about one in four users had insurance and still paid full price.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: How far down that line should your plan pay?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1A BMI of forty with four conditions
    What it buys
    Pays first for the people at the highest risk, like England's first rollout, which pairs its line with a commercial deal on price with the maker. A risk line aims at the people with trial outcomes behind them.
    What it costs
    It reaches a small share of those eligible in its first three years: England plans to fund at least 220,000 of about 3.4 million eligible people in that time, and everyone eligible only within 12 years.
    Not measured
    Whether a risk line keeps people healthier or saves money: no study tests any coverage rule, and these lines are too new to have results.

    On the record: row 14.2 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 14.3 of the fact-check log, row 14.1 of the fact-check log, row 11.1 of the fact-check log

  2. 2Thirty-five, or lower with conditions
    What it buys
    Medicare's Bridge: a BMI of 35, or 30 or 27 with certain conditions, at a bargained price of $245 a month net and a $50 copay once you qualify. A small copay matters, because cost is the top reason people stop.
    What it costs
    It ends in 2027, and what follows it is uncertain. Its bargained price is a Medicare deal, not your plan's. Its lifestyle condition is a signature on a form, not a program. And fewer than half of those meeting its clinical criteria are eligible.
    Not measured
    How many people the Bridge has reached: Medicare hasn't published uptake.

    On the record: row 2.5 of the fact-check log, row 2.2 of the fact-check log, row 1.4 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 2.15 of the fact-check log, row 9.7 of the fact-check log

  3. 3Twenty-seven with an approved condition
    What it buys
    Keeps paying for people with a BMI of 27 or more and an FDA-approved condition, like Massachusetts' plan for state workers. The same drug is covered when the doctor shows it's being used to treat a condition.
    What it costs
    It dropped weight alone, so people taking the drug only for weight lose coverage, and the line still shuts out the person one BMI point short.
    Not measured
    Whether people dropped from coverage cost the plan more later in other care: no study has measured that.

    On the record: row 6.7 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 16.55 of the fact-check log, row 10.17 of the fact-check log, row 5.27 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected)

  4. 4Anyone it's approved for
    What it buys
    Nobody falls off a line: the drug is covered for its approved use. The federal plan also pays for the program, up to 12 to 22 sessions a year.
    What it costs
    At today's net price, coverage costs every member's premium: one person's drug runs about $6,800 a year, about what the average worker pays toward a year of family coverage. In the federal plan, these drugs are among the costs behind a nearly 11% rise in what members pay toward premiums in 2027. And that plan makes the drug wait on a certified program, which rations by who can get to the sessions.
    Not measured
    Whether requiring a program improves results or saves money: no study has tested it, and the nearest record, from weight-loss surgery, cuts the other way.

    On the record: row 1.6 of the fact-check log, row 4.3 of the fact-check log, row 1.8 of the fact-check log, row 15.2 of the fact-check log, row 11.1 of the fact-check log

  5. 5No one
    What it buys
    Takes the drug off the plan's bill for weight loss, like North Carolina's state plan. Some employers chip in at the pharmacy counter instead, on a pen sold at a set price of $449; that isn't a discount, and the help is whatever the employer adds.
    What it costs
    The cost doesn't go away; it moves, to the people still taking it. About one in four users had insurance and still paid full price, and more than half of users say the drugs are hard to afford.
    Not measured
    Whether those counter deals help: they are the companies' own announcements with no results published, and nobody has said whether that spending counts toward your deductible.

    On the record: row 9.8 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 9.9 of the fact-check log, row 5.22 of the fact-check log, row 5.25 of the fact-check log, row 10.13 of the fact-check log

Wherever the line sits: The drug works while you take it, and in the trials most of it comes back when you stop. Cost is the top reason people stop.
