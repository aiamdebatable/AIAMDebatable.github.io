Weight-Loss Drug Coverage: Should Your Health Plan Pay, and for Whom?

The question: Should your health plan pay for weight-loss drugs, and for whom, at what price?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

Same pen, three rules: pay by risk, a program first, or not at all. Two AI teams each build one; you set the line.

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in October 2026, workers were heading into open enrollment, and employers were picking different rules for the same drug. In one survey of big employers, 14% had dropped coverage for weight loss or planned to in 2027, and about a third of those still paying wanted members in a program first.

The fight

Green arguesPay by risk

Pay for the people whose weight is already harming their health, checked before the first fill. In people with heart disease, the drug cut major heart events from 8% to 6.5%, and that benefit didn't track weight lost. Bargain the price and keep the copay small, because cost is the top reason people stop.

Gold arguesLifestyle first

Lifestyle first, then the drug: the label pairs it with diet and more activity, and a signature on a form isn't a program. When one person's drug costs a plan about $6,800 a year, the plan should buy the whole treatment, program included. And under this rule nobody one BMI point short falls off a line.

What both sides quietly agree on

The drug works while you take it, and in the trials most of the weight comes back when you stop. It doesn't pay for itself. And it isn't a shortcut: in the trials both groups did the same diet-and-exercise program, and the drug changed the result.

What the shouting gets wrong

Almost no plan is choosing yes or no; the fight is over what earns you the drug, your medical need or a program under way. The design that survives both teams: pay by risk, at a bargained price, with the program covered and offered but not required. What's left is where the line sits, knowing every step down costs everyone's premium.

The verdict

Walt This one doesn't resolve, and no design wins it. Three things from me. What's settled, what survived, and the dial that's yours.

Walt Both teams accepted the same record. The drug works while you take it, and in the trials most of it comes back when you stop. It doesn't pay for itself. And it isn't a shortcut: in the trials, both groups did the same program, and the drug changed the result.

Walt What survived from green. The filter. Risk tiers aim at the people with trial outcomes behind them, while the program requirement has no record of its own. And the small copay, because cost is the top reason people stop.

Walt What survived from gold. The price. At today's net price, coverage costs every member's premium, and green's bargained price is a Medicare deal, not your plan's. And the program, as a benefit: a signature isn't a program.

Walt What fell. It's a shortcut. It pays for itself. A program requirement makes the drug work better: untested. And dropping coverage saves the money: about one in four users had insurance and still paid full price.

Walt So here's the design that survives both teams. Pay by risk, at a bargained price, with the program covered and offered, but not required. Green's line, gold's price discipline, and gold's program as a benefit, not a gate.

Walt That leaves one dial neither team can set for you: where the line sits. A BMI of forty with four conditions, like England's first rollout, which reaches a small share of those eligible in its first three years. Thirty-five, or lower with conditions, like Medicare's Bridge, which ends in twenty twenty-seven. Twenty-seven with an approved condition, like Massachusetts, which dropped weight alone.

Walt Anyone it's approved for, like the federal plan, where these drugs are among the costs behind higher premiums. Or no one, like North Carolina's state plan, which leaves the whole bill to the people still taking it. So how far down that line should your plan pay? That one's yours.
