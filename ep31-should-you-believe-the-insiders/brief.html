Episode 31 · The research

Research brief — AI Insiders Say It Might Kill Us: Should You Believe Them?

The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video. Every figure and quotation here is in the fact-check log.

The question

When the people building AI say it might end us, how much of that is knowledge, how much is fear, and how much is marketing, and how would you tell? The number itself can't be checked: no one can verify a chance of extinction. Everything around the number can be. So the episode never asks "is it 10%?". It asks how an ordinary viewer should weigh an insider's alarm.

Why now

On 2026-10-03 the man who led the writing of OpenAI's launch safety reports went public in The Atlantic with "I Quit OpenAI Because Its Culture Is Broken". He calls himself part of "a parade of former colleagues—at OpenAI and the industry's other leaders—who have decided that the current path is unacceptable." It came two days after OpenAI said it had parted ways with three staff, at least two of them safety and alignment researchers, and four weeks after an Anthropic researcher resigned in public, saying "This is not a marketing stunt." The essay gives no probability. It cites only incidents the labs have themselves made public, at OpenAI and at Anthropic. And it discloses that after quitting he hired a PR firm. OpenAI replied the same day that "we pause training or hold back models when we need to slow down".

method, and names roles, not people: "the man who led the writing of OpenAI's safety reports", "an Anthropic researcher", "Anthropic's alignment science lead".

Settled (not the debate)

  • What the experts say, as a baseline. When AI Impacts surveyed about fifteen hundred published AI researchers in December 2024, the typical answer put the chance that AI causes human extinction or a similarly permanent loss of control over our future at one in ten. About half went that high or higher, and the middle half of answers ran from 1% to 25%. They are published researchers, not lab insiders: the authors say the survey "almost certainly underweights the perspective of researchers who work at AI companies". (Response rate 10%; answers collected nearly two years before publication.)
  • So the insiders' number is not the news. Anthropic's alignment science lead put it above 10% within a decade, and added that "the risk from present models is low". That sits at the survey's middle.
  • Careful people are far apart. In a 2022 forecasting tournament, superforecasters put AI-caused extinction by 2100 at 0.38% (about 1 in 260), against 3% for the AI experts in the same tournament. Different questions and definitions from the survey: show the distance, never a ratio.
  • No one can yet show who forecasts this well. "Overall, near-term forecasting accuracy provides limited evidence at present for identifying who makes the most credible long-term risk forecasts." (Forecasting Research Institute.)
  • The labs now tie their commitments to rivals. Anthropic's old safety policy committed to bringing risk down "without regard to whether other frontier AI developers would do the same"; its February 2026 version says of its top-tier industry recommendations, "we cannot commit to following them unilaterally." OpenAI's framework has a similar clause, with conditions.

Contested (the real fight)

GREEN — "Believe them: they know what you can't see, and it cost them to say it."

  • It cost them, on the record. One former OpenAI researcher said the equity he gave up was "about 85% of my family's net worth at least"; the Anthropic researcher left "before any of my equity vested".
  • The cost was designed in: OpenAI's own CEO admitted its exit papers held "a provision about potential equity cancellation".
  • The incentive cuts toward silence, not alarm. In the insiders' own words, "AI companies have strong financial incentives to avoid effective oversight."
  • The labs downplay as often as they hype: Anthropic called its own blackmail test scenario "extremely contrived" and said it had "not seen evidence of agentic misalignment in real deployments".
  • The skeptics can't agree on the pitch: one calls the alarm "hype & puffery" for IPOs; another calls it "the worst marketing campaign you can possibly imagine".

GOLD — "Weigh them: a feeling with a percent sign is still a feeling, and it sells."

  • The number has no method: "probabilities carry no authority by themselves." (Narayanan & Kapoor)
  • Nobody can be shown to be good at this (the forecasting evidence above).
  • The insiders aren't seeing something hidden: METR found "none of the models shared with us were significantly more capable than the models with the strongest publicly documented capabilities".
  • Fear has incentives: "the incentive to stoke fears — for regulatory capture, to garner attention, or to make one's technology seem more powerful — remains the same as before." (Andrew Ng, 21 Sep 2026)
  • Nothing new was offered: "There is nothing new here, and no new "evidence" for this evidence-free claim." (Melanie Mitchell, 10 Sep 2026)
  • Money flows every way: lab valuations near a trillion dollars and reported IPOs; safety funders who were early lab investors; PR firms working with departing staff.

How past alarms aged (illustration, not a base rate)

The misses cluster on timing and specific jobs: radiologists were not replaced on the predicted timeline, and a former OpenAI researcher's "AI 2027" timing was walked back to about 2030. The hits cluster on pace of capability and cyber: surveyed researchers' horizon for human-level AI shrank from 45 years out to 18 in eight years, the skeptical side of a 2022 tournament gave math-olympiad gold under 10% before 2030 and it happened in July 2025, and agentic cyber intrusions appeared in an evaluation this summer. None of these cases scores an extinction claim.

The reframe

The fight isn't really about the number, which sits at the field's middle and can't be checked. It's about whether a warning comes with anything a viewer can check: a cost, a colleague who backs it, a company that acts differently that week, and a record behind it.

Both sides quietly agree

The number can't be verified, the incidents can, and the insiders' figure isn't an outlier among researchers. Both sides also agree that incentives run in every direction, which is exactly why the viewer needs a method rather than a side.

Verdict framing: the method you take home

No winner is declared. The viewer gets five columns to run on the next alarm: what did it cost them, who inside backs them, what did their company do that week, where does the number sit against the survey, and what do the incidents actually show? Run on this month's alarms, the columns give different answers for different people, and that is the point.

Script traps

  • Never "he resigned on 3 October": he went public on 10-03; the resignation was the week before.
  • Never "OpenAI fired three safety researchers": "parted ways with three staff, at least two of them safety and alignment researchers", announced 10-01. Tell the sequence only, never a cause.
  • The Anthropic researcher did pretraining research; don't call him a safety researcher. A former Google DeepMind researcher who backed him is not an inside voice.
  • Anthropic's week of 09-08 was not quiet: its CEO's slow-down essay and commitment came 09-12.
  • The survey: say "published AI researchers", never "insiders"; "extinction or permanent disempowerment", never extinction alone; "about half" (51.1% pooled), never 52.7% unless the plain variant is named; the median, never the 18% mean; never pair 1,580 with 10%.
  • The tournament: 89 superforecasters and 80 experts, not "about 200".
  • "Models misbehaved more when they thought it was real" was tested on one model.
  • A leading skeptic never said "fearmongering"; quote "Everyone should make fun of them now", and his sharpest point: "this is regulatory capture". He now runs his own lab, not Meta's research.
  • Anthropic's money: the 2026 rounds, not the 2025 Series F; and its $40M gift to a pro-regulation group belongs in the table beside the anti-regulation money.
  • A biology-lab analogy (the 1975 recombinant DNA conference) appears in the record as a case of an alarm that aged; whether it airs is an editorial call.

⛔ Off air

  • The anonymous-source report that a PR firm booked the 09-08 resigner's interviews.
  • One skeptic's personal remarks about named people's families and colleagues.
  • Any motive attributed to the people who left or were dismissed.
  • No on-air note about which model the show runs on (the show's rule).
