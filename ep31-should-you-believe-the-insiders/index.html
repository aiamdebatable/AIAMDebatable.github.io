AI Extinction Risk: Should You Believe the People Building It?

When the people building AI say it might end us, how much of that is knowledge, how much is fear, and how much is marketing — and how would you tell?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in October 2026, an Anthropic researcher had quit in public in September, and the company's alignment science lead put the chance AI kills all humans above ten percent within a decade. On October 3, the man who had led the writing of OpenAI's safety reports went public that he had quit too.

Settled

What both sides accept

Nobody can check a chance of extinction. The insiders' number sits in the middle of what published researchers say, some who spoke paid for it while some show no cost at all, and nothing they've cited so far was a secret.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesBelieve them

A researcher walked away from shares that hadn't vested, and within hours a lead at his own company backed him by name. Their number sits at the field's typical answer, one in ten, and on how fast AI moves, the more worried side has come closer. When insiders pay to warn you, the burden moves to whoever says they're wrong.

Gold arguesWeigh them

A cost proves he means it, not that he's right. The inside runs about two months ahead of the public, the incidents they cite were already public, and a chance of extinction has no method or track record. So weigh them: what it cost, who backs them by name, what the company did that week, and where the number sits.

What the shouting gets wrong

The record leans, on both counts: these warnings look sincere, and they don't look like secret knowledge. Neither "they know what you can't see" nor "it's just marketing" survived. What's left is whose number you start from, and what the next alarm has to show you before you move it.

The verdict

Where the record leans
, and it does lean. These warnings look sincere. They don't look secret. That isn't a split decision. It's what the evidence says, on both counts.
What didn't hold up

Still argued · your call

Your dial: Whose number do you start from?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1The people inside
    What it buys
    You start where their own letter asks: with the people closest to the work. Some paid real costs to speak: one researcher walked away from shares that hadn't vested, and colleagues still inside backed him by name, within hours.
    What it costs
    It fails when they can't see more than you, and the record doesn't show that they do. On capability, the inside runs about two months ahead of the public, and nothing they've cited so far was a secret. Some who spoke show no cost at all: the man who quit OpenAI in October named none.
    Not measured
    What, if anything, insiders see about the risk that the public can't: nobody has shown it.

    On the record: entry 1 in the fact-check log: Right to Warn: the insiders' own case that they are the check, entry 2 in the fact-check log: What it cost him: unvested equity, in his own words, entry 3 in the fact-check log: A second Anthropic lead backed him, in a personal capacity, entry 4 in the fact-check log: METR: no hidden capability lead inside the labs, entry 5 in the fact-check log: The essay states no probability, no personal cost and no other person who resigned

  2. 2The field's survey
    What it buys
    The typical answer from about 1,500 published AI researchers, asked in December 2024: a one in ten chance that AI leads to human extinction or a similarly permanent loss of control. The middle half of the answers ran from 1% to 25%, and the insiders' number sits right in that middle.
    What it costs
    It fails on who answers it. Only about one in ten of the people emailed answered, and the authors say the survey almost certainly underweights researchers who work at AI companies. It also asks about extinction or a permanent loss of control, not extinction alone.
    Not measured
    Whether the researchers who didn't answer see it differently: the check for that was not repeated in this round.

    On the record: entry 1 in the fact-check log: The pooled median is 10% for "extinction OR similarly permanent and severe disempowerment", entry 2 in the fact-check log: 12% gave zero; the middle half spans 1% to 25%, entry 3 in the fact-check log: 19,874 emails, 2,052 responses (10%), 1,580 analysed, about 470 excluded, fielded 9–24 Dec 2024, entry 4 in the fact-check log: ★ NEW, load-bearing for this episode: the survey underweights people at AI companies, entry 5 in the fact-check log: The non-response check was not repeated in 2024; all but the least attentive share the 10% median

  3. 3The forecasters
    What it buys
    A number from people with records of accurate predictions. In a 2022 tournament, the typical superforecaster put AI causing human extinction by 2100 at about 1 in 260, against 3 in 100 for the typical AI expert.
    What it costs
    They can't yet show they're any good at this either. Short-term accuracy gives limited evidence for whose long-term risk forecasts to trust. On the AI questions that came due, both groups underestimated how fast AI would move, and the experts came closer. After the two groups argued it out, few minds changed.
    Not measured
    Who forecasts extinction well: it can only happen once, so nobody has a track record on it.

    On the record: entry 1 in the fact-check log: XPT: superforecasters 0.38% vs domain experts 3% (AI extinction by 2100), entry 2 in the fact-check log: The groups did not converge after incentivised discussion, entry 3 in the fact-check log: FRI follow-up: superforecasters 9.7% vs experts 24.6% on outcomes that happened; IMO gold 2.3% vs 8.6%; no correlation with long-run x-risk forecasts, entry 4 in the fact-check log: FRI: near-term accuracy does not identify credible long-run x-risk forecasters

Whichever you pick: Nobody can check a chance of extinction. And the judge leaves the same question with all three: what the next alarm has to show you before you move it.
