Research brief — Should You Be Able to Bet on Your Own Election?

The full research behind the episode. Debate type. Researched 2026-10-03: peg hunt, then a deep-research fan-out, then two hand passes that opened every relied-on primary (the CFTC orders and proposed rule, the court opinions, the statutes, the bills, the exchanges' own notices). Every number below is in the fact-check log with its source.

⛔ Frame (ruled 2026-09-30): the process and why the viewer should care, never who is ahead. No live odds, no chart or ticker, never which party a market favours. Enforcement subjects are named by ROLE.

The question

Should Americans be allowed to bet on elections, and if so, who should be barred?

Why now

For a decade the federal markets regulator said no. In 2012 the CFTC blocked election contracts, worried they could pay people "to vote for particular candidates even when such a vote may be contrary to the voter's political views"; in 2023 it blocked them again, adding worries about manipulation, misinformation and "the perception of integrity of elections". A federal judge then ruled (Sept 2024) that the agency had no legal hook — elections "are neither" unlawful activity nor gaming — and expressly did not decide whether the contracts are in the public interest. The CFTC dropped its appeal (May 2025), withdrew its proposed ban (Feb 2026), and in June 2026 proposed a rule (91 FR 35806) that calls its old orders' reasoning preliminarily "incorrect" on several legal grounds — "political elections are not gaming" — and lists election results among contracts that "would generally fall outside" its power to ban. It restates the old integrity worries only as history, neither adopting nor disowning them. No final rule as of 3 Oct.

Meanwhile the states are moving the other way: 23 make election betting entirely illegal and 9 more in some cases (Pew); a Washington court ordered an exchange to stop election, politics and "mention" markets there (Aug 2026); Arizona filed criminal charges including four election-wagering counts (a federal court has paused that prosecution). The courts haven't settled it: the Ninth Circuit sent the election-contract question back down (Aug 2026), and the Supreme Court petition is about sports, with a response due 9 November — after the vote. And this summer the CFTC settled with a former member of Congress who traded on whether he'd attend the State of the Union while posting misleading things about it, and with a White House teleprompter operator who traded on which words the President would say. On 22 Sept its staff called contracts that hinge on one person's conduct "presumptively readily susceptible to manipulation".

So 3 November is the first federal midterm with election contracts live on federally registered exchanges.

Settled (not the debate)

  • No federal rule, statute or appeals court has decided whether election contracts are good for the public. The only court ruling was on the agency's legal hook; the CFTC withdrew its appeal; the June proposal is not final.
  • The integrity fears were never found as fact. 2012 and 2023: "can potentially" / "could potentially". The 2023 order recorded commenters on both sides of whether manipulation had happened, and made no finding.
  • Everyone draws the same first line: people who can affect or know the outcome shouldn't trade on it. Kalshi's rules bar candidates, campaign staff and "anyone working in a vote-tallying capacity"; Polymarket US bars anyone with "authority or influence" over an outcome; a Republican-led House bill covers Members and families; Democratic bills go further; CFTC staff warn about one-person contracts; the UK, which allows political betting, prosecutes insiders as cheating.
  • That line mostly rests on the exchanges' own rules. CRS: whether federal law reaches a candidate trading on their own race is "unclear", and at least one commentator doubts the CFTC's theory.
  • The insider record is real, and it is about conduct, not vote counts. Two CFTC orders (attendance and mention markets); Kalshi suspended three congressional candidates in April — two from one party's primaries, one from the other's — for five years, fines $540–$6,229; more in August.
  • A big trader can move a price for a while. 2012: one trader held a candidate's price up for hours on Election Day; when he stopped, it jumped to where other markets already were. He lost close to $7 million.
  • Politics is a small slice of the money. Kalshi's politics volume: $1.13 billion in November 2024, $169 million in July 2026, out of $53 billion traded across the two big exchanges that month, mostly sports.

Contested (the real fight)

Team Green — "Let people put money on it": a price carries money-backed judgment. In the longest academic test, a small university market beat 964 individual polls about three times in four across five presidential elections (one line only; accuracy is ep28's question). Elections aren't gambling — a federal judge and now the regulator say so. A supervised exchange with ID checks and surveillance is how the candidate cases were caught at all; an offshore site would never have flagged them. The UK model works: allow the market, prosecute the insider. Manipulation is expensive and temporary.

Team Gold — "Bar it, or at least bar a lot more people": the people closest to an election can and do try to trade on it — candidates in both parties' races already have. Exchange self-policing screens federal filings and misses volunteers, families, state and local races. Thin midterm markets are where a moderate amount of money can move a price, and that price now runs on CNBC, CNN, Dow Jones outlets and CBS through data deals. A county election chief in Los Angeles says returns that differed from "market expectations" led people to question normal ballot counting. And it overrides states whose laws are a century old: Wisconsin and New York bar a person who has bet on an election from voting in it; Utah makes a candidate's bet a felony.

The reframe

The shouting frames this as "markets versus democracy." The record is narrower. No court has weighed election integrity — the federal fight has been about legal definitions and, mostly, about sports money. The documented harms are insiders trading on conduct and damage to TRUST in the count, not changed votes; the vote-buying fear behind 2012 and 2023 has no case behind it. Green's "better supervised than offshore" is also asserted, not measured. The question a voter can actually weigh: who near an election should be forbidden to trade, and who enforces it when federal law doesn't clearly reach them?

Both sides quietly agree

Candidates on their own races, campaign staff, officials with inside information, and the people who count the votes should not trade. A Republican-led bill, Democratic senators' letter, a bipartisan House bill, both exchanges' rulebooks, CFTC staff and Britain's prosecutors all share that floor. Today it rests on exchange rulebooks, not a clear federal law.

Verdict framing (no winner; you're the jury)

The dial you set: who may bet on an election? ① Nobody (23 states; the 2012 and 2023 CFTC; the Democratic-led and bipartisan House bills) — failure: the betting moves where nobody watches (asserted on both sides, not measured). ② Everyone except insiders (today's federal posture plus the exchanges' rules; the Republican-led House bill for Members) — failure: the floor rests on exchange rulebooks, and CRS says it is unclear whether federal law reaches a candidate trading on their own race. ③ Everyone, and let the price speak (the June proposal's direction) — failure: one trader held a price for hours in 2012; in Los Angeles, returns that differed from "market expectations" led people to question normal ballot counting. And the side question the viewer also sets: should a newsroom show that price as news?

What we could not establish

  • Any study of whether odds shown on TV change turnout, giving or trust (none found).
  • Whether "regulated beats offshore" actually displaces offshore betting (asserted by both sides, unmeasured).
  • Kentucky "banned election betting": the enrolled law contains no such ban; Pew's line is unsupported. Not used.
  • Minnesota's July injunction against its statewide ban (press only). The Arizona appeal's current status.
  • Any peer-reviewed account of a single trader moving a 2024 price. Not used.
