Election Betting: Should You Be Able to Bet on Your Own Election?

Should Americans be allowed to bet on elections, and if so, who should be barred?

Last changed 2026-11-08 · See every change

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

Candidates from both parties got caught betting on their own races. The regulator just said its old 'no' was wrong. Who should be allowed to bet?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in October 2026, shortly before the fall vote, the federal regulator had proposed in June that its old reasons for blocking election bets were wrong, while states moved the other way. In April an exchange had suspended three congressional candidates for betting on their own races.

Settled

What both sides accept

No court has weighed whether election betting harms democracy, and the regulator's old fears were never shown to happen. Candidates from both parties' primaries tried to bet on their own races and were caught. Both teams want candidates, staff, officials and the people who count ballots barred by law, and at the time, mostly they weren't.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesLet them trade

The old case against election bets was a "could potentially", and nobody has shown a bet changing a vote. A judge and the regulator say elections aren't gaming, and the exchange's own screening caught the candidates. Let the public trade and bar insiders by law: a price you can watch is safer than a bet you can't see.

▶ Hear Green at 2:52 on YouTube
Gold arguesBar the bets

Candidates, a former member of Congress and a White House staffer have already traded on things they were close to, and the rule against insiders mostly lives in company rulebooks. In Los Angeles, a market price fed doubt about an honest count. Ban contracts on election results, bar every insider by law, and let states keep their bans.

▶ Hear Gold at 6:55 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

Nobody defends a candidate betting on their own race, and nobody has shown a bet changing a vote. The open question is who near an election may trade, and who enforces it when federal law is unclear. The choice runs from nobody, as in 23 states, to everyone except insiders, to everyone.

The verdict

What didn't hold up
Green's comfort: nobody has measured that a supervised exchange pulls betting off the offshore sites. Gold's fear: nobody has shown a bet changing a single vote.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: Who may bet on an election?

The judge named these three answers and declared no winner. The judge also handed you a second, smaller dial, with no sides named: should a newsroom show that price as news? Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Nobody
    What it buys
    No contracts on election results, and states that already ban it keep the right to. Today 23 states make betting on elections entirely illegal, and 9 more in some cases. It meets two points that survived from Gold: federal law is unclear about a candidate betting on their own race, and the floor against insiders mostly lives in rulebooks. Congress has bills for it: Democrats' bills would bar election contracts for everyone, and one House bill, from a Republican with a Democrat, would ban contracts on election results.
    What it costs
    Gold's fear didn't hold up: nobody has shown a bet changing a single vote, and the regulator's old fears were could potentially, never shown. None of those bills is law. And state bans are being fought: when Minnesota passed the first statewide ban on prediction markets, the federal government sued the next day, and a federal court blocked Arizona's charges at the request of the United States and the CFTC.
    Not measured
    Whether a ban pushes betting offshore, where nobody's watching: Gold's own debater said some of it may go there, and nobody has measured it.

    On the record: row 7 of the fact-check log, row 31 of the fact-check log, row 22 of the fact-check log, row 12 of the fact-check log, row 11 of the fact-check log

  2. 2Everyone except the insiders
    What it buys
    This is what the exchanges' own rules do now, and they caught people: the exchange's screening caught three candidates from both parties' primaries in April. Kalshi bars candidates, campaign staff and anyone working in a vote-tallying capacity; Polymarket's US exchange bars anyone with authority or influence over an outcome. The CFTC also settled with two insiders this summer, over a State of the Union and a speech, not election results, with the exchange's help in one of them.
    What it costs
    The floor against insiders mostly lives in rulebooks, and federal law is unclear about a candidate betting on their own race. Gold's point: a company's screening catches the names it knows to check. The insider bill that advanced in the House covers members of Congress and their families, not candidates, staff or the people counting ballots.
    Not measured
    Green's comfort, that a supervised exchange draws betting away from offshore sites: nobody has measured it.

    On the record: row 20 of the fact-check log, row 21 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 18 of the fact-check log, row 22 of the fact-check log, row 31 of the fact-check log

  3. 3Everyone, and let the price speak
    What it buys
    A federal judge and now the regulator say elections aren't gaming. Politics is a small slice of the money: Kalshi's politics trading was about $1.13 billion in November 2024 and $169 million this July, out of $53 billion traded across the two big exchanges that month. On accuracy, one long test of a small university market found its price beat individual polls about three times in four, from 1988 to 2004; Green's own debater called that evidence small and decades old.
    What it costs
    Nobody on the show defended a candidate betting on their own race, and both teams want candidates, staff, officials and the people who count ballots barred by law. In Los Angeles, a price on the screen fed doubt about an honest count: the county's election chief said early returns differed from market expectations, and people questioned normal ballot counting. And one big bettor can hold a price up for a while: in 2012 one trader did it for hours on Election Day, and lost close to $7 million.
    Not measured
    What showing odds on air does to turnout, donations or trust: we found no study.

    On the record: row 3 of the fact-check log, row 25 of the fact-check log, row 29 of the fact-check log, row 24 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 27 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected)
