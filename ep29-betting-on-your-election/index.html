Election Betting: Should You Be Able to Bet on Your Own Election?

The question: Should Americans be allowed to bet on elections, and if so, who should be barred?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

Candidates from both parties got caught betting on their own races. The regulator just said its old 'no' was wrong. Who should be allowed to bet?

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in October 2026, shortly before the fall vote, the federal regulator had proposed in June that its old reasons for blocking election bets were wrong, while states moved the other way. In April an exchange had suspended three congressional candidates for betting on their own races.

The fight

Green arguesLet them trade

The old case against election bets was a "could potentially", and nobody has shown a bet changing a vote. A judge and the regulator say elections aren't gaming, and the exchange's own screening caught the candidates. Let the public trade and bar insiders by law: a price you can watch is safer than a bet you can't see.

Gold arguesBar the bets

Candidates, a former member of Congress and a White House staffer have already traded on things they were close to, and the rule against insiders mostly lives in company rulebooks. In Los Angeles, a market price fed doubt about an honest count. Ban contracts on election results, bar every insider by law, and let states keep their bans.

What both sides quietly agree on

No court has weighed whether election betting harms democracy, and the regulator's old fears were never shown to happen. Candidates from both parties' primaries tried to bet on their own races and were caught. Both teams want candidates, staff, officials and the people who count ballots barred by law, and at the time, mostly they weren't.

What the shouting gets wrong

Nobody defends a candidate betting on their own race, and nobody has shown a bet changing a vote. The open question is who near an election may trade, and who enforces it when federal law is unclear. The choice runs from nobody, as in 23 states, to everyone except insiders, to everyone.

Kate What survived from green. A federal judge and now the regulator say elections aren't gaming. The exchange's screening caught the candidates, and the CFTC settled with two insiders. And politics is a small slice of the money.

Kate What survived from gold. Federal law is unclear about a candidate betting on their own race. The floor against insiders mostly lives in rulebooks. And in Los Angeles, a price on the screen fed doubt about an honest count.

Kate What fell. Green's comfort: nobody has measured that a supervised exchange pulls betting off the offshore sites. Gold's fear: nobody has shown a bet changing a single vote.

Kate So here's what both teams agree on, and what nobody has done. Candidates, staff, officials and the people who count ballots shouldn't trade on an election. Both teams want that in law. Today it mostly isn't.

Kate That leaves one dial neither team can set for you. Who may bet on an election? Nobody, like twenty-three states. Everyone except the insiders, like the exchanges' own rules. Or everyone, and let the price speak. And a second, smaller dial: should a newsroom show that price as news?

Kate So the question we're handing you isn't markets or democracy. It's this: who may bet on an election? That one's yours.
