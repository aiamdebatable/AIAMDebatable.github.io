Four-Day Workweek: Should the Law Shorten Your Week, or Your Boss?

Should the law shorten your week, or should your boss?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

The verdictYour dialNumbers you heardWhat the fact-check caughtUp next

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

Four tens, thirty-two hours, or a law: three different weeks, and the real question is who counts your hours.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

In October 2026, Brazil's Senate voted 75 to 1 to amend the constitution and end the six-day working week, taking the legal maximum from 44 hours to 40 with no cut in pay. In the US no law caps the week, and a 32-hour bill introduced in Congress in September hadn't moved.

Settled

What both sides accept

"Four-day week" means three different things: the same hours in fewer days, fewer hours for the same pay, or a shorter week set by law. Federal law puts a price on hour 41 and caps nothing, and for about nine in ten plant workers the employer writes the schedule. For salaried, exempt staff, nothing in law counts the hours.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesSet it by law

The forty-hour line is already federal law, drawn by Congress for everyone it covers. For about nine in ten plant workers there's no union contract, so the employer decides, and the auto workers asked for 32 hours in 2023 and didn't get it. A law can be phased in with pay protected, as Mexico and Chile are doing.

▶ Hear Green at 4:11 on YouTube
Gold arguesSet it at work

The pilots' own authors say their results don't justify a law, and France's one-hour cut lost 2 to 4% of the affected jobs. The plant schedules that work were designed locally: 14 days' notice at Ford, a 60% vote at U.S. Steel. Keep the law pricing long hours, and let the people who run the work design the week.

▶ Hear Gold at 9:55 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

The real question is who draws the line that counts your hours: the law, your employer, or a contract you vote on. Where firms choose it, a shorter week for the same pay leans clearly good for the people in it, but the record leans against that justifying a law, in the pilots' own words. Where law has cut hours, it shortened them by less than it wrote, with mixed results for jobs.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: Who draws the line that counts your hours?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1The law caps the week
    What it buys
    Reaches everyone: law is the only lever that reaches every week. Mexico and Chile are phasing theirs in, two hours at a time, with pay protected in writing: Mexico from 48 hours to 40 by 2030, Chile to 40 in 2028. These laws take long weeks down to 40, which is the five-day week arriving, not a four-day one.
    What it costs
    Fails when jobs or real hours don't follow. Where law has cut hours, it shortened them by less than it wrote, with mixed results for jobs. France's one-hour cut cost 2 to 4% of the affected jobs, hardest at the minimum wage. Japan's legal cut moved actual hours by about 0.14 of an hour for each hour written.
    Not measured
    A 32-hour week set by law: no country on this record has legislated one, and the American bill hasn't moved.

    On the record: row 14.1 of the fact-check log, row 14.3 of the fact-check log, row 14.6 of the fact-check log, row 14.12 of the fact-check log, row 1.12 of the fact-check log

  2. 2The law prices the long hours
    What it buys
    It's the line already in law, and it's why the long week costs more: federal overtime starts after 40 hours in a week. Gallup's explanation for why hourly workers report shorter weeks is what overtime costs. The bill in Congress works this way too, stepping the overtime line down toward 32 and adding overtime after 8 hours in a day, but it hasn't moved.
    What it costs
    Never reaches a salaried, exempt worker. Once someone is exempt, the full salary is owed for any week with any work, whatever the hours. Salaried workers report 49 hours a week. And it caps nothing: a plant can run four tens or three twelves without owing any federal overtime.
    Not measured
    How much salaried staff work on the day off: nobody has clocked it, because their HR records usually show 40 whatever they work.

    On the record: row 2.1 of the fact-check log, row 4.5 of the fact-check log, row 11.14 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 11.15 of the fact-check log, row 11.11 of the fact-check log

  3. 3A contract decides
    What it buys
    The plant schedules that work were designed locally, contract by contract. At GM, a new schedule needs the company and the union both to sign. At U.S. Steel, 60% of the affected workers have to vote yes, and after six months a simple majority can vote it back out.
    What it costs
    Reaches fewer than one plant worker in ten: in 2025, 8.4% of manufacturing workers were covered by a union contract. Bargaining never forces either side to agree: the auto workers asked for 32 hours in 2023 and didn't get it. And some contracts give the company the call: Ford can switch a plant to four tens on 14 days' notice after discussing it with the union.
    Not measured
    Whether a plant's move to four tens or twelve-hour shifts pays off: no US plant on this record has published its output, overtime cost or turnover.

    On the record: row 6.1 of the fact-check log, row 6.2 of the fact-check log, row 6.14 of the fact-check log, row 6.15 of the fact-check log, row 6.5 of the fact-check log

  4. 4Your employer decides
    What it buys
    Works where the employer wants it. Where firms choose it, a reduced week leans clearly good for the people in it: in Britain's 2022 trial, 56 of 61 firms kept going, and the biggest study found less burnout and better health. Where it stuck, firms redesigned the work: in Portugal, 8% of firms that changed two or more ways of working went back to five days, against 38% of those that changed one thing or nothing.
    What it costs
    Nowhere else. For most plant workers it means the employer decides alone, and the good results come from firms that chose the shorter week, which tells you nothing about the employers who won't. When Utah imposed four tens on its own staff, it expected $3 million a year in utility savings, got about $502,000, couldn't measure productivity, and its Legislature ended it.
    Not measured
    How a shorter week would do at employers who didn't volunteer: every pilot was firms that chose it, and the biggest study's authors say their result likely overestimates the average effect.

    On the record: row 9.1 of the fact-check log, row 9.7 of the fact-check log, row 9.10 of the fact-check log, row 9.15 of the fact-check log, row 12.2 of the fact-check log
