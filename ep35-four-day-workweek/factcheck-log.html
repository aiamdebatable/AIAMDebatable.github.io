Fairness audit

  • The law side is not written beyond its evidence. Brazil has not voted; Mexico and Chile cap hours from long weeks (48 and 45), which is the five-day week arriving, not the four-day week; the US bills move only a price line and none has passed. The record of legal cuts elsewhere is mixed (France 1982 cost jobs among those affected; the 35 hours did not move net employment and intensified work; Portugal and Japan held monthly pay but actual hours fell far less than legal hours). All of that is in the brief.
  • The boss side is not written beyond its evidence. The pilots are volunteer, mostly office firms, self-reported, and run or written up by organisations that campaign for the policy; the strongest "leave it to the boss" line (Portugal's authors) is a statement about method, not proof that firms will spread it on their own. For about 9 in 10 plant workers "the boss decides" means the boss alone.
  • Advocacy and lobby sources are marked: Autonomy, Alda, 4 Day Week Global (advocates); CNI (industry lobby); union booklets; Circadian (a 12-hour scheduling consultancy). None carries a figure on air without attribution.
  • No politics. Brazil is about the amendment and the vote, never a party or a personality; the US bill is named by its title, not its sponsor's party. The executive quotes are dated and attributed, and Huang's paraphrase is not used.
  • Lucas's own hypothesis is tested, not adopted. The catch-up day is reported where it holds (some leakage, self-reported) and where it does not (no one has clocked salaried staff; hours still fell 4–6).
  • Plant workers are not a footnote. The contract differences (Ford consult, GM and Stellantis both sign, U.S. Steel 60% vote, Smithfield company decides) are reported as the contracts say, not as union or company claims; the 12-hour safety record is split into what is settled and what is not.
