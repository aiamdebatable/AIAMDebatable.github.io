_The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video._

## One-line pitch

Everyone argues about "the four-day week" as if it were one thing. It is three, and for most American
workers the law, the boss and the union contract each decide a different one, so the real question is
not four days or five but **who gets to count your hours**.

## The viewer's moment

It is Thursday night. You have one of three weeks, and you probably know which.

- **You clock in.** You work 4×10s, or 3×12s that flip to 4×12s every other week, or a plain 40. The
  posting on the break-room board changed your rotation, and nobody asked you. You get time and a half
  on the long week, but not because of the long days.
- **You are salaried.** Your company just announced "Fridays off". Your manager says 32 hours. Your
  inbox says otherwise, and Friday is the day you finally catch up.
- **You are in a union.** Your schedule is in the contract, and changing it means a vote, or a
  signature, or fourteen days' notice, depending on whose contract it is.

Meanwhile, a whole country, Brazil, has just voted to end the six-day week by law, and a bill in
Congress would make every hour past eight in a day cost overtime and phase the weekly line down toward
32 (who the phase-down covers is a drafting question the record could not settle; row 5.1). The episode follows one week from each of those three lives
and asks who should decide what it looks like: Congress, or the person who writes the schedule.

---

## The question

**Should the law shorten your week, or should your boss?**

"Your boss" means the workplace: the employer's call where there is no contract (about nine in ten plant
workers, row 6.1), the bargained contract where there is one. "The law" means three different levers: a
legal cap on hours or days (Brazil, Mexico, Chile), a lower overtime line (every US "32-hour" bill), or
state rules on the working day (California, Oregon). The episode keeps the levers apart.

---

## Why now

six-day working week across the whole economy, **75 votes to 1 in each of two rounds**. It cuts the legal maximum from **44 to 40 hours**, with
two paid rest days and no cut in pay: 42 hours two months after it is published, 40 hours a year after
that ([Agência Senado, 2026-10-06](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2026/10/06/pec-que-acaba-com-escala-6x1-comeca-a-tramitar-no-plenario);
[Agência Senado, 2026-09-02](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2026/09/02/aguardar-fim-da-escala-6x1-passa-na-ccj-e-vai-a-plenario)).

- **Where it stands today (2026-10-15, re-checked):** after five discussion sessions (first 2026-10-06),
  the Senate approved it on Wednesday 2026-10-14 under a special calendar; a leaders' request waived the

## First: what "four-day week" means (the definitions box)

⛔ The episode's hinge, not a footnote (Ruling 2). Put this on screen early and use the three words the
same way every time.

| | **Compressed** | **Reduced** | **Fewer days by law** |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Same hours, fewer days | Fewer hours, same pay | A legal cap on hours, or a rule on days of rest |
| Examples | 4×10, 3×12, 36/48 rotations; Utah's state workforce 2008–11; Belgium's right to request a four-day week | The "100-80-100" pilots (UK, US/Ireland, Portugal); the UAW's 2023 32-for-40 demand (not won) | Brazil's PEC (44 → 40, two rest days); Mexico (48 → 40, still one rest day in six); Chile (44 → 40) |
| Hours a week | 36–48 (40 on average for a 4×10) | 32 on paper; 34–35 reported | 40–42 |
| Who decides in the US today | The employer, inside state rules and any contract | A deal: the employer, or a contract; no law requires it | No US law does this; US bills only move the overtime line |

Two consequences the script must carry:

- **The laws abroad are not four-day weeks.** Brazil, Mexico and Chile are taking long weeks (44–48
  hours, often six days) down to 40. That is the five-day week arriving, not the four-day week.
- **A 4×10 is not a 32-hour week.** Ford's 4×10 is 40 straight-time hours; the UAW asked for 32 in 2023
  and did not get it (log 6.7, 6.14).

## Settled (not the debate)

Neither seat may fight these.

**What US law decides, and what it doesn't**
- **Federal overtime is weekly.** Time and a half is owed only past 40 hours in a workweek; there is no
  federal cap on adult hours and, "generally", no daily overtime. A 4×10 (40) or a 3×12 (36) owes no
  federal overtime ([29 U.S.C. § 207](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/207);
  [29 CFR 778.102](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/section-778.102)).
- **"Generally" is load-bearing:** state law and contracts can add daily premiums, and the federal law
  leaves them in force ([29 CFR 778.102](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/section-778.102)).
- **Each week stands alone.** No averaging across weeks, swing shifts included; a 36/48 rotation owes
  8 overtime hours in the 48-hour week ([29 CFR 778.104](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/section-778.104)).
  The only averaging exception for private employers is hospitals and residential care (8/80), and it
  still pays past 8 a day ([29 CFR 778.601](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/section-778.601)).
- **An employer may cut an hourly worker's scheduled hours, and the pay with them**
  ([DOL Fact Sheet 70](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/70-flsa-furloughs)). A "reduced" week
  without a pay guarantee is legal for hourly staff.
- **Salaried is not exempt.** Exemption needs a duties test, a fixed salary and a salary of at least
  $684 a week ($35,568 a year); the 2024 increase to $844 applied from July 1 to Nov 15, 2024, then was
  vacated ([Federal Register 2026-09839](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/15/2026-09839);
  [29 CFR 541.600](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/section-541.600)).
- **An exempt worker's salary is owed for any week with any work, whatever the hours.** So an unpaid
  catch-up fifth day is lawful ([29 CFR 541.602](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/section-541.602)).
  DOL estimates about 37.9 million white-collar exempt workers, about one in four or five wage and salary
  workers, and says no data source identifies them directly ([Federal Register 2024-08038](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/04/26/2024-08038)).
- **No US "32-hour" bill has passed, and none shortens the week by law.** Each moves the overtime price
  line: H.R. 10323 (federal, in committee), Washington HB 2611 (32 from 2028, no action since January),
  California AB 2932 (died 2022), Rhode Island H7362 (status unconfirmed)
  ([congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/10323/all-actions);
  [WA HB 2611](https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/2025-26/Htm/Bills/House%20Bills/2611.htm);
  [CA AB 2932](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billHistoryClient.xhtml?bill_id=202120220AB2932)).

**Who decides, in practice**
- **About nine in ten US manufacturing workers have no union contract** (8.4% covered in 2025), so for
  them the employer sets the schedule, inside state rules ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/union2.t03.htm)).
- **Where there is a contract, hours must be bargained.** Hours are a mandatory subject; an employer may
  not change them unilaterally during bargaining or mid-contract unless the union waived it
  ([29 U.S.C. § 158(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/158);
  [NLRB v. Katz](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/369/736);
  [NLRB](https://www.nlrb.gov/about-nlrb/rights-we-protect/the-law/bargaining-in-good-faith-with-employees-union-representative)).
  Bargaining never compels agreement.

**What the evidence on shorter weeks shows**
- **The pilots work for the firms that choose them.** UK 2022: 56 of 61 firms continued, 18 made it
  permanent ([Autonomy](https://autonomy.work/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/The-results-are-in-The-UKs-four-day-week-pilot.pdf)).
  The largest peer-reviewed analysis: 2,896 staff at 141 organisations in six countries, with less
  burnout and better health, "a pattern not observed in 12 control companies"
  ([Nature Human Behaviour 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02259-6)).
- **They cannot answer the scale question.** Firms volunteered; hours are self-reported; the samples
  are mostly white-collar (in the 2023 global sample, half professional services and 8% construction or
  manufacturing); the authors added that their findings "likely overestimate the average true effect"
  ([Peer Review File](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41562-025-02259-6/MediaObjects/41562_2025_2259_MOESM2_ESM.pdf);
  [2023 working paper](https://www.asanet.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Worktime-Reduction-and-Workers-Well-Being-2023-12-16-4DW_wellbeing.pdf)).
- **Legal cuts move actual hours less than legal hours.** Japan 1987–97: each legal hour cut actual hours
  by about 0.14, with no cut in monthly pay ([ESRI](https://ideas.repec.org/p/esj/esridp/202.html)).
  Korea's 52-hour cap reduced but did not end very long weeks ([IZA](https://docs.iza.org/dp16023.pdf)).
- **In US history, hours fell mostly with wages, partly with law.** Rising wages were the "most important
  force"; legislation "also had a noticeable role"; unions explain about one-seventh of the decline
  ([Huberman & Minns](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/minns/Huberman_Minns_EEH_2007.pdf);
  [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)).

## Contested (the real fight)

**Side A — "The law should shorten your week."**

1. **It is the only lever that reaches everyone.** Nine in ten plant workers have no contract to bargain
   with; for them "let the boss decide" means the boss decides alone (row 6.1). The UAW, one of the
   strongest unions in the country, asked for 32 hours in 2023 and did not get it (6.14). If even that
   table cannot win it, a cashier will not.
2. **The pilots are proof of concept, and they are the best case for the boss, not against the law.**
   Where firms tried it, 56 of 61 kept it (9.1); well-being improved and did not in the comparison firms
   (9.7). The firms that make it stick are the ones that redesign the work (Portugal: 8% reverted vs 38%,
   9.15). A law forces the redesign everyone else skips.
3. **The law has done this before.** Federal law already draws a national line at 40 hours (2.1); in
   US history, legislation "also had a noticeable role" in shortening the week, and unions explain about
   one-seventh of the decline (14.16).
4. **Whole economies are doing it right now, in steps.** Mexico (48 → 40, two hours a year), Chile (44 →
   42 → 40), Brazil voted 44 → 40 (14.1, 14.3, 1.3, 1.9). The cuts are phased and protect pay in writing.
   Portugal's 1996 cut raised hourly wages, kept monthly pay flat and lowered job separations for the
   workers it covered (14.11).
5. **The lighter US lever is a price, not a ban.** Lowering the overtime line (H.R. 10323, WA HB 2611)
   does not forbid a 40-hour week; it makes the long week cost more, which is exactly how federal law
   already treats hour 41 (2.1). And state
   hours law already reaches the shift: Oregon caps plant days at 10 hours plus up to 3 of overtime and
   requires a 10-hour rest between shifts (3.6); California makes a plant vote two-thirds to run 4×10s
   without daily overtime (3.3).
6. **Salaried workers need it most, because nothing counts their hours.** The law guarantees the salary,
   not the hours (4.5). Without a legal line, "Fridays off" is a label.

**Side B — "Your boss should (or your contract)."**

1. **The pilots' own authors say they do not justify a law.** Portugal's government-run pilot: "The
   results of this study serve to inform but do not justify implementation by legislation" because
   firms selected themselves (9.16). The Nature authors concede an upper bound (9.10). Critics add: no
   randomisation, no peer review for the organiser reports (10.1, 10.2).
2. **Where law cut hours, the record is mixed.** France's 1982 one-hour cut cost 2–4% of affected jobs,
   hardest for minimum-wage workers (14.6). The 35 hours did not move net employment, but affected
   employment fell, second jobs rose (14.7) and more workers reported more stress than less (14.9).
   Japan's legal cut moved actual hours by a fraction (14.12).
3. **One rule does not fit a plant, a hospital and an office.** The schedules that work in plants are
   built locally and differ sharply: Ford picks from a menu after consulting the union; GM and
   Stellantis need both signatures; U.S. Steel needs 60% of affected workers to vote yes, and a majority
   can vote it out (6.5, 6.11, 6.12, 6.15). The contract, not Congress, is where a 3×12 gets designed.
4. **Compressed weeks imposed from the top have disappointed.** Utah moved about 13,000 of its 17,000 full-time state
   workers to 4×10 in 2008: utility savings were $502,000, not $3 million; total savings likely under
   $1 million; productivity could not be measured; managers reported fatigue on 10-hour days; the
   Legislature ended it over a veto (12.1–12.7).
5. **Even Brazil's supporters want it slower, and its industry wants it bargained.** A senator who backs
   the amendment says two months is too fast (1.6); industry wants collective bargaining, "not a
   uniform constitutional rule" (translated, 15.6) and puts the cost at up to R$267 billion a year (its own
   estimate, 15.5).
6. **Belgium gave workers a legal right to ask for a compressed four-day week; about 0.5% used it**
   (14.5, APPROXIMATE, compression not reduction). A legal option is not the same as a change.

**Where each side is weakest (the steelman cuts both ways)**
- Side A's best cases abroad are cuts **to 40**, not to 32; no country in the record has legislated a
  32-hour week. The US bills are price lines with no committee action. And Portugal's 1996 cut also made
  workers already under 40 hours more likely to lose their jobs (14.11).
- Side B's "let the workplace decide" is, for about 9 in 10 plant workers and every non-union salaried
  worker, "let the employer decide alone". The pilots' good results come from firms that **chose** it;
  that says nothing about firms that won't.

## The plant-worker reality (Ruling 3)

### Who decides a plant's schedule

1. **Federal law does not decide the shape.** It caps neither daily nor weekly hours for adults (2.1,
   2.6), and the federal hour limits OSHA lists cover aviation, trucking, rail and nuclear, not factories
   (8.9). The law prices hour 41.
2. **For about nine in ten plant workers, the boss decides** (8.4% covered by a contract, 6.1), inside
   the few state rules that reach the shift (below).
3. **Where there is a contract, the contract decides, and contracts differ sharply:**

| Contract | Who decides an alternative schedule (4×10, 12s) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Ford–UAW (2023)** | The company, after notifying and discussing with the union, on 14 days' notice; only an invented "unique" schedule needs national written approval (6.5) | Consultation, not consent. 4×10 = 40 straight-time hours, reverts to 5 days if production needs more than 40; time and a half past 10 hours a day (6.7). "Three twelves" = 36-hour base with weekends and holidays as regular work days (6.8); a 36/48 rotation pays a 2.5% premium (6.9) |
| **GM–UAW (2023)** | The company **and** the International Union, in writing (6.11) | |
| **Stellantis–UAW (2023)** | National union and company leaders must agree, then 14 days' notice; the company keeps its 5×8 right (6.12) | The union says it deleted the 2019 letter that let management impose 12-hour days (6.13, the union's account) |
| **USW–U.S. Steel (2022)** | Local union officers **and 60% of the affected workers** vote; a simple majority can revoke after six months (6.15) | Daily overtime past 8 off an agreed schedule; heavy-overtime schedules "undesirable" (6.16) |
| **USW–Chevron (oil production, 2009)** | Mutual consent; either side can end the 12s on 30 days' notice, not grievable (6.17) | 36/48 rotation built to be pay-neutral against 8s (6.18) |
| **UFCW–Smithfield (meatpacking, 2023–27)** | The company may schedule 5-, 6- or 7-day weeks with a week's notice; forced overtime by inverse seniority (6.19) | Caps: 16 hours a day without an 8-hour rest; no more than 13 straight days |

And even a contract can set a floor on hours and no ceiling (USW–BP Texas City, 6.21).

So "law vs boss" is really **law, boss or contract**, and for most US plant workers it is the boss.

### The overtime math for common plant rotations (our arithmetic on the law; log "Script derivations")

|---|---|---|---|---|

- On a 36/48 fortnight, federal law pays 88 hours' worth of straight pay for 84 hours worked, about 5%
  more. Contracts can engineer that away: Chevron's 12s cut the straight-time rate to 0.977 so there is
  "no significant pay difference" (6.18). ⛔ Don't say 12s are a raise.
- Contracts often add their own daily line: Ford pays time and a half past 10 hours (6.7); U.S. Steel
  past 8 unless on an agreed alternative schedule (6.16). Those are the contract's, not the law's.
- ⚠ The H.R. 10323 column is our arithmetic on the bill's daily-trigger text; how the weekly and daily
  triggers combine, and who gets the phase-in, is not settled in the text read (5.1). Keep it to "every
  10-hour day would owe 2 hours of overtime" on air.

### State law that reaches the shift
- **Oregon** caps plant hours: 10 a day and 55 a week, up to 3 more hours a day as overtime, a 10-hour
  rest after a shift of 8+; union-represented plants are exempt (3.6).
- **California** prices the day: 1.5× after 8, 2× after 12; a 4×10 without daily overtime needs a
  two-thirds secret ballot and two consecutive days off; union plants with a qualifying contract are
  outside the rule (3.2–3.5).
- **Nevada** (4×10 by mutual agreement), **Alaska** (10-hour plans by approval or contract) and
  **Colorado** (overtime past 12, all employers) are lighter (3.7–3.9). Day-of-rest laws exist in some
  states (California, Illinois; a sample, 3.10).

### Twelve-hour shifts: what's settled, what isn't
- **Settled:** risk climbs over the hours of a shift. NIOSH's review of 52 studies ties hours 9–12 to
  lower alertness and more fatigue, especially when 12s are combined with more than 40 hours a week
  (8.5). Across industries, 12+ hour days carry a **37%** higher injury hazard (an association, 8.1).
- **Settled, as a model:** relative to an 8-hour shift, a 10-hour shift carries about **13%** more risk
  and a 12-hour shift about **27.5%** more (Folkard & Lombardi 2006, 8.2). In the same model, four
  12-hour day shifts in a 48-hour week add about 25% over five 8s; breaks and day-vs-night matter as much
  as length (8.4). Night vs morning is a different 27.9% (8.3).
- **Not settled:** whether a 3×12 or 4×12 rotation is safer or riskier per week than 5×8. Fewer shifts
  means fewer handovers and commutes; reviews from 1998 and 2004 call the 8-vs-12 evidence equivocal
  (8.5, 8.6). No manufacturing study comparing 4×10 and 3×12 was found.
- **What the disasters show:** Texas City (2005) was 12s for 29+ straight days with no fatigue rule
  (8.7). The industry answer was a voluntary refinery standard that allows 12s and caps them (14 hours a
  shift, 92 a work-set, 8.8), not a ban. Danger comes from 12s **stacked** with overtime and no days off.

## The catch-up day (Ruling 4): the verdict is "the day off leaks, a bit"

Lucas's hypothesis: for a salaried worker, the "four-day week" is 32 or 4×10 on paper, and the fifth day
becomes the day they catch up. Tested against every pilot that measured it.

**The law half is settled:** an exempt worker's salary is owed for any week with any work, whatever the
hours (4.5), so an unpaid catch-up day is legal. For hourly workers it is the opposite: clocked hours are
paid, so a worked fifth day is paid time, and past 40 it is overtime.

**Where it holds (partly), on the reduced-hours design:**
- Hours fell about **half to three-quarters** of the promised 8, not 8: UK 38 → 34 (11.1); US/Ireland
  40.8 → 34.8 (11.7); the 2023 global sample 39.1 → 34.5 (11.8); Portugal's workers ~41 → 36.5 (9.13).
- Days worked fell to **4.5** (UK) and **4.4** (US/Ireland), not 4.0 (11.2, 11.7). The UK authors read
  it as "modest amounts of work on the fifth day", and both reports name day-off work as one reason the
  cut fell short (11.1, 11.7).
- **11%** of UK staff had less than a full day off (11.3); in a minority of firms the day off was
  "weakly protected" (11.6); **62%** felt their pace rose (11.5).
- The leak path exists for salaried, degree-holding staff: in 2014, salaried workers reported 49 hours vs
  44 for full-time hourly, and a quarter reported 60+ (11.14, 11.15); in 2025, **51%** of workers with a
  degree did some work at home on days they worked, vs **19%** with high school only (11.16).

**Where it doesn't hold as stated:**
1. Part of the shortfall is **design, not leakage**: some firms started above 40 or planned less than an
   8-hour cut, and the programme minimum was 4 hours (11.9).
2. It is all **self-report** from volunteer, mostly office firms (11.10, 9.12).
3. **Nobody has measured it for salaried staff.** No study splits day-off work by exempt status, and the
   largest study's authors say salaried staff's HR records "register their hours as 40" whatever they
   work (11.11, 11.13).
4. Hours **still fell 4 to 6 a week** on average; the catch-up day is real for some, not the typical
   outcome.
5. For hourly plant workers the mechanism differs: hours are clocked, and Iceland's shift workers got
   real cuts that had to be paid for with new hires (13.6).

**Plain line for the script:** "On average, people got about half a day to most of a day back, not a
full day. Some worked part of the day off. And nobody has clocked salaried workers to say how much."
⛔ Not supportable: "salaried workers just work the fifth day anyway."

**Pushback to put to Lucas:** his instinct holds for the salaried and leaks for them by design of the
law (nothing counts their hours); it fails for hourly workers, who are paid for a worked fifth day. The
record supports "leaks, a bit", not "the fifth day becomes a workday".

## The reframe

The argument treats "four-day week" as one thing; it is three, and US law treats each differently. A
compressed 4×10 is the same 40 hours, and federally the boss can impose it at no extra cost. A reduced
32-hour week at full pay is a deal, or a change to the overtime line; no US law requires one. Fewer days
by law is what Brazil, Mexico and Chile are doing, and it takes long weeks down to 40, not 40 down to 32.

So the real question is not four days or five. It is **which mechanism counts your hours**:
- **hourly:** the overtime line counts them, by the week (and in a few states, by the day);
- **union:** the contract counts them, and decides the shape;
- **salaried exempt:** nothing counts them. That is why the catch-up day is both legal and invisible.

"Should the law shorten your week, or your boss?" becomes: **should the line that counts your hours be
drawn by law, by your employer, or by a contract you vote on?**

## Both sides quietly agree

**Hours on the schedule are not hours worked, and it only works when the work is redesigned.** Portugal's
firms that changed two or more processes almost all kept the shorter week (8% reverted vs 38%, 9.15);
the pilots' authors say self-report is all they have (11.10, 11.11); Utah's auditors could not measure
productivity at all (12.4); Japan's legal cut moved actual hours by a seventh of an hour (14.12). Law
or boss, both sides accept that the label changes nothing unless someone redesigns the work and someone
counts the hours. And both accept that a plant that runs around the clock has to cover the line, so a
shorter week there means more crews or more overtime, never a free day (Iceland's shift workers needed
new hires, 13.6; the Chevron 12s were built to cost the same, 6.18).

## Verdict framing

No winner. The verdict hands the viewer a dial they can use at work tomorrow: **who draws the line that
counts your hours?** Four settings, each with a real institution behind it and a documented failure:

1. **The law caps the week** (Brazil's amendment, Mexico, Chile). Reaches everyone. Failure: France
   1982 cost jobs among the workers it covered; legal hours fall faster than actual hours (Japan, Korea).
2. **The law prices the long hours** (the FLSA's 40, California's 8-hour day, H.R. 10323's 32). Leaves the
   shape to the employer but makes long weeks cost. Failure: reaches no salaried exempt worker, and no
   32-hour bill has passed anywhere in the US.
3. **The contract decides** (U.S. Steel's 60% vote, GM and Stellantis both signing). The workers on the
   schedule get a say. Failure: covers 8.4% of plant workers, and even the UAW could not win 32 hours.
4. **The employer decides** (the pilots, Ford's consult-and-switch, every non-union plant). Fast and
   tailored where the firm wants it. Failure: works where firms choose it; Utah's imposed 4×10 was
   reversed; 11% of UK pilot staff never got a full day off.

The viewer's own question to take home: **which of the three weeks do I have, and who counts its hours?**
If you are hourly, the overtime line; if you are in a union, your contract; if you are salaried, nobody,
unless your employer or a future law decides to.

## Make it Matter — the viewer's own week

- Open on the three weeks in "The viewer's moment", not on a statistic. The research desk follows one
  week at a time: **the clocked week** (what federal law counts, the 36/48 rotation, Oregon and
  California), **the salaried week** (the catch-up day, what the pilots measured), **the contract week**
  (Ford vs U.S. Steel), then **the country that's voting** (Brazil).
- Ringmaster beats the viewer is thinking: "Wait, my 12-hour shift isn't overtime?" "So my Fridays off
  are just a label?" "Who actually voted on my schedule?" "Is a 12 more dangerous?"
- The comparator a viewer can picture: the "40-hour week" is not the average. Private employees averaged
  about 34 paid hours in September; production workers in plants about 42, including 4 of overtime.
- End beats on "Hold that."

## Where the record leans (no false balance)

- **On well-being, for firms that choose it:** leans clearly **for** the shorter week (9.1, 9.7, 9.13),
  with the authors' own upper-bound caveat (9.10).
- **On whether the pilots license a law:** leans **against**, in the pilot authors' own words (9.16) and
  the critics' (10.1–10.3). Say so plainly; do not balance it with an advocate's adjective.
- **On whether law can shorten the week:** it can and has, but **less than written** (Japan 0.14, Korea's
  cap reduced but did not end long weeks), and with mixed job effects (France 1982 vs the 35 hours vs
  Portugal 1996). In US history, **mostly wages, partly law** (14.15–14.16).
- **On compressed weeks imposed from the top:** the best-documented US case (Utah) leans **against** the
  claims made for it (savings overstated, productivity unmeasured, reversed). Workers liked it.
- **On 12-hour shifts:** per-shift risk rises with length (settled); per-week comparison of rotations is
  **not settled**. Do not let either seat claim it.
- **On the catch-up day:** leans toward **"leaks, a bit"**, self-reported; unmeasured for salaried staff.
- **On the question itself (law vs boss):** the record does not settle it, because it is a question of
  who should decide. What the record does settle is that each lever has reached a different set of
  workers, and that the US has no legal lever on the week at all, only on its price. The debate is
  genuinely two-sided; the claims inside it are not, and the script holds each seat to the record above.

## Script traps

**The three definitions**
- ⛔ Never say "four-day week" bare. Say compressed, reduced, or by law. The laws abroad are cuts **to
  40**; Mexico caps hours and keeps one rest day in six; Belgium's right is to **compress**, not reduce.
- ⛔ A 4×10 is 40 hours. Ford's 4×10 is not a 32-hour week; the UAW did not win 32 in 2023.

**US law**
- ⛔ "Federal law says 8 hours a day" is a myth; federal overtime is weekly, and the Act does not
  "generally" require daily overtime. "Generally" is load-bearing: never say "no daily overtime anywhere".
- ⛔ The law does not cap the week at 40; 40 is the price line, not a limit.
- ⛔ "Only CA, AK, NV and CO have daily overtime" is wrong: add Oregon's plants (and a cap), and Colorado's
  rule covers all employers.
- ⛔ H.R. 10323 would add overtime past 8 hours **in a day**, so every 10-hour day owes 2 hours; not "a
  4×10 only owes overtime past 32". It has not passed and has no committee action.
- ⛔ "Salaried" is not "exempt". The $844 salary level **did** take effect (July 1 – Nov 15, 2024); the
  $1,128 level never did.
- ⛔ The hospital 8/80 averaging rule does not reach factories. A 36/48 rotation is never "basically 40".

**Plants and unions**
- ⛔ Say **which contract**: Ford consults; GM and Stellantis need both signatures; U.S. Steel needs 60% of
  affected workers. The blanket line that is true: "for about nine in ten plant workers, there's no
  contract, so the boss decides."
- ⛔ Ford's "three twelves" make Saturday, Sunday and holidays regular work days. Don't sell 3×12 as
  "weekends off".
- ⛔ 12-hour rotations are often built to be pay-neutral (Chevron's 0.977); don't call 12s a raise.
- ⛔ Cite Ford's 2023 **changed pages**; the "Volume I" book is the 2015 agreement.
- ⛔ The Chevron MOA is an **oil-production** unit, not a refinery.
- ⛔ Pilot manufacturers are anecdotes: one small brewery, one power-outage week ("a small manufacturer",
  not "another manufacturer"). No US plant case with numbers exists in the record.

**Safety figures**
- ⛔ **27.5%, not "28%"**, for 12-hour vs 8-hour shifts, and it is **Folkard & Lombardi 2006**, a model,
  not Folkard & Tucker. Spoken: "about a quarter more risk". On screen: 27.5%.
- ⛔ The night-shift 27.9% is a **different** number (timing, not length). Never merge them.
- ⛔ The 37% (Dembe) is 12+ hour days across all industries, an association; not "a 3×12 is 37% more
  dangerous". Pair it with "the 8-vs-12 evidence is equivocal".
- ⛔ Texas City is a month of 12s with no days off, not a normal rotation.
- ⛔ "The only BLS shift data are from 2004" is wrong; use 2024-25 (15.3% of manufacturing workers on
  non-day shifts, 2.4% rotating). No count of 4×10 or 3×12 workers exists.

**The pilots and the famous numbers**
- ⛔ **Microsoft Japan's "+40%"**: Microsoft Japan itself said (2019-11-08) the figure was not achieved by
  the trial alone. Never air it as the trial's result.
- ⛔ **Iceland's "86%"** is contracts that cut hours **or give the right to**, not 86% on a four-day week.
  The public-sector "13 minutes a day" is what the agreements fixed for daytime workers; the union says
  most workplaces took the maximum 4 hours (to 36). Neither measures hours worked. If aired, say which is
  which. Iceland is public services, not plants.
- ⛔ Every pilot hours figure is **self-reported**: "people reported", never "time records show".
- ⛔ The UK "+35%" and US/Ireland "+37.55%" revenue figures are year-on-year comparisons on a minority of
  firms; the trial-period change was +1.4% (UK, 23 firms) and +8.14% (US/Ireland, 16 firms).
- ⛔ The Nature paper's dose-response holds for **individuals**, not firms; its controls are 12 firms that
  attended an information session and did not join.
- ⛔ Gallup's 49 vs 44 is the **2014** survey, self-reported.
- ⛔ Do not borrow from the Senate testimony that says "Japan cut the week from 46 to 30 hours": false.

**Abroad and history**
- ⛔ Brazil: passed both houses (Senate 75–1, 2026-10-14); never "in force" or "the law there now"
  until promulgated, and no promulgation date on air. "Almost 40 million" is the
  Senate President's claim; the rapporteur says 37 million; attribute either. Six-day weeks hit services
  and retail hardest; don't say Brazilian plants run 6x1. CNI's R$267bn is the lobby's own estimate.
- ⛔ Germany: no "weekly 48-hour max from 2027-01-01". Not used.
- ⛔ Spain's defeat is secondary-sourced: "narrowly rejected", not the 178–170 count.
- ⛔ US history: "mostly wages, partly law", never "the law did nothing".

**Executive quotes (re-dated; none is 2026)**
- Dimon, "three-and-a-half days a week": Bloomberg TV, 2023-10-02. Gates, "probably okay": Trevor Noah's
  What Now?, 2023-11-21. Yuan, "three days, four days a week": The New York Times, 2025-09-14 (the
