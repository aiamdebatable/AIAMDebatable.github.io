## Script derivations (figures the brief works out itself)

All are arithmetic on rows above; none is a source's own figure.

- **A 36/48 rotation under federal law** (row 2.3): two weeks, 84 hours worked; the 48-hour week owes 8
  hours at time and a half, so the fortnight pays 76 + (8 × 1.5) = 88 hours' worth of straight pay for 84
  hours worked, about 5% more than 84 straight hours. Chevron's 0.977 straight-time factor (row 6.18) takes
  that back to about 86.
- **A 3×12 under federal law** (row 2.1): 36 hours, no overtime. In a non-union California plant on an
  alternative workweek (row 3.4), the 2 hours past 10 each day are overtime: 6 hours a week. In an Oregon
  plant (row 3.6), a 12-hour day is 2 hours over the 10-hour line, allowed as overtime (up to 13).
- **Under H.R. 10323 if it passed** (row 5.1): every hour past 8 in a day, up to 12, is overtime: a 4×10
  carries 8 overtime hours a week, a 3×12 carries 12.
- **The catch-up day** (rows 11.1, 11.2, 11.7, 11.8, 11.13): reported hours fell 4 to 6 a week against an
  8-hour promise (UK 4.0; US/Ireland 6.0; global sample 4.6); days worked fell by about a third of a day
  (UK, 4.86 → 4.52) to two-thirds of a day (US/Ireland, 5.00 → 4.36). "About half a day to most of a day
  back, not a full day."
- **Exempt share** (row 4.7): 37.9 ÷ 167.3 = 22.7%, "about one in four or five".
- **Plant workers without a contract** (row 6.1): 100 − 8.4 = 91.6%, "about nine in ten".
- **UK revenue sample** (row 9.4): 23 of 61 firms = 38%.

## Fairness audit

- **The law side is not written beyond its evidence.** Brazil has not voted; Mexico and Chile cap hours
  from long weeks (48 and 45), which is the five-day week arriving, not the four-day week; the US bills
  move only a price line and none has passed. The record of legal cuts elsewhere is mixed (France 1982 cost
  jobs among those affected; the 35 hours did not move net employment and intensified work; Portugal and
  Japan held monthly pay but actual hours fell far less than legal hours). All of that is in the brief.
- **The boss side is not written beyond its evidence.** The pilots are volunteer, mostly office firms,
  self-reported, and run or written up by organisations that campaign for the policy; the strongest
  "leave it to the boss" line (Portugal's authors) is a statement about method, not proof that firms will
  spread it on their own. For about 9 in 10 plant workers "the boss decides" means the boss alone.
- **Advocacy and lobby sources are marked**: Autonomy, Alda, 4 Day Week Global (advocates); CNI (industry
  lobby); union booklets; Circadian (a 12-hour scheduling consultancy). None carries a figure on air
  without attribution.
- **No politics.** Brazil is about the amendment and the vote, never a party or a personality; the US bill
  is named by its title, not its sponsor's party. The executive quotes are dated and attributed, and
  Huang's paraphrase is not used.
- **Lucas's own hypothesis is tested, not adopted.** The catch-up day is reported where it holds (some
  leakage, self-reported) and where it does not (no one has clocked salaried staff; hours still fell 4–6).
- **Plant workers are not a footnote.** The contract differences (Ford consult, GM and Stellantis both
  sign, U.S. Steel 60% vote, Smithfield company decides) are reported as the contracts say, not as union
  or company claims; the 12-hour safety record is split into what is settled and what is not.
