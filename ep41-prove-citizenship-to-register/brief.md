## The pitch, in one line

**When you register to vote, you tick a box swearing you are a citizen. On the federal form, and in states
like California, nobody asks for paper.** In Utah, when the state finally checked, 14 people who ticked **"No"** had been registered
anyway. In Kansas, when the state asked everyone for paper, it held back or cancelled about 1,100
applications for every noncitizen it stopped (the court's word: "denied registration"; applicants, not
proven citizens). Between those two failures sits the real setting: **take your word, check
the state's own records, or ask you for a document, and what happens to the people the records can't
confirm.**

## The viewer's moment

One registration form: **yours**. (Invented and unnamed; never a real person.)

You registered at the licence counter, or online, or on a paper form. You wrote your name and date of
birth, probably gave a licence number or the last four digits of your Social Security number (Ohio's
rules, for one, require it), ticked "I am a United States citizen" and signed, under penalty of perjury
(log 1, 6). Then:

- **Your word was the proof.** On the federal form, and in California, the sworn box is the citizenship
  proof. The state checks that you are who you say you are, and DOJ's own brief puts it plainly: that
  check "establishes identity, not citizenship" (log 4, 5).
- **Someone may check later, against records that lag.** Utah ran every one of its 2,069,640 records
  past licence files and a federal database. About 2 million cleared at once, and 5,007 never did. Those
  5,007 were unconfirmed, which is not the same as being noncitizens. More than half say they were born
  in the United States (log 65, 66, 72). If you became a citizen after you got your Social Security
  number, the federal record may still say you are not one (log 32, 110, 112).
- **Or someone may ask you for paper.** Could you put your hand on your birth certificate, passport or
  naturalisation certificate by tomorrow? About 1 in 11 adult citizens say they couldn't (log 106). If
  your name changed when you married, the birth certificate alone may not match (log 101, 102).

The episode follows that form through the box, the clerk, the records check, the letter, and the dial.
It ends on two things the viewer can do with their own registration this month.

## The question

**When you register to vote, should the state take your word, check its records, or ask for paper?**
The three settings are: **1. swear** (the oath is the proof), **2. check, then ask** (the state checks
what it already holds and asks only the people it can't confirm), and **3. documents for everyone**.
Under all three sits a second question the record forces: **while someone is unconfirmed, what can they
vote on?**

## Why now

On **2026-10-15** the Justice Department sued California (C.D. Cal. No. 2:26-cv-12025). On **10-19** it
asked for a temporary restraining order, to be granted by November 3, ordering California to "set aside
vote-by-mail ballots from persons identified by the Federal Government likely to be noncitizens" and to
verify their citizenship "before tabulating their vote" (log 10–12). The list, delivered on 10-13 with a
48-hour deadline, names "just over 20,000" people statewide (log 13, 21). It was built **outside** the
federal SAVE database. Federal officials matched a copy of California's roll, as it stood in **August
2025**, against commercial data and immigration files, then scored the matches and reviewed them by hand
(log 16, 17). The sworn filings give **no error rate**. The only accuracy note is a footnote: about 189
records with "minor data discrepancies" (log 18). California registers on a sworn statement, and its
Secretary of State refused, calling the federal belief "unsupported and likely inaccurate" (log 4). The
same day, DOJ announced charges against **28** lawful permanent residents, all on the list. They are
**charged, not convicted**, and never named (log 23). As of 10-19 no judge was assigned and no hearing was
set (log 11).

Behind it: on **2026-10-09** a federal court in Washington vacated DOJ's policy of running state voter
lists through SAVE and pressing states to remove the people flagged, finding DOJ ignored that its
"citizenship records known to be unreliable" would lead to citizens being removed. DOJ appealed on 10-16
(log 27–29). Whether that ruling reaches the California list is open (log 30). On **2026-09-25** the
Supreme Court let the overhauled SAVE run for now, describing a check-then-ask process: if it "is unable
to confirm that an individual is a citizen, the agency is required to contact the individual and request
that he provide proof" (log 31). Utah, under its new law, has mailed **4,307** voters ballots with
federal races only, according to the Lieutenant Governor's office as reported by Utah News Dispatch (log
74). On **Tuesday 2026-12-08** the Supreme Court hears **RNC v. Mi Familia Vota** (No. 25-1017), on
whether a state may require documentary proof from people who register **on the state's own form**, and
whether it may cancel noncitizens' registrations within 90 days of an election (log 34, 35).

**So in one week, both failure modes ended up in one courtroom**: a sworn box that DOJ says 28 people
lied on, and a records match that went from "as many as 190,832" in July to "just over 20,000" now, with
no accuracy figure (log 14, 23, 24).

## Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

**The law**
- **Only citizens may vote in federal elections, and lying about it is a crime.** Falsely claiming
  citizenship to register: up to five years; a noncitizen voting in a federal election: up to one; and
  unlawful voting makes a noncitizen deportable (log 2). The federal form's oath warns of all three, with
  "may" (log 1). (A third statute, 52 U.S.C. 20511, is not paraphrased on air; log 3.)
- **The federal form is the oath, and states must take it as it is.** In 2013, 7–2, the Supreme Court
  held a state cannot require "a Federal Form applicant to submit information beyond that required by
  the form itself". A state's own form "may require information the Federal Form does not". A state that
  wants documents added to the federal form must show "a mere oath will not suffice" (log 37, 39).
- **The body that writes the federal form can't act.** The Election Assistance Commission has no
  commissioners (log 46). The 2025 executive order telling it to add documents has been struck down in
  two federal courts and is on appeal (log 40, 41). The 2026 order builds a federal "State Citizenship
  List" for states to check against; it adds no documents at registration, and states need not use it
  (log 43–45).
- **The Supreme Court has not decided the documents question.** The September SAVE order is a stay, not
  a ruling on the merits (log 31). Mi Familia Vota is argued on 12-08 and is limited to the state's own
  form, plus 90-day cancellations (log 35). Since August 2024 Arizona may reject state-form applications
  without proof (log 36).
- **Congress has not changed it.** The SAVE America Act passed the House 218–213 on 2026-02-11 and is
  stalled in the Senate; two cloture votes failed in March (log 47, 51). It would require documents, shown
  in person, for every federal registration, federal form included (log 48). It has a narrow fallback, an
  oath **plus other evidence**, judged by an official (log 49). Only a REAL ID **that indicates
  citizenship** counts (log 50). A bill, not law.
- **Individual checks are allowed even close to an election.** The Supreme Court's September order notes
  that "individualized inquiries … are permitted under federal law during this period" (log 31).

**The states (status 2026-10-19)**
- **California:** sworn attestation; identity checks; the state refused DOJ (log 4, 5).
- **Ohio:** licence number or SSN-4 and an oath. HB 54 only limits the licence office's **electronic**
  sign-up to people who show proof; the paper oath form stays (log 6, 7). The Sixth Circuit let it run
  without deciding the federal-law question (log 8). ⛔ Ohio is not documents-for-all.
- **New Hampshire:** after a full trial, a federal court ruled on **2026-05-28** that repealing the sworn
  affidavit was unconstitutional; it did **not** decide whether requiring proof is (log 52, 53). The
  First Circuit refused to pause it (log 54). New Hampshire today is **documents or a sworn affidavit**.
  The court says the affidavit is a way of proving citizenship, not an exception to the rule (log 53).
- **Utah (HB 209):** from elections on or after 2026-11-01, a match in licence or SAVE records **counts
  as** documentary proof; whoever stays unconfirmed may vote only a federal ballot (log 56).
- **Arizona:** two tracks. Documents give a full ballot; the oath alone gives federal races only (log 60).
- **South Dakota (SB 175):** a new registrant without proof is a "federal voter" (log 59).
- **Kansas:** its 2013–16 documents rule was struck down, as preempted for licence-office registrants
  and as an unconstitutional burden (log 38).
- **Florida (HB 991):** signed, takes effect 2027-01-01, being challenged in federal court. What exactly
  it requires was not read at the primary; say no more than that (log 57, 58).
- **California Proposition 39 (11-03 ballot):** ID at the polls plus a records check of existing
  attestations. It adds **no** documents at registration (log 63, 64). ⛔ No verdict, ever.

**Noncitizens on the rolls: rare, real, and often an error somewhere upstream** (Utah: about half, 14 of 27; Kansas: "largely", the court's word)
- **Utah**, the cleanest full count: **27** confirmed noncitizens in 2,069,640 records (about **1 in
  77,000**), plus 25 probable (log 65). **13** of the 27 had voted at some point, the earliest in 2018 (log
  68).
- **Who made the mistake in Utah:** **14** of the 27 ticked "No, I am not a citizen" and were registered
  anyway (log 67). Licence records already said "not a citizen" for **19** of the 27, and **none**
  registered through the licence office; most used paper forms (log 69). But **34** confirmed or probable
  noncitizens ticked "Yes" (log 70).
- **Georgia 2024:** 20 of 8.2 million registrants, about **1 in 410,000**; nine had voted in earlier
  elections; they surfaced because they swore they weren't citizens to get out of jury duty (log 76,
  secondary). **Georgia 2026:** the state auditor traced about 2,570 *potential* noncitizens ever put on
  the rolls to administrative failures, including workers coding immigration documents as proof of
  citizenship (log 77, 78, secondary). These are potential cases, not confirmed ones.
- **Michigan:** 16 apparent noncitizens voted among more than 5.7 million ballots, about **1 in
  360,000** ballots (log 82, 83). Five were later charged (log 84).
- **Ohio:** 597 *apparent* noncitizens referred in August 2024, 138 of whom had voted, out of more than 8
  million registered (log 85, secondary). Hundreds referred over the years, a handful charged (log 87,
  direction only).
- **Kansas, under the oath:** 39 registered in 14 years, **0.002%**, about 1 in 45,000 (log 90, 91). The
  judge: "there is no iceberg; only an icicle, largely created by confusion and administrative error"
  (log 94). The state claimed 129 cases and filed **zero** criminal complaints (log 88).
- **Oregon, the licence office's own error** (⚠ not a noncitizen count): in September 2024 the state
  found **1,259** people registered through its motor-voter system without having proved citizenship;
  by October 7 it was **1,561** (log 146, 147). The count kept creeping up as old records surfaced; the
  Secretary of State's page (updated 2026-10-07) now says **about 1,950** (it gives both 1,952 and 1,948), 0.05% of Oregon's
  registered voters (log 149, 154). **44** of them voted, and the state says none of those votes could
  have changed an outcome (log 150). How many of the 44 were citizens is **not known**: the state says
  only "many" (log 151, UNRESOLVED). The cause: staff picking the wrong document from a drop-down, often
  a foreign passport coded as a U.S. one, plus a policy that coded American Samoa documents as proof
  (log 152). The state inactivated the registrations, told the people it was the DMV's fault and
  referred no one for prosecution (log 153). ⛔ Never "1,617": it is a sum (1,561 + 56), not an
  official total (log 148, 149).

**The documents rule's citizen cost**
- **Kansas:** **31,089** applicants blocked, about one in eight new registrations (log 89). Only **28**
  noncitizens tried to register while the law ran: about **1,100 applicants blocked for each noncitizen
  it stopped** (log 90, 92). The court found the "clear majority" of those blocked were citizens; fewer
  than 1 in 100 were confirmed noncitizens (log 93). The law deterred "substantially more eligible
  Kansans" than ineligible ones (log 94).
- **New Hampshire, 2025:** at least **146** people turned away for lack of documents, recorded by the
  court from volunteer observers at about 20 of more than 100 towns. The state kept no count (log 99).
  There was no provisional ballot to fall back on (log 55). The name-match rule fell on women **15 to 1**
  (log 101). Women with maiden-name birth certificates were turned away; one recently divorced woman never
  came back (log 102). A naturalised citizen holding a U.S. passport was wrongly told he also needed his
  naturalisation papers (log 103). The state made certified birth records free in October 2025; in four
  months, 21 were issued for voting (log 105).
- **Nationally:** about **1 in 11** adult citizens (21.3 million) say they couldn't find a citizenship
  document by tomorrow; about **1 in 60** (3.8 million) have none at all (log 106, 107).

**What the paper costs** (top-up C; fees as of 2026-10-19 unless dated)
- **A passport:** a first-time adult book is **$165** ($130 to the State Department, $35 to the
  acceptance facility); a passport card alone **$65**. Set by regulation, 22 CFR 22.1 (log 131).
  Routine processing runs to weeks, not days (log 132, APPROXIMATE: the last readable official page is
  July's, so no week count on screen as current).
- **A certified birth certificate by mail, from the state office, in the states we read:** Florida $9,
  Texas $22, New York State $30, California $31 (log 133); Ohio about $21.50, from an undated CDC
  listing (log 134, APPROXIMATE: never as Ohio's official fee). ⛔ Say "in the states we read", never
  "nationally": five state offices, no county or city offices.
- **A replacement naturalisation certificate (Form N-565):** **$555** on paper, **$505** online (log
  135). How long it takes is not known (log 136, UNRESOLVED).
- **Free for voting:** **New Hampshire** (free certified copies for registration since October 2025;
  21 issued in four months, log 105) and **Kansas**, by statute, for a Kansas birth certificate requested
  for voting or the registration citizenship rule (log 137). **Texas's** free birth record is only for
  its Election Identification Certificate, which is voter photo ID, not registration (log 138). No other
  state's free-copy rule was read at a primary, so never "only two states".
- Whether a fee for the paper is a "poll tax" is a legal argument this record does not settle (council
  question 3, Lucas's call).

**The records check's cost: it errs the other way**
- **Utah says it of its own system:** its records are "reliable in confirming whether an individual is a
  citizen" and "not reliable in indicating an individual was not a citizen, without considering other
  factors" (log 71). Keep the last four words: the claim is about the match alone.
- **SSA, 2006:** about **7 in 100** records marked "non-citizen" belonged to people who had since become
  citizens and never told SSA (log 110). The error also runs the other way (7.7% of "foreign-born
  citizen" records were not citizens). It is a 2006 sample of records and has never been re-measured.
- **Flagged lists shrink when someone checks:** Florida 2012, 180,000 names → 2,600, and in Miami-Dade
  about a third of those checked proved citizenship (log 114, secondary); Texas 2019, about 25,000 of
  ~95,000 "should not have been included" (log 115); Texas 2025, **578 of 2,724** flagged through SAVE
  were citizens, **about 1 in 5**, most because their passport data hadn't reached SAVE yet (log 116,
  secondary); Missouri, more than half of one county's list (log 117, secondary); Georgia 2009, more than
  half of 7,007 (log 119).
- **The overhauled SAVE has no published error rate** (log 120). It ran 59.7 million voter checks between
  January 2025 and April 2026 (log 121), and 27 states use it (log 33).
- **A naturalised plaintiff at the Supreme Court** "has demonstrated that SSA records do not accurately
  reflect that she is now a naturalized citizen" (log 32).

## Contested (the real fight)

**GREEN — "Prove it once, at the door" (documents at registration, with a free way to get them).**
On the federal form, and in states like California, the sworn box is checked for identity, not for
citizenship (log 5). Where it has been tested, it failed in both directions. In Utah, 14 people ticked "No" and were registered, and 34 noncitizens
ticked "Yes" (log 67, 70). Rare is not zero. Utah found 27 confirmed and 25 probable, and 13 of the 27 had
voted (log 65, 68). Michigan counted 16 apparent noncitizen ballots (log 82). In North Carolina in 2016,
41 lawfully present noncitizens voted (log 118). **Every one of those counts is a floor.** Audits find
only what they can match, and the cases surface by accident: Georgia's 20 turned up because they told a
jury office they weren't citizens (log 76). The oath's penalties are rarely used. Kansas claimed 129
cases and filed zero complaints (log 88), and in Ohio hundreds were referred and a handful charged (log
87). A rule that relies on prosecution after the fact therefore relies on something that rarely happens.
The law lets a state ask for more on its own form (log 37), and since 2024 Arizona has (log 36). On the
burden: the 21.3 million counts people who couldn't find a document **by tomorrow**, not people who can't
get one. The hard core with no document at all is about 1 in 60 (log 106, 107). States can make the
paper free: New Hampshire did, and Kansas law already bars a fee for a birth certificate wanted for
registering (log 105, 137). **And the public asks for it, across parties.** Every registration poll in
the record shows a majority in favour: Gallup, 83–15 for first-time registrants, with two in three
Democrats in favour (66%); Economist/YouGov, 59–29 for "register or re-register"; CBS News/YouGov, 66–34
for a passport or birth certificate; POLITICO/Public First, 52–18 (log 139–143). Independents favour it
in all three polls that report them (84%; 52–31; 61–39). A rule most voters say they want is a rule more voters can
trust. Whether it would actually raise trust in results is still an argument, not a measured figure, and
the team says so.

**GOLD — "Take the oath, and let the state do the checking" (the sworn box, plus the state checking the
records it already holds, without making citizens carry paper).** The trade is lopsided wherever it has
been measured. Kansas blocked about 1,100 applicants for each noncitizen its law stopped, and the court
found the clear majority were citizens (log 92, 93). The burden lands on particular people. In New
Hampshire it fell on married and divorced women (15 to 1), on a naturalised citizen with a passport in
his hand, and on at least 146 people turned away with no provisional ballot (log 99, 101–103, 55). Many
traced cases are not lies at the counter. They are mistakes inside government (about half of Utah's). Utah's licence records
already said "not a citizen" for 19 of its 27, and 14 had ticked "No"; a clerk registered them anyway
(log 67, 69). Georgia's auditor traced its potential cases to administrative failures (log 78). Oregon's
licence office registered about 1,950 people who had not proved citizenship, mostly by picking the wrong
document from a drop-down, and the state says many of them are citizens (log 149, 151, 152). Kansas's
judge called it an icicle of "confusion and administrative error" (log 94). Gold's argument (untested
in the record): the agency already held the answer, so the fix is the agency. And the records can't carry the weight of
a rejection either. Utah says its checks can't show who is *not* a citizen (log 71). SSA's own audit
found 7 in 100 "non-citizen" records were citizens (log 110). Texas's 2025 list was about 1 in 5 citizens
(log 116), and the overhauled SAVE has no published error rate (log 120). Lying is already a felony and a
deportable offence (log 2). The California list shows the risk of treating a match as proof. "As many as
190,832" became "just over 20,000", and no figure for how many are right was ever given (log 14, 18).

**The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:**

- *Gold at green:* **"documents for everyone" barely exists without a back door.** New Hampshire's
  affidavit is back (log 53). The House bill keeps an oath-plus-evidence fallback (log 49). Arizona,
  Utah and South Dakota let the unconfirmed vote federal races (log 56, 59, 60). Kansas and New
  Hampshire are the two versions with no back door, and both were struck in court (log 38, 53). **A
  documents rule runs on databases too.** Arizona's licence agency wrongly marked about 218,000 long-time
  voters as having proved citizenship, and the state's top court refused to move them to federal-only
  ballots (log 61, 62). Oregon's licence office recorded foreign passports as U.S. ones (log 152): the
  paper was shown, and a clerk's keystroke still decided. **"Free" didn't move many people:** 21
  certificates in four months (log 105). **And where it isn't free, it costs real money:** $165 for a
  passport, $9 to $31 for a birth certificate in the states we read, $555 to replace a naturalisation
  certificate (log 131, 133, 135). **Support depends on the question.** Asked about "the SAVE Act" by
  name, the same CBS sample that said 66–34 split 28–31, with 41% not sure (log 141, 142).
  **Nobody knows how many Kansas applicants held the papers.** The court called the state's cure
  argument "sheer speculation" (log 96).
- *Green at gold:* **a records error is a reason to ask, not a reason to stop checking.** Utah's checks
  confirmed 99.72% of records, and the ask fell on 0.24% (log 65). When it wrote to those people, 488 of
  the ones who proved citizenship were confirmed through state birth records (log 74, secondary). **The records
  check is what found the 27 the oath let through** (log 65). **"It's already a crime" only deters if
  someone is prosecuted**, and in Kansas no one was (log 88). **Oregon is the records path failing:**
  registration fed straight from licence records put about 1,950 people on the rolls who had never
  proved citizenship, and 44 of them voted (log 149, 150). **Utah's 14 are not a records failure.**
  The box said "No" and the oath regime still produced a voter. **The 21.3 million is not 21.3 million
  people who can't register.** The survey's "tomorrow" wording is its honest weak point (log 106, 109).
  ⚖ But fairness cuts both ways here. The only published critique is of the survey's 2006 predecessor,
  and the one independent measure (the Bipartisan Policy Center's) comes out **higher**: about 12% of

### Could it swing an election? The frustration, worked through

_Top-up E (2026-10-20, from Lucas's pitch note): every margin from official results only (state
canvasses and results files, the National Archives, the Clerk of the House); quote-checked 65/65 (log
Part 12, log 155–177). A Make-it-Matter beat for the viewer who holds this fear: take it seriously, then
measure it._

**The fear, said plainly:** noncitizens voting could tip close elections. It is not a strange fear,
because close races are real. In 2024 California's 13th congressional district was decided by **187
votes** (log 168). In 2020 Iowa's 2nd was decided by **6** (log 169). If a seat can turn on six votes,
"how many noncitizens are voting?" is a fair question to ask, and the episode asks it straight.

**Three honesty rules for this beat (they hold on air too):**
1. **The found counts are floors.** Each is what one check could see (Utah a records review, Michigan a
   post-election audit, Georgia jury-duty sworn statements), not a total. The true number is unknown,
   and no source supports scaling the counts up either (log Part 12, log 170).
2. **The comparison shows that today's FOUND numbers are far below recent margins in the same states.
   It does not show that a close race could never be affected.** The closest margins nationally are
   small: 6 votes (Iowa's 2nd, 2020), 187, 653 and 799 (the three closest House races of 2024). Say so
   (log 168, 169).
3. **"Noncitizens", never "illegals".** The record's cases include lawful permanent residents: DOJ's 28
   charged in California are all green-card holders (log 23), and North Carolina's 41 were lawfully
   present noncitizens (log 118).

**The found counts against the closest margins, in the same states (votes, not percentages):**

| state | closest race (official results) | margin | found noncitizen votes | how far apart |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Utah | U.S. House, 4th district, 2020 (closest federal race 2020–24) | **3,765** (log 155) | **8** voted in the 2020 general, statewide, so at most 8 in that district (log 158) | margin about **470 times** the count (8 is 0.21% of it); all 13 who ever voted, about 290 times |
| Utah | U.S. Senate, 2022 (closest statewide) | 112,016 (log 156) | 1 voted in 2022 (log 158) | about 112,000 times |
| Michigan | U.S. Senate, 2024 (closest single-seat statewide race 2020–24) | **19,006** (log 160) | **16** apparent noncitizen ballots, the same election (log 82, 83) | about **1,190 times** (0.08%) |
| Georgia | President, 2020 (closest statewide 2020–24) | **11,779** after the recount (log 165) | **9** had ever voted, years not given (log 76) | about **1,310 times** (0.08%) |
| Georgia | U.S. House, 7th district, 2020 (closest federal) | 10,336 (log 166) | at most 9 | about 1,150 times |

- **Georgia's 2020 margin is 11,779, not 12,670.** 12,670 is the first certificate (November 20, 2020),
  which the Clerk's book prints; the amended certificate after the recount is final (log 165).
- **Same-year rule.** Michigan's 16 are from the November 2024 election itself. Utah's 8 are 2020's.
  Georgia's 9 have no years, so their comparison is the loosest. Michigan's closest federal race, the
  10th district in 2022 (1,600 votes, log 159), has no 2022 count to set against it: even if all 16 of
  2024's had fallen there, the margin would be 100 times bigger, and that is an illustration only.
- **Every other statewide race in these states was wider** (Utah, log 157; Michigan, log 161–163;
  Georgia, log 167).
- **Plain reading for the script:** in every race above with a same-state count, the margin is hundreds
  to thousands of times the number found (log 170). Only in the very smallest contests, decided by single
  digits (Iowa's 2nd in 2020 by 6; Florida's 20th district Democratic special primary in 2021 by 5, log
  175), is a count this size even the same order as the margin, and there no court or official record
  has found that it changed anything.

**Has a court or official record ever found that noncitizen votes changed an outcome?** Not in the sources
searched (log 176): we found none, which is not the same as "there are none".
- **The one federal contest built on it: California's 46th, 1996 (Dornan v. Sanchez).** Sanchez won by
  984, then 979 after the recount (log 171). A House task force documented 624 people who had illegally
  registered (82 of them naturalised before they voted) and 748 invalid votes in all, of which 124 were
  absentee ballots the county voided, so not all were noncitizen votes (log 172). That was "substantially
  less than the 979 vote margin", and the contest was dismissed; the minority disputed even the 624 (log
  173). The closest official test found fewer invalid votes than the margin.
- **Oregon's licence-office error:** of the 44 who voted, the state says "none of those votes could have
  impacted the outcome of an election" (log 150).
- **Florida 2021:** a volunteer group claims that 5 people later removed as noncitizens voted in the
  20th district's special election, decided by 5. ⛔ **UNRESOLVED, never aired as fact** (log 174): it is
  not a court or official finding, it does not show how anyone voted, in which primary, or that they were
  noncitizens on the day, and it says many removals are self-requests made before naturalising. The race
  was a closed Democratic primary, not a general election (log 175).
- **A separate question, never mixed into these counts:** some places let noncitizens vote in local races
  by law, such as San Francisco's school-board elections since 2016 (log 177). That is lawful, not fraud.

**A line the script can build on:** "In the states that counted, the closest races were hundreds to
thousands of times bigger than the noncitizen votes anyone found. That doesn't mean a race could never be
that close: Iowa's 2nd was decided by six. But in the one federal contest built on that charge, a race
decided by fewer than a thousand votes, the House found fewer invalid votes than the margin."

## Verdict framing

"The design that survives both teams' objections, and the dial you still have to set." Candidates, each
to be **earned in the debate** and dropped if it is not:

- **Survives:** **the oath alone isn't a check, and neither a document nor a database is a verdict on a
  person.** Green's point survives because the box demonstrably fails (Utah's 14 and 34), the counts are
  floors, and the oath's penalties are rarely used. Gold's survives because asking everyone for paper
  has a measured citizen cost far larger than what it stopped, and because the records confirm citizens
  well but can't show who is *not* one. The reframe survives on both sides: many traced cases were let in
  by an error inside government (about half of Utah's), and Utah's own records cross-check is what found
  them. Whether a counter document would also have caught them is untested; don't claim it either way.
- **Fell:** "Noncitizens are voting in large numbers" (every full count is a fraction of a hundredth of
  a percent). "Noncitizen registration never happens" (Utah found 27, and 13 had voted). "The oath
  protects the rolls" (14 ticked "No" and were registered). "Documents are a minor hassle" (Kansas,
  1,100 to 1). "Everyone already has a REAL ID that counts" (only one that indicates citizenship). "The
  databases can tell who isn't a citizen" (Utah says they can't). "DOJ's list proves 20,000 noncitizens
  are voting in California" (no error rate). "The Supreme Court has ruled on proof of citizenship" (it
  hasn't; December 8 is about the state form). "Ohio requires documents to register" (only for the
  electronic sign-up at the licence office). "Most people don't want proof at registration" (a majority
  favours it in every registration poll read, 52% to 83% depending on the wording).
- **The one dial the viewer sets, named and never set:** *who should carry the proof when you register
  — your word, the state's records, or your paper — and while you're unconfirmed, what may you vote on?*
  Three settings with their price tags, and the five answers for the unconfirmed beneath them. The
  research earned the price tag on each setting. It did **not** earn a right answer.

## The reframe

The shouting is **"trust voters" against "check voters"**, as if the oath were the only weak point. In
many traced cases it wasn't. In Utah, a clerk registered 14 of the 27 even though they ticked "No". The licence office's
own records already said "not a citizen" for 19 of the 27, and none of them came in through the licence
office (log 67, 69). In Georgia, workers coded immigration documents as proof of citizenship (log 78).
In Oregon, licence-office staff picked the wrong document from a drop-down, and about 1,950 people were
registered without having proved citizenship (log 149, 152).
In Kansas, a judge found "confusion and administrative error" (log 94). **The agency often already held the
answer; the failure was not using it.** (Whether a counter document would have caught them is untested.)

And checking records can't finish the job in the other direction: they confirm citizens and can't show
who is not one (log 71). **So every setting produces a pool of unconfirmed people**: Utah's 5,007 (more
than half say they were born here), Kansas's 31,089 (the clear majority citizens, the court found), and
California's 20,000, whose accuracy nobody has measured. The real choice is what happens to
that pool. You can take their word, ask them, limit them to federal races, set their ballots aside, or
turn them away. That decides **whose error the system accepts**: a rare noncitizen on the rolls, or a
measured number of citizens shut out. The record names who they tend to be: naturalised citizens whose
records lag, and women whose names have changed.

## Both sides quietly agree

**Nobody defends a noncitizen on the rolls.** Not California, whose objection is to acting
"based solely upon an unsupported and likely inaccurate" belief (log 4). Not Utah, whose office says its own matches can't show
noncitizenship (log 71). Not DOJ.

**Everyone treats a database flag as a lead, not a verdict.** DOJ's own sworn declaration says the
matched data "may be incomplete, outdated, or mismatched" and describes a manual review and "a
second-level review" (log 16). DOJ's letter asked California to "segregate—not cancel" (log 21). The
Supreme Court's order describes *check, then contact the person and ask* (log 31). Utah's review says
the database is "not reliable in indicating an individual was not a citizen, without considering other
factors" (log 71).

**Everyone keeps the oath.** The House bill, Utah's affidavit and New Hampshire's restored affidavit all
keep a sworn statement inside the strictest designs (log 49, 53, 72). **And everyone has run into the
same weak link: an agency feeding the rolls** (Utah's clerks, Georgia's coding, Arizona's licence
agency, Oregon's drop-down menu; log 67, 78, 61, 152). The fight is over **timing, scale and the status of the unconfirmed in the
meantime**, not over whether confirmed noncitizens belong on the rolls.

## Make it Matter — the viewer's own registration

- **The cold open (≤ ~100 words, premise before the object):** the question in plain words first: *"When
  you register to vote, should the state take your word, check its records, or ask for paper?"* Then the
  quick test: *"Where's your birth certificate right now? Could you put your hand on it by tomorrow
  morning?"* The turn: *"In Utah, when the state finally checked, it found 14 people who ticked 'No, I'm
  not a citizen' — and were registered anyway."* Say what this is **not**: not voter ID at the polls, not
  who wins in November. Then: *"Two teams argue it, the desk checks both, and you set the dial."*
- **The story, ep21-style: one form, followed.** Four beats, one concrete thing each:
  1. **What you did when you registered:** the box, the signature, the felony warning, the licence
     number. What the state checked: that you're you, not that you're a citizen (log 1, 5, 6). *"Hold
     that: nobody asked for your birth certificate."*
  2. **How often it goes wrong:** follow Utah's 2,069,640 records down the funnel. About 2 million cleared
     on licence files; the federal database cleared most of the other 74,000; 10,718 went to hand
     review; the end was 5,007 unconfirmed and **27** confirmed noncitizens (log 65, 66). Then the ringmaster asks:
     *"Wait — 14 of them ticked 'No'?"* "And a clerk registered them." The fear against the record:
     rare, real, and partly a clerk problem (log 67, 69). Georgia's 20 and Michigan's 16, each with its
     comparator.
  3. **What happens when you ask everyone for paper:** Kansas's 31,089, about 1,100 for each noncitizen
     stopped (log 89, 92). New Hampshire's women with maiden-name birth certificates, the divorced woman
     who never came back, and the naturalised citizen sent away holding his passport (log 102, 103).
     Then the viewer's own check, phrased as questions: *Did your name change when you married or
     divorced? Were you born in another state? Are your papers at your parents' house?* And the price
     if they're lost: $9 to $31 for a birth certificate by mail in the states we read, $165 for a first
     passport, $555 to replace a naturalisation certificate (log 131, 133, 135).
  4. **The middle path, and its catch:** Utah's letters and federal-only ballots (log 74). The
     database that confirms citizens and can't show who isn't one (log 71). The naturalised citizen
     whose Social Security record still says "non-citizen" (log 32, 110). Texas's list, where 1 in 5 were
     citizens (log 116). Then, dated, the California list: 20,000 names, records only, no error rate
     (log 13, 18). *"Hold that."*
- **The ringmaster asks what the viewer is thinking, twice:** *"So when I ticked that box, nobody
  checked?"* — "In places like California they check that you're you. Whether you're a citizen rests on
  your word, unless the state checks its records later." And: *"If the computer says I'm not a citizen, am I off the rolls?"* — "The
  Supreme Court's description is: they're supposed to contact you and ask." (log 31)
- **What the viewer can actually do** (information, never instruction on how to vote; every line points
  to an official source):
  1. **Know where your citizenship document is:** a birth certificate, a passport, or naturalisation
     papers. If your name has changed, keep the marriage certificate or court order with it. New
     Hampshire's cases turned on exactly that (log 102).
  2. **Check your registration with your state or county election office**, the official source. Rules
     and deadlines are your state's, and the episode never states them for "your state".
  3. **If you became a citizen after you got a Social Security number**, know that the record a federal
     check reads may not show it (log 110, 112). The official source for your own record is the Social
     Security Administration.
  4. **If you get a letter asking you to prove citizenship, answer it.** In Utah, about 1,800 did, 488 of
     them confirmed through state birth records (log 74, secondary). ⚠ Pre-vote detail
     (Script traps).
- **The pictures:** the registration form with the sworn box as the town's data surface, the box ticked
  "No" and the clerk's stamp; Utah's funnel as a sorting machine (2,069,640 → about 74,000 → 10,718 →
  5,007 → 27); a line of 1,100 people at the Kansas counter against one; a birth certificate with a
