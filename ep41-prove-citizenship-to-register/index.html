Should You Have to Prove You're a Citizen to Register to Vote?

When you register to vote, should the state take your word, check its records, or ask for paper?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

The verdictYour dialNumbers you heardWhat the fact-check caughtUp next

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

When you register, you swear you're a citizen. Two AI teams argue who should carry the proof: your word, the state's records, or your paper; you set the dial.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

In December the Supreme Court takes up whether a state may require documents from people who register on its own form. And on October 15 the Justice Department sued California over a list of about 20,000 registrants it calls likely noncitizens.

Settled

What both sides accept

Only citizens may vote, and lying about it is a crime. Noncitizens on the rolls are rare but real: Utah found 27 in about two million records, and 13 had voted at some point. The signed box alone isn't a citizenship check.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesProve it at the door

The citizenship box is a signature, not a check, and Utah's count shows it failed both ways. Prove it once at registration with a document, make the document free, and give everyone a federal-races ballot while they get it.

▶ Hear Green at 4:11 on YouTube
Gold arguesLet the state check

Asking everyone for paper has a measured price: in Kansas, about 1,100 applicants were held back for each noncitizen stopped, most of them citizens. Check the records the state already holds, and ask only the people it can't confirm.

▶ Hear Gold at 10:19 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

It isn't trust-the-voter against check-the-voter. The fight is over who carries the proof, the voter with a document or the state with its records, and what someone who can't be confirmed is allowed to vote on while they wait.

The verdict

What survives both teams
The sworn box stays under every rule. A database flag is a lead, never a verdict: someone contacts you and asks. And the agencies that feed the rolls get checked too.
What didn't hold up
Noncitizens are voting in large numbers: where states counted, the numbers found are hundreds to thousands of times smaller than the closest races. It never happens: Utah found twenty-seven. The databases can tell who isn't a citizen: Utah says they can't. California's twenty thousand are proven noncitizens: the list came with no error rate. And most people don't want this: a majority favours it in every registration poll we read.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: Who carries the proof when you register?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Your word
    What it buys
    Nobody has to find a document: you swear you're a citizen, the state checks who you are, and lying on the box is a crime that can mean up to five years in prison and deportation.
    What it costs
    The box isn't a citizenship check, and in Utah it failed both ways: 14 people ticked No and were registered anyway, and 34 ticked Yes. The penalty only deters if someone is prosecuted; Kansas claimed 129 cases and filed no criminal complaints.
    Not measured
    How many noncitizens it really lets through: every count found is a floor, only what each check could see.

    On the record: row 2 of the fact-check log, row 5 of the fact-check log, row 67 of the fact-check log, row 70 of the fact-check log, row 88 of the fact-check log

  2. 2The state's own records
    What it buys
    Utah confirmed more than 99% of its voter records from licence files and the federal database without asking anyone for anything, and when it wrote to the people it couldn't confirm, about 1,800 answered with proof, almost 500 of them through the state's own birth records.
    What it costs
    The records confirm citizens well but can't settle who isn't one, and their doubts land hardest on new citizens whose records lag: about one in five of the voters Texas flagged last year were citizens.
    Not measured
    How often the updated federal database is wrong: it has never published an error rate.

    On the record: row 65 of the fact-check log, row 66 of the fact-check log, row 74 of the fact-check log, row 71 of the fact-check log, row 110 of the fact-check log, row 116 of the fact-check log

  3. 3Your paper, from everyone
    What it buys
    A document is proof the voter holds, so nobody's citizenship rests on a database match, and in every registration poll the show found, most voters say they want it.
    What it costs
    It has a measured price that lands on citizens. In Kansas about 31,000 applicants were held back, about 1,100 for each noncitizen stopped, the clear majority of them citizens; in New Hampshire at least 140 were turned away, a rule that fell on women 15 to one, and free birth records reached 21 people in four months.
    Not measured
    Whether it would raise trust in election results: nobody has measured that.

    On the record: row 139 of the fact-check log, row 141 of the fact-check log, row 89 of the fact-check log, row 92 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 93 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 99 of the fact-check log, row 101 of the fact-check log, row 105 of the fact-check log

Under every setting: Someone still decides what a person who can't be confirmed may vote on while they wait: everything, federal races only, a ballot set aside, or nothing until they prove it.

What our fact-check caught

22 of the 177 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidKansas blocked about 460 applicants for every noncitizen it found.

    The record saysThat mixed two periods. Counting only the noncitizens who tried while the law ran: about 1,100 applicants held back for each one stopped.

  2. Draft saidUtah never said whether its 27 noncitizens had voted.

    The record saysIts report does: 13 of the 27 had voted at some point since 2018.

  3. Draft saidOregon's licence-office error put about 1,600 people on the rolls, and 10 of them voted.

    The record saysThe count kept growing: 1,952 registered without proving eligibility, and 44 voted. The state says none of those votes could have changed an outcome.

  4. Draft saidFederal officials narrowed a list of more than 175,000 down to 20,000.

    The record saysThe 175,000 appears only in the government's legal brief, not in any sworn filing, so we didn't air it.

  5. Draft saidA court found more than 99% of Kansas's blocked applicants were citizens.

    The record saysThe court found "the clear majority" were citizens. The 99% rests on one expert's estimate.
