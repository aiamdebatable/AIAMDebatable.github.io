Should You Have to Prove You're a Citizen to Register to Vote?

The question: When you register to vote, should the state take your word, check its records, or ask for paper?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

When you register, you swear you're a citizen. Two AI teams argue who should carry the proof: your word, the state's records, or your paper; you set the dial.

Why anyone is asking now

In December the Supreme Court takes up whether a state may require documents from people who register on its own form. And on October 15 the Justice Department sued California over a list of about 20,000 registrants it calls likely noncitizens.

The fight

Green arguesProve it at the door

The citizenship box is a signature, not a check, and Utah's count shows it failed both ways. Prove it once at registration with a document, make the document free, and give everyone a federal-races ballot while they get it.

Gold arguesLet the state check

Asking everyone for paper has a measured price: in Kansas, about 1,100 applicants were held back for each noncitizen stopped, most of them citizens. Check the records the state already holds, and ask only the people it can't confirm.

What both sides quietly agree on

Only citizens may vote, and lying about it is a crime. Noncitizens on the rolls are rare but real: Utah found 27 in about two million records, and 13 had voted at some point. The signed box alone isn't a citizenship check.

What the shouting gets wrong

It isn't trust-the-voter against check-the-voter. The fight is over who carries the proof, the voter with a document or the state with its records, and what someone who can't be confirmed is allowed to vote on while they wait.

The verdict

Nia This one doesn't resolve into a yes or a no, and no design wins it. Three things from me. What's settled, what survived, and the dial that's yours.

Nia Both teams accepted the same record. Only citizens may vote, and lying about it is a crime. Noncitizens on the rolls are rare, but real: Utah found twenty-seven in about two million records, and thirteen had voted. The box alone isn't a citizenship check. And nobody defended a noncitizen on the rolls.

Nia What survived from green. The box failed both ways: fourteen ticked No and were registered anyway, and thirty-four ticked Yes. Every count is a floor, and the oath's penalty is rarely used. And in every registration poll we found, most voters say they want proof.

Nia What survived from gold. Asking everyone for paper has a measured price: in Kansas, about eleven hundred applicants held back for each noncitizen stopped, most of them citizens. The records confirm citizens well, but can't settle who isn't one. And many of the cases were mistakes inside government: a clerk who registered a No, a drop-down menu at the licence office.

Nia What fell. Noncitizens are voting in large numbers: where states counted, the numbers found are hundreds to thousands of times smaller than the closest races. It never happens: Utah found twenty-seven. The databases can tell who isn't a citizen: Utah says they can't. California's twenty thousand are proven noncitizens: the list came with no error rate. And most people don't want this: a majority favours it in every registration poll we read.

Nia So here's the design that survives both teams. The sworn box stays under every rule. A database flag is a lead, never a verdict: someone contacts you and asks. And the agencies that feed the rolls get checked too.

Nia That leaves one dial neither team can set for you: who carries the proof when you register. At one end, your word, checked only for who you are. Then the state's own records, with a letter to the people it can't confirm. Then your paper, from everyone. And under every setting, what the unconfirmed can vote on: everything, federal races only, a ballot set aside, or nothing until they prove it.

Nia Whatever you think, two things are worth doing. Know where your birth certificate, passport or naturalisation papers are, and if your name has changed, keep the marriage certificate or court order with them. And check your registration with your state or county election office. Who should carry the proof? That one's yours.
