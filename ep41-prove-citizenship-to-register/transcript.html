The verdict

NiaThis one doesn't resolve into a yes or a no, and no design wins it. Three things from me. What's settled, what survived, and the dial that's yours.

NiaBoth teams accepted the same record. Only citizens may vote, and lying about it is a crime. Noncitizens on the rolls are rare, but real: Utah found twenty-seven in about two million records, and thirteen had voted. The box alone isn't a citizenship check. And nobody defended a noncitizen on the rolls.

NiaWhat survived from green. The box failed both ways: fourteen ticked No and were registered anyway, and thirty-four ticked Yes. Every count is a floor, and the oath's penalty is rarely used. And in every registration poll we found, most voters say they want proof.

NiaWhat survived from gold. Asking everyone for paper has a measured price: in Kansas, about eleven hundred applicants held back for each noncitizen stopped, most of them citizens. The records confirm citizens well, but can't settle who isn't one. And many of the cases were mistakes inside government: a clerk who registered a No, a drop-down menu at the licence office.

NiaWhat fell. Noncitizens are voting in large numbers: where states counted, the numbers found are hundreds to thousands of times smaller than the closest races. It never happens: Utah found twenty-seven. The databases can tell who isn't a citizen: Utah says they can't. California's twenty thousand are proven noncitizens: the list came with no error rate. And most people don't want this: a majority favours it in every registration poll we read.

NiaSo here's the design that survives both teams. The sworn box stays under every rule. A database flag is a lead, never a verdict: someone contacts you and asks. And the agencies that feed the rolls get checked too.

NiaThat leaves one dial neither team can set for you: who carries the proof when you register. At one end, your word, checked only for who you are. Then the state's own records, with a letter to the people it can't confirm. Then your paper, from everyone. And under every setting, what the unconfirmed can vote on: everything, federal races only, a ballot set aside, or nothing until they prove it.

NiaWhatever you think, two things are worth doing. Know where your birth certificate, passport or naturalisation papers are, and if your name has changed, keep the marriage certificate or court order with them. And check your registration with your state or county election office. Who should carry the proof? That one's yours.
