## The research · What you did when you registered, and the three kinds of state
**Andre:** Start with your form. You wrote your name and your date of birth, and probably a driver's licence number or the last four digits of your Social Security number. Then you ticked a box: I am a United States citizen. And you signed it, under penalty of perjury.
**Kate:** And then somebody checked?
**Andre:** Somebody checked that you're you. On the federal form, and in states like California, your number is matched to make sure you exist. Whether you're a citizen rests on that signed box. The Justice Department's own brief, in its case against California, puts it this way: that check establishes identity, not citizenship.
**Kate:** So lying on the box is just… allowed?
**Andre:** It's a crime. Falsely claiming citizenship to register can mean up to five years in prison. A noncitizen voting in a federal election, up to one. And it makes that person deportable. The form warns you about all three.
**Kate:** Can a state just ask for my birth certificate instead?
**Andre:** On the federal form, no. In twenty thirteen, the Supreme Court ruled seven to two that a state can't demand more than the federal form asks. On its own state form, it can ask for more, and since August twenty twenty-four, Arizona may reject a state-form application that comes without proof. In December, the Supreme Court takes up exactly that: whether a state may require documents from people who register on its own form.
**Kate:** So what are the rules right now?
**Andre:** Three kinds of state. Some take your word: the federal form, and California. Some check their own records first, and ask only the people they can't confirm. That's Utah's new law: if you can't be confirmed, you get a ballot with federal races only. And some have asked everyone for paper. Kansas, from twenty thirteen to twenty sixteen. New Hampshire, from twenty twenty-four, until a federal court ruled in May that it had to bring back its sworn affidavit.
**Kate:** And what do people actually want?
**Andre:** Most say paper. Every registration poll we found shows a majority in favour of proof of citizenship. Gallup asked about first-time registrants in twenty twenty-four: eighty-three to fifteen, including two in three Democrats.
**Kate:** Every poll?
**Andre:** Every one we found, but the wording moves it. In one CBS poll this March, a passport or birth certificate requirement got sixty-six to thirty-four. Asked about the bill in Congress by its name, the same people split twenty-eight to thirty-one, with forty-one percent not sure.
**Andre:** So in most places your word is the proof, a few states check their records, and the paper rule has been tried and pulled back. Hold that.

## The research · The records check, and its catch: the lag, Utah's letters, the California list
**Andre:** Not supposed to be. When the Supreme Court let the federal database run in September, it described the process: if the database can't confirm you, the agency has to contact you and ask for proof. That was an order letting it run for now, not a ruling on the merits.
**Kate:** How often is the computer wrong?
**Andre:** About the people it confirms, it does well. About the people it doesn't, it's often wrong. Utah says it plainly: its records are reliable in confirming that someone is a citizen, and not reliable in indicating that someone isn't, without considering other factors.
**Kate:** Why would a citizen look like a noncitizen?
**Andre:** Because records lag. If you got your Social Security number before you became a citizen, and never told them, the record may still say non-citizen. A government audit in two thousand six found that about seven in a hundred records marked non-citizen belonged to people who had since become citizens. It has never been re-measured. One of the people who brought the Supreme Court case this year is exactly that: a naturalised citizen whose record still doesn't show it.
**Kate:** And when a state runs a whole list?
**Andre:** The lists shrink when someone checks. Texas, last year: of about twenty-seven hundred voters flagged through the federal database, nearly six hundred turned out to be citizens, about one in five, mostly because their passport data hadn't reached the system yet. And the updated federal database has never published an error rate.
**Kate:** So what does Utah do with the people it can't confirm?
**Andre:** It writes to them, and until they prove it, they get a ballot with federal races only. Of the people Utah wrote to, about eighteen hundred proved citizenship, almost five hundred of them through the state's own birth records. That's from the Lieutenant Governor's office, as reported by Utah News Dispatch.
**Kate:** And California? I keep hearing twenty thousand.
**Andre:** On October fifteenth, the Justice Department sued California. Its list names just over twenty thousand registrants it calls likely noncitizens, and it asked a court to set their mail ballots aside until they were verified. The list was built outside the federal database: a copy of California's rolls from August twenty twenty-five, matched against commercial data and immigration files, then reviewed by hand.
**Kate:** And is it right?
**Andre:** Nobody has said how often. The sworn filings give no error rate. The only accuracy note is a footnote: fewer than two hundred records with minor data discrepancies. In July, the federal government had said there may be as many as a hundred and ninety thousand.
**Kate:** And the people who were charged?
**Andre:** The same day it sued, the department charged twenty-eight green-card holders who are on the list. They're charged, not convicted, and in the Los Angeles federal court the charges are mainly about naturalisation forms. Those twenty-eight tell you nothing about how accurate the rest of the list is. California registers on a sworn statement, and its Secretary of State refused to set those ballots aside.
**Andre:** So a records check confirms citizens well, and its doubts land hardest on new citizens. Hold that.
