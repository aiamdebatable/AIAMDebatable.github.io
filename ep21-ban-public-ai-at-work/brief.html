Episode 21 · The research

ep21 — Should your company ban public AI at work?

The full research behind the episode. Episode grade: a peg hunt (2026-09-24), a 41-agent deep-research-v3 fan-out (run wf_88bd2279-b2e: 116 claims, 12 verified with the primary opened, 12 held, 0 flagged, 18 gaps), and a four-way hand pass that opened the primaries the fan-out could not: the court dockets, the bill text, the original Korean report, every vendor figure's own publication, and the labs' own terms pages. Provenance per claim is in the sources list and the fact-check log.

The question. When employees can type company information into a free AI tool on any browser or phone, should the company block those tools outright — or is the thing to control the data rather than the tool?

Disclosure, on air. This show is written and voiced with Anthropic's Claude (this episode's research ran on Claude Opus 5.5 with Claude Sonnet workers). Anthropic's consumer product is one of the tools this episode examines, and three of the record's facts cut against it: its consumer training switch was shown already ON in its own pop-up (with five-year retention), Claude has the second-highest personal-account share in Cyberhaven's data (58.2 %), and Claude shared chats were indexed by Google twice. They air at the same weight as the OpenAI material.

⛔ Read first — what the record does not support

  • No case has been documented of confidential data an employee typed into a public AI tool later appearing in another user's output. Not Samsung (the original report calls it misuse — data entered and unretrievable; Samsung never confirmed it), not Amazon (a lawyer's warning that output "closely matches existing material" — a suspicion, not a finding). The one cross-user exposure on record, ChatGPT's March 2023 bug, exposed chat titles and payment details through a caching library, not prompt content. Say "no case has been documented", never "it has never happened".
  • Nobody has measured whether a workplace ban reduces exposure or relocates it. The fan-out and a second dedicated hunt found no before/after study at any organisation. Netskope's 78 % → 47 % fall in personal-account use coincides with enterprise rollouts and has no ban-only comparison. The only before/after at all is Italy's 2023 regulator block — a national order, not an employer. The title premise "a ban moves the leak to phones" is a plausible inference, not a finding; say so.
  • Every telemetry figure is blind to personal phones by construction. Cyberhaven, Netskope, LayerX/Akamai, Harmonic and Verizon's DBIR all see managed browsers or gateways on company devices. Only surveys reach phones, and only as self-report.
  • Trinidad v. OpenAI does not establish that a company loses its trade secrets when an employee pastes them. It was a self-represented plaintiff's own ideas, dismissed with prejudice at the pleading stage; her appeal was dismissed on procedure. No employer, no employee, no evidence taken.

Why now

  • Hot peg (description). On 2026-08-31 the US Department of War added ChatGPT Mil to GenAI.mil, its internal AI platform accredited for Controlled Unclassified Information at Impact Level 5 and "built for scale to support more than 3 million Department personnel" (war.gov release). On 2026-09-23 its chief digital and AI officer said "over 2 million people in a week across all three models" had logged in (Defense One). The Department answered the ban question by bringing the tools inside the wall. The no-training assurance is OpenAI's, not the Department's (OpenAI).
  • Structural peg (script). Samsung — whose 2023 chip-code incidents made it the best-known corporate ban — reopened in two steps in June 2026: its DX division adopted ChatGPT, Gemini Enterprise and Claude from 12 June, access granted only after security training (News1); then ChatGPT Enterprise and Codex for all employees in Korea and all DX staff worldwide "within the company's security policies and governance framework" (announced ~22 June; OpenAI).
  • Watch item (re-check before recording). California SB 574 would bar attorneys from entering confidential information into a generative AI system "for which access … is not restricted to the attorney and persons authorized by the attorney." Presented to the governor 2026-09-09; under Art. IV §10(b)(2) he must act by 2026-09-30 (leginfo). If signed, it is the first statute to draw exactly this episode's line, for one profession.
  • Re-run 2026-09-28 (before scripting): the peg holds. Nothing newer or stronger found for 09-10 → 09-28. Defense One (09-23) re-fetched: the 2-million line stands; war.gov now returns 403 to fetching, the text saved on 09-24 stands. SB 574 is still "Enrolled and presented to the Governor" on leginfo and appears in neither the governor's 09-20 nor 09-27 signed/vetoed lists (09-27 update); re-check after 09-30. ⚠ Found in passing and not yet in this brief (June, so not a peg, but it bears on GREEN's "the law treats consumer terms as disclosure"): two state courts held a litigant's consumer-chatbot prompts protected as work product — Tate Group Automotive v. Legacy Automotive Capital (Texas Business Court, 2026-06-03) and Assini v. Hayward (N.Y. Sup. Ct., Nassau, 2026-06-04), per Spencer Fane (secondary; the orders themselves not opened).
  • Fresh figure. Zapier's survey (n = 1,005 US workers at 500+-person firms that already provide paid AI, fielded May 2026): 26 % whose employer restricts or blocks AI tools used a personal device or account anyway (Zapier).

Settled (not the debate)

  1. Employees put sensitive data into AI tools, routinely. Cyberhaven (222 companies, 2025 telemetry): 39.7 % of AI interactions involve data the customer's own rules mark sensitive — "once every three days" per employee. Its same-measure series: 10.7 % → 27.4 % → 34.8 % of corporate data going into AI was sensitive (2023 → 2025). Harmonic: 4.37 % of prompts and ~22 % of uploaded files (Q2 2025). Vendor telemetry, each with its own definition of "sensitive" — the direction is settled, the magnitudes are the vendors'.
  2. A large share of workplace AI use runs on personal accounts — and the trend is down where companies supply a tool. Netskope: 78 % → 47 % of genAI users on personal apps, managed accounts 25 % → 62 % (Oct 2024 → Oct 2025). LayerX/Akamai: 47.11 % of conversations under personal identities. ⛔ The two 47 %s are different reports and different denominators (users vs conversations) — never merge them.
  3. The business/consumer line is real for training. OpenAI: "By default, we don't use inputs or outputs from ChatGPT Business, ChatGPT Enterprise, ChatGPT Edu, or our API"; consumer ChatGPT may train unless "Improve the model for everyone" is off. Anthropic (from Aug 2025): trains on Free/Pro/Max "when this setting is on", with five-year retention; Claude for Work, Government, Education and the API excluded. ⚠ It is not a line for courts: the NYT v. OpenAI preservation order (13 May → 26 Sept 2025) covered ChatGPT Team, a paid business tier.
  4. The documented harms are losses of control, not regurgitation: retention under someone else's policy, court preservation and production (20 million de-identified consumer logs ordered produced, affirmed 2026-01-05), stolen consumer logins (Group-IB: 101,134 infected devices with saved ChatGPT credentials, 2023; IBM: over 300,000 ChatGPT credentials exposed in 2025; no Claude figure disclosed), and share-links indexed by search engines (ChatGPT's opt-in checkbox, July 2025; Grok, 370,000+ conversations; Claude, just under 600 in Sept 2025 and again in July 2026).
  5. The 2023 banners moved to sanctioned tools. Samsung (June 2026), JPMorgan (LLM Suite, 200,000 users in eight months — its restriction was reported as routine third-party-software compliance, "not because of a particular issue"), Goldman (GS AI Assistant firmwide), Morgan Stanley (its assistant over ~100,000 research documents). None kept total prohibition.
  6. No regulator checked recommends a blanket ban on staff use. Each regulates data classes and accounts: the EDPB taskforce ("it should be assumed that individuals will sooner or later input personal data"); CNIL (consumer tools for non-confidential work, with work accounts, reuse switched off and a list of allowed and forbidden uses); the UK AI Playbook ("Never enter any official information directly into public AI applications … unless it's already publicly available"); Australia's DTA (public tools allowed for OFFICIAL information, never OFFICIAL: Sensitive or above); Singapore's MAS (an inventory and proportionate controls); OMB M-25-21 ("forward-leaning and pro-innovation"). The one outright ban is vendor-specific: Australia's DeepSeek removal (Feb 2025).
  7. Sanctioned tools have their own documented failures — each patched, none shown exploited: EchoLeak in M365 Copilot (zero-click, Microsoft rated 9.3, NIST 7.5; "exploited: No"), Slack AI's private-channel extraction (patched 2024-08-20), Salesforce Agentforce "ForcedLeak" (CVSS 9.4), and a Claude data-exfiltration report Anthropic first closed as out of scope and then said "should not have been closed". Sensitive Information Disclosure is #2 on OWASP's 2025 LLM Top 10 (it was #6 in 2023).

The contested core (both steelmanned)

⚠ Topic-shape verdict: as literally pitched ("ban AI or allow it?") the question is lopsided — nobody in the record keeps a bare ban with no alternative, and no regulator recommends one. The genuine fight, with evidence on both sides, is what exactly gets banned: the consumer TOOLS, or the sensitive DATA? That is the debate below. (Proposed to Lucas at the checkpoint; the title may want to follow.)

GREEN — "Block the consumer tools. Give people a company one."

  • The law treats consumer terms as disclosure. Heppner (S.D.N.Y., 2026-02-17): a defendant's Claude chats "were not protected … by either the attorney-client privilege or the work product doctrine", reasoned in part from Anthropic's consumer privacy policy. Trinidad: "Because Trinidad consented to disclosure … by accepting OpenAI's Terms, the Court cannot conclude that those protocols and frameworks are trade secrets." Thin and district-level — but the direction is one-way so far for consumer terms, and a company cannot un-type what an employee typed.
  • Employees cannot tell what is sensitive. If 39.7 % of interactions carry data the company's own rules call sensitive, "use judgement" is a control that fails a third of the time. Samsung's engineers — at a company that trains harder than most — produced three incidents in under 20 days of permission.
  • Consumer tiers change underneath you. Anthropic moved its consumer default to training with five-year retention in 2025, and its own pop-up showed the switch already on. A court held consumer ChatGPT logs for four months. The company is not party to any of it.
  • The data rule is unenforceable without the tool rule. A proxy or DLP can block a domain; it cannot read an employee's judgement. Every regulator's "don't enter sensitive data" depends on the employee getting it right each time.
  • Every serious banner that reopened did it through a contract, not the public tool — Samsung behind training and a governance framework, the Pentagon at IL5.

GOLD — "Block the data, not the tools."

  • Bans correlate with more leakage, not less. KPMG / University of Melbourne (48,340 people, 47 countries): uploading sensitive company information into public AI tools is "most common of employees who report their organization has banned generative AI (67%)", vs 33 % with no policy. ⚠ Correlation, self-reported, and workplaces with a mere policy also score 56 % — the report itself says it "suggests outright bans may be ineffective".
  • People route around restrictions even when a sanctioned tool exists. Zapier's whole sample works at firms that already provide paid AI, and 26 % of those facing a restriction used a personal device or account anyway. Italy's regulator block: Italian developers' output fell ~50 % for two business days, then recovered, with searches for circumvention tools up.
  • A blocked tool is invisible, a permitted one is loggable. A company that permits and logs consumer use through work accounts (CNIL's model) sees what is typed; a company that blocks it pushes the same paste to a phone no telemetry can see.
  • The regulators, the bar and the statute all draw the data line. ABA Formal Opinion 512 requires informed consent before confidential information goes into a self-learning tool — not abstention. SB 574 conditions use on access restriction. The UK, Australia and CNIL permit public tools for public or low-classification work.
  • Sanctioned tools are not a safe harbour. EchoLeak, Slack AI, ForcedLeak; the NYT order reached ChatGPT Team. "Bring it inside" swaps one vendor risk for another and, on the Pentagon critic's argument, "convenience is exactly what can grind down security discipline over time" (Cody, FPIF, 2026-09-07).

The reframe — what the shouting gets wrong

The shouting assumes the danger is "the AI will tell someone else your secret." No such case is documented. What is documented is a loss of control: the data sits under another company's retention rules, a court can order it kept, a stolen login can open it, a share-link can index it, and a lawyer can later argue you disclosed it. So "ban or allow" is the wrong axis. Every actor on the record — Samsung, the banks, the Pentagon, California's legislature, Europe's data regulators — ended up drawing the same line: a controlled, logged, no-training channel on one side and consumer accounts on the other. The disagreement is about how far down the sensitivity ladder the consumer side gets closed off, and whether you close it by blocking the tool or by rules on the data.

Both quietly agree

Confidential data should not go into consumer accounts the company does not control — neither side defends that. And people denied a usable tool will find one — which is why every 2023 banner later supplied one. The live fight is how much to trust the employee's judgement on what counts as sensitive, and how much to trust the sanctioned tool.

Verdict framing (no winner — hand it to the viewer)

The dial: how far down the sensitivity ladder does "no consumer AI" reach? Three live settings, each with a real institution behind it and a documented failure mode: (1) block the consumer tools entirely and supply a company one (Samsung 2023→2026, the Pentagon, the DNC) — fails when people use their phones, and nobody has measured how often; (2) permit consumer tools for public or low-classification work only (Australia's OFFICIAL line, CNIL, the UK Playbook) — fails when employees misjudge sensitivity, which the telemetry says is often; (3) data rules plus consent, no tool rule (ABA 512 for lawyers) — fails when the consumer terms change under you. You're the jury.

Script traps

  • "Entered", never "leaked" for Samsung. Spoken: "engineers pasted chip code and a meeting transcript into ChatGPT, and Samsung couldn't get it back." "Reportedly" — one outlet's report; Samsung declined to confirm. "Under 20 days" (the report's own words), and it was the chip division (DS) that had permission.
  • The May 2023 restriction: Bloomberg saw a memo to "one of its biggest divisions"; the device scope is TechCrunch's paraphrase. Attribute it.
  • The Samsung 2026 reopening is two steps and three vendors (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude on 06-12; then the OpenAI deal ~06-22). The OpenAI page carries no date — say "June". Do not claim the DS division is named.
  • GenAI.mil: >2 million people in one week across all three models (Gemini, Grok, ChatGPT Mil) — not ChatGPT Mil users. GenAI.mil launched Dec 2025; ChatGPT Mil was added 2026-08-31. "Not used for training" is OpenAI's assurance.
  • Trinidad: "a federal judge, in an early ruling on a self-represented plaintiff's case about her own ideas". Never "the courts have ruled companies lose trade secrets". Quote only the order's words.
  • Heppner vs Warner is not a clean split: different facts (criminal defendant acting without counsel's direction vs a civil party's own trial preparation). "Two courts, one week apart, different results on different facts." Heppner involved Claude — say so.
  • FINRA: Regulatory Notice 24-09 does not mention recordkeeping. The SEC's off-channel sweep (100+ firms, $2bn+ since Dec 2021) was about texting apps. Any link to AI chats is an inference, not a regulator's position.
  • Akamai's 14.4 % is a share of conversations under a corporate identity (~7.6 % of all), not an overall rate. The report's own prose mis-states it; use the figure caption's definition or skip it.
  • Cyberhaven's 34.8 % / 10.7 % is from its 2025 report, not 2026.
  • Harmonic: 4.37 % / ~22 % is Q2 2025; "22 million prompts" is a different, full-year dataset (≈2.6 % exposures). Never mix them.
  • Zapier's "50 % ignored restrictions": say "26 % used a personal device or account anyway" — the 50 % may double-count.
  • Verizon DBIR has no "67 %" — the real figure: of the ~15 % routine GenAI users on corporate devices, 72 % used non-corporate email identifiers.
  • Gartner's 69 % is security leaders who suspect or have evidence — suspicion, not measured behaviour. Release dated 2025-11-19.
  • The "2 % say AI is prohibited" is a content site's opt-in survey — APPROXIMATE, off air.
  • Italy: a national regulator (Garante), not a company; "about half, for two business days", a preprint. The €15 M fine on OpenAI was annulled on appeal (Court of Rome, March 2026) — never present it as standing.
  • Productivity: the Copilot RCT (+26 %, 4,867 developers; Microsoft-affiliated authors, ±10-point SE) and METR (−19 %, 16 developers) measure using AI, not the cost of a ban. Present as "uneven", never as the price of a ban.
  • Fairness: if ChatGPT's share-link episode or credential numbers air, Claude's two indexing episodes and "no Claude figure disclosed" air with them. Anthropic's consumer default: "asked everyone to decide, with the switch shown on" — not "opt-in".

Open questions carried to the script

  • Has any organisation measured personal-account or phone use before and after a ban? (None found in two passes — the script says so.)
  • Security outcomes for Samsung 2026 or GenAI.mil: none reported — "adoption confirmed, outcome unmeasured".
  • SB 574: the governor's action by 2026-09-30.
  • German works-council co-determination (BetrVG §87) as a check on unilateral bans — not researched; don't imply every employer decides alone.
  • The HHS proposed Security Rule (Jan 2025) — finalisation not checked; healthcare has no staff-chatbot guidance on record.
  • MAS: consultation checked, final guidelines not.
