ChatGPT at Work: Should Your Company Ban Public AI?

Does banning public AI tools at work protect company secrets, or just move the leak to personal phones the company cannot see?

Every famous ban ended in a company tool. The fight is what, exactly, to close off: the app, or the data.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in September 2026, the Pentagon had put ChatGPT inside its own network, and its chief digital and AI officer said over two million people used that platform in a single week. Samsung, whose 2023 ban everyone cites, had reopened in June through security training and then a contract.

Settled

What both sides accept

Employees routinely put sensitive data into AI tools, and the famous bans ended in company tools. No regulator we checked recommends a blanket ban, nobody has documented a secret coming back out in another user's answer, and nobody has measured whether a ban reduces exposure or moves it. The danger the record supports is losing control of your data.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesBan the data, not the tool

Nobody has documented a secret coming back out of a chatbot. The documented risk is data sitting under the wrong terms. So decide what never goes in without a contract, give people work accounts with training off, and keep public tools for public work, because monitoring only sees company laptops and a ban just hides the paste.

▶ Hear Green at 4:00 on YouTube
Gold arguesBlock the public tools

What's typed into a personal account lives under someone else's terms, and those terms change: Anthropic changed its own. A data rule needs every employee to judge sensitivity right, every time. So block the public tools for anything that matters and supply a company tool, which is where Samsung, the banks and the Pentagon all ended up.

▶ Hear Gold at 9:26 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

Everyone on the record drew the same line in the end: a controlled channel on one side, consumer accounts on the other. The open question is how far down the sensitivity ladder no consumer AI reaches. You can block consumer tools and supply your own, which fails when people use their phones; allow them for public work only, which fails when people misjudge; or rely on data rules and consent, which fails when the terms change.

The verdict

What the record supports
The danger isn't the AI repeating your secret. It's losing control of it. And everyone on this record drew the same line in the end: a controlled channel on one side, consumer accounts on the other.
What didn't hold up
Green's comfort: her rule leans on the same judgement the telemetry says lets sensitive data through. Gold's fear: he can't show a ban that reduced anything, and the company tool he trusts has had holes.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: Where on the sensitivity ladder does your company close the door to consumer AI?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Block the consumer tools entirely and supply your own
    What it buys
    Like Samsung and the Pentagon. What's typed stays under terms your company signed, and every serious banner went back through a contract, not the public app: Samsung reopened in June 2026 with three tools after security training, then a deal with OpenAI, and the Pentagon put ChatGPT inside its own network. It doesn't rest on every employee judging right, every time.
    What it costs
    It fails when people use their phones. Every measurement of what's typed comes from company devices, so a blocked app goes dark rather than away. Where employers banned AI, more workers reported uploading sensitive data, not fewer: about 67% against 33% where there was no policy, though that's a correlation. And the company tools have documented holes: Microsoft rated a Copilot flaw 9.3 and Salesforce had one rated 9.4, all patched and none shown exploited, and a court order even reached a paid business tier.
    Not measured
    Whether a ban reduces exposure or moves it: nobody has counted phone or personal-account use before and after one.

    On the record: “Samsung 2026: ChatGPT Enterprise to all Korea staff…” in the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), “GenAI.mil: ChatGPT Mil, IL5/CUI, >3 M personnel, data…” in the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), “KPMG 57 % hide use; ~half breach policy” in the fact-check log, “A second sanctioned-tool exposure besides EchoLeak” in the fact-check log, “Any before/after measurement of a workplace ban” in the fact-check log

  2. 2Allow them for public work only
    What it buys
    Like Australia and the UK. It's the regulators' line: none we checked recommends a blanket ban. The UK says never put official information into public AI unless it's already public; Australia allows public tools for its lowest classification, never above it. The work stays on the laptop, where it can be seen, on work accounts with the training switch off.
    What it costs
    It fails when people misjudge. A data rule needs every employee to judge right, every time, and the monitoring says a lot of sensitive data goes in anyway: about four in ten AI interactions involved data the company's own rules call sensitive.

    On the record: “EDPB, CNIL, UK, MAS, HHS, Australia — stances” in the fact-check log, “Cyberhaven 39.7 % of AI interactions involve sensitive…” in the fact-check log, “OpenAI consumer trains by default; business tiers don't” in the fact-check log, “Device vs account” in the fact-check log

  3. 3Data rules and consent with no tool rule
    What it buys
    Like the bar association, which says get the client's consent first. Not abstain. Consent. No tool is blocked, so nothing is pushed out of view.
    What it costs
    It fails when the terms change under you, and Anthropic changed its own last year: it started training on personal Claude accounts and keeping that data for up to five years, and its pop-up showed the switch already on. A consumer privacy policy has already been read against a user in court, though other judges have ruled differently on different facts. And it still needs every employee to judge right, every time.

    On the record: “ABA Formal Opinion 512 requires informed consent” in the fact-check log, “Anthropic consumer training is opt-in with 5-year retention” in the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), “Heppner held AI chats not privileged” in the fact-check log, “Warner v. Gilbarco held "the opposite"” in the fact-check log (our draft, corrected)

Wherever the line sits: Nobody has measured which one leaks less. The danger isn't the AI repeating your secret; it's losing control of it.

What our fact-check caught

17 of the 51 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidAnthropic's training on personal Claude accounts is opt-in.

    The record saysAnthropic trains on personal accounts when the setting is on, and its own pop-up showed the switch already on. Data can be kept up to five years.

  2. Draft saidSamsung banned ChatGPT company-wide in 2023.

    The record saysBloomberg saw a memo restricting public AI tools across one of its biggest divisions.

  3. Draft saidIn June 2026 Samsung gave ChatGPT Enterprise to all its Korea staff in one move.

    The record saysIt came in two steps: first three tools, ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, after security training; then a deal with OpenAI.

  4. Draft saidA second court ruled the opposite of the New York judge on AI chats.

    The record saysA judge in a civil case, on different facts, called ChatGPT a tool, not a person, and protected the material. That's not a clean split.
