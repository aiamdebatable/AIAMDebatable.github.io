## The pitch, in one line

**The extra help on Marketplace premiums ended with 2025. Since then the average net premium is up 58%
(up 30%, about $22 a month, for people who still get a credit), and paid enrollment in February was 12%
lower, about one in eight, a drop that also includes CMS ending 1.5 million ineligible subsidies (log 61, 71, 193).**
What the record does *not* settle is whether bringing the help back is worth what CBO projects (about $35
billion a year over ten years for a permanent version, assuming 2025 enactment; about $13–32 billion a year for
the three-year bill, log 133, 141, 145) when only about half to two-thirds of new enrollees are newly insured
(log 142, 151), and how much of the old enrollment was fraud.
Between those sits the real choice: **where the help should stop, and whether anyone should pay $0.**

**Thesis, one line:** the lapse measurably raised bills and cut paid coverage; the fight over bringing it
back is about value and targeting, with fraud's size genuinely unmeasured, so the decision is a design:
the cliff or a phase-out, and a $0 plan or a floor.

**Each rung, in words.**
- **$20,000: the low earner who lost a $0 plan.** Say the state type every time: a single adult earning
  $20,000 **in one of the 10 states that did not expand Medicaid**, such as Texas or Florida (log 249). Under
  the 2021–25 rules they paid **nothing** for the benchmark plan (log 15); since 2026 they pay 2.10% of income,
  about $420 a year, and 2.15% (about $430) for 2027 (log 246, 247). In the 41 expansion jurisdictions the same
  person gets **Medicaid** instead, and no credit (log 250, 251); about 89% of 2026 sign-ups at 100–138% of poverty
  were in those 10 states, 4.7 million of them in Florida and Texas alone (log 252, our sums from CMS's state
  file). ⛔ Never a couple: at $20,000 two people are below poverty and get no credit anywhere (log 248). This is
  also exactly where the fraud fight lives. HHS's own analysts found that about **half** of people moved
  automatically from a $0 plan into one with a premium in 2024–26 never paid and lost coverage, against about
  18% historically. They read that as people unaware they were enrolled, people who may not exist, or people
  who didn't value the plan, and they can't say which (log 195). **Dial two lives on this rung:** $0, a small
  minimum (HHS's $5 rule, vacated, log 38), or a share of income (the law now, log 26).
- **$45,000: the biggest group, the middle of the ladder.** The bill roughly doubles against the 2021–25
  schedule (about $4,350 against $2,375 a year, our arithmetic, log 308). The measured average for people who
  still get a credit: from $74 to $96 a month, up 30% (log 61). Many here kept the monthly bill down by trading
  to bronze: bronze went from 30% to 40% of plan choices, and the average deductible rose 37%, most of it from
  that trade-down (log 66, 67). About half of adults who buy Marketplace coverage are self-employed or work for
  a small business (log 173), and their surprise comes at tax time: from tax year 2026, if income comes in
  higher than estimated, they repay **all** of the excess advance credit (log 20, 21); Treasury expects about
  3.7 million returns with a repayment in 2028 (log 10).
- **$64,000: the cliff is one rung, not the opening.** For 2027 the help stops at $63,840 for one (log 30).
  This rung is $160 over it and gets nothing under the law now; under the 2021–25 schedule they would pay at most
  $5,440 (log 308). A real price on this rung: a 40-year-old in Indianapolis earning $65,000 paid $316 a month for
  one silver plan in 2025, $477 in 2026, and $546 in 2027 if the filed rates are approved (log 272). For a
  60-year-old just over the line in 2026, the yearly increase ran from about $4,500 in New York to about $22,500
  in Wyoming (log 174). Sign-ups just above the line fell 44% (log 84). (For a couple the line is $86,560; KFF's
  West Virginia couple, over $50,000 a year, stays in the dial table only, log 176.)
- **$86,000 and $130,000: no help under the law now, and little under any setting.** Their bill is the sticker
  price. Under the 2021–25 schedule they get help only where the benchmark costs more than 8.5% of income
  (about $7,310 or $11,050 a year, log 308): the Indianapolis plan at $546 a month (about $6,550 a year, our
  arithmetic, log 272) would get the $86,000 earner nothing even then. A cap near 700% would cover the $86,000
  rung's eligibility, not the $130,000 one (log 308); no cap design is scored (log 51). What does touch them is
  the **sticker price**: insurers estimate the lapse added about 4 points to 2026 rates (log 122); CBO projects a
  bill passed now leaves 2026 unchanged and makes 2027 sticker premiums about 5.7% lower (log 144, 269); and
  off-exchange buyers share the same pool (log 270). The HSA bill would put $1,000 or $1,500 a year into an HSA
  for bronze buyers up to 700% of poverty, for 2026–27 only (log 287); never scored independently (log 290).

### The taxpayer next door: a rung of their own (pitch Ruling 5)

The question, in the words viewers use: *"How much of my money pays for these premiums? My own insurance is
expensive enough. Why do I pay for other people?"* The neighbour has **job-based insurance**, like about 64% of
working-age adults (log 267); Marketplace coverage is about 4% of people by Census's survey (log 265, 266).

- **Who pays: you do, through your taxes.** Say it plainly. The credit is federal money, so it comes from
  taxpayers; and because an extension is **borrowed unless Congress offsets it**, it is also paid through future
  taxes and interest on the debt (log 141, 255, 257, 263). How it works, said once and never as a reassurance:
  the Treasury pays the insurer each month under a standing law, so it comes out of your taxes, **not out of
  your premium** (log 254, 256).
- **Your share, on average.** A permanent extension: about **$255 a year per household** (about $215 per tax
  return); the three-year bill: about **$180 a year per household** (about $155 per return) in fiscal 2026–28
  (log 260, 261). Each is CBO's cost divided by the 137.1 million US households or the 162.75 million income-tax
  returns (log 258, 259): **our arithmetic, a flat average**. Income tax is not paid equally, so a given
  household's share is not this number, and the record has no source for the distribution. Beside it, on the
  same yardstick: the tax break for **job-based** insurance is about **$1,750 a year per household** (log 262),
  income tax only, so a floor (log 164).
- **Does it touch my premium?** Two answers, both stated with their limits.
  - **If you buy your own plan without a credit** (above the line, or off the exchange): yes, a little, and in
    the other direction from the fear. CBO projects an extension makes sticker premiums **lower**, because
    healthier people stay in (log 268). For a bill passed now, 2026 is unchanged and 2027 about **5.7% lower**
    (log 144, 269); the **7.6%** is only for a permanent extension enacted by September 2025 (log 136). A buyer
    who gets a credit is mostly shielded either way (log 271, 273).
  - **If your plan comes through work:** **no measured effect.** The lapse measurably raised one hospital
    chain's uninsured patients (log 116), uncompensated care rises with the uninsured (log 275, 276), and the
    law itself records a 2010 finding of a $1,000-a-year "hidden tax" on family premiums (log 274). Whether any
    of that reaches employer premiums is **contested**: CBO and the research review lean against large,
    general cost shifting (log 278, 279), and no study measures it for 2026 (log 277). Say "contested".
- **Both sides at full strength.**
  - **Gold:** your money pays for many who would be insured anyway: on CBO's two estimates, only about half to
    two-thirds of new enrollees are newly insured (log 142, 151), and an extension pulls some workers off
    job-based plans, 2.1 million in 2028 under the three-year bill (log 143, 280). And it is borrowed (log 257).
  - **Green:** your own plan is subsidised too, and by more: about $1,750 a household a year against about $255
    (log 260, 262), roughly seven times the cost (log 163). And without the extension, the people who leave are
    the healthier ones, which raises the sticker price for those who stay (log 147, 185).

The episode walks up those rungs: what changed in the mail at each income, what people did, the part about
fraud that both sides argue (on the $20,000 rung), what bringing it back would cost and who pays (the
neighbour's rung), and the dial. It ends on things the viewer can check on their own notice this month.

## The question

**The extra help on Marketplace premiums ended last year. Should it come back, for whom, and where should
it stop?** Two settings sit under it: **where the help stops** (the 400% cliff, a cap or phase-out
higher up, or no income limit with a ceiling on what anyone pays), and **whether anyone pays $0**.

## Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

**How the help was built, and how it ended**
- **The extra help was written as temporary.** The 2021 pandemic relief law set 0% of income up to 150%
  of poverty, an 8.5% ceiling at 400% and above, and removed the 400% cutoff, for 2021–22, under a
  heading that says "Temporary" (log 15, 16). The 2022 law extended it through 2025 (log 17).
- **It ended by its own sunset. No 2025 law cut it** (log 18). ⛔ Never "Congress cut the subsidies".
- **The 2025 tax-and-budget law (Pub. L. 119-21) is a separate change to the base credit**: no repayment
  cap from tax year 2026, narrower eligibility in 2026 and 2027, pre-enrollment verification from 2028
  (log 19–25). CBO sizes those Marketplace sections at 2.9 million fewer insured in 2035 (repealing them
  would cost $272 billion; log 138).
- **The base credit now.** For 2026, the most anyone pays for the benchmark plan runs from 2.10% of
  income (below 133% of poverty) to a flat 9.96% (300–400%), and the table stops at 400%: above it, no
  credit (log 26, 27). For 2027 the top rate is **10.22%** (log 28). The line is **$63,840** for one and
  **$86,560** for two for 2027 coverage (log 30).

**What the lapse did, measured**
- **What people pay went up 58%.** Across all plan selections, the average monthly premium after credits
  rose from **$113 to $178** (2025 to 2026 open enrollment; log 58). ⛔ **Not 114%**: that was KFF's
  pre-season projection for people keeping the same plan (log 59, 60). The sticker price rose from $619
  to $741 a month (log 61), and the gross benchmark for a 40-year-old 26% (log 64).
- **People traded down.** Bronze went from 30% to 40% of choices, silver from 57% to 43% (log 67). The
  average deductible rose 37%, from $2,759 to $3,786; at 2025's mix of plans it would have risen 6% (log
  66). The share in cost-sharing-reduction silver plans hit a record low of 37% (log 69).
- **Sign-ups barely moved; paid coverage did.** 23.1 million people **selected a plan** for 2026, about
  5% fewer (log 70). **Effectuated (paid) enrollment** in February was **19.2 million, against 21.8
  million**, down 12% (log 71). The share of sign-ups who paid fell from 90% to 83% (log 74). In South
  Carolina, sign-ups fell 7% and paid enrollment 29% (log 77). ⛔ Say which measure every time; HHS's
  analysts use a different February 2025 baseline, 22.1 million (log 72).
- **Every state fell except New Mexico (+14%)** (log 80). HealthCare.gov states fell 15%, states with
  their own exchanges 6% (log 75). Ohio and Oklahoma fell just over 32% (log 76).
- **Who left:** people just above the cliff (400–500% of poverty) were 3% of sign-ups and 27% of the drop;
  their sign-ups fell 44% (log 84). Young adults, 18–34, were 46% of the decline (log 85). Rural sign-ups
  fell 10%, against 4% elsewhere (log 86).
- **The whole individual market**, on and off the exchanges, fell from 25.2 to 20.5 million, June to June
  (log 83).

**What it did to the uninsured: weakly measured so far**
- Census's 7.9% and the federal health survey's 2025 figures are **pre-lapse** (log 107, 108). ⛔ Never use
  them as evidence the lapse changed nothing.
- The only federal post-lapse reading, Census's early "current coverage" table, shows 11.9% of adults
  19–64 uninsured against 11.6%, a change inside its margins (log 109, 110).
- KFF re-interviewed 2025 Marketplace enrollees: 9% now uninsured, 22% in other coverage, 69% re-enrolled;
  14% of the youngest former enrollees are uninsured (log 114, 115). One large hospital chain blamed lost
  exchange coverage for most of a $400 million quarterly hit (log 116).
- **Say on air:** "no federal survey has yet measured a post-lapse national uninsured rate, except one
  early Census table" (log 118).

**New Mexico, the one state that grew**
- Paid enrollment **+14%**, with 96% of sign-ups paying (log 80, 82). The average net premium rose 6.8%,
  to $141 a month, after state aid; with federal credits alone it would have nearly doubled, $152 to $299
  (log 98).
- **What it costs:** state premium help went from about $19.2 million a year to about **$147.6 million**
  (log 94). Federal credits still pay most of it, $494.4 million projected (log 95). The legislature's
  analysts say the fund "may be insolvent by FY28"; the state health agency projects an $85.3 million
  shortfall rising to $273.3 million by FY30 (log 96). It runs on a premium surtax (log 97).
- **Others replaced part:** California $190 million rising to $300 million, against about $2 billion a
  year in lost federal help (log 100, 101); Maryland all of it below 200% of poverty (log 103); Colorado
  about 40% (log 104). Every state that put in its own money beat the median state (log 81).

**2027 prices**
- Insurers proposed a **median 15%** for 2027, after a 20% finalised median for 2026 (log 120). The main
  driver is rising health care prices (a 10% medical trend); the lapse's sicker pool adds about 4 points
  a year (log 121, 122). Approved rates range from **6% in New York** to **22.2% in Washington** (log 124,
  127). Eleven insurers have announced exits, Cigna from all 11 of its states (log 129, 130).
- **HHS's own 2027 rule** projects average enrollment 1.2 to 2 million below an 18.881 million baseline,
  and expects the people who remain to cost 1.7–2.4% more because healthier people leave (log 34, 35).

**What bringing it back would cost, and who gets the money** (CBO, read in CBO's own text)
- **Permanent extension:** **$349.8 billion** added to deficits over 2026–2035; **3.8 million** more
  insured in 2035; gross benchmark premiums **7.6% lower** on average, because a cheaper market keeps
  healthier people in (log 133, 134, 136).
- **H.R. 1834** (through 2028, passed by the House): **$80.6 billion**, with 3.0 million more insured in
  2027 and 4.0 million in 2028, and 2.1 million fewer people on job-based coverage in 2028 (log 141–143).
- **Many new enrollees are not newly insured, and CBO's two estimates differ on how many.** In CBO's 2022 analysis, a permanent extension
  added 4.8 million Marketplace enrollees but cut the uninsured by only 2.2 million (46%, our ratio); the
  rest came from job-based and other plans (log 151, 152). CBO's January 2026 score of the three-year bill (H.R. 1834) is
  higher: in 2028, 4.0 million more insured against 6.2 million more Marketplace enrollees, about two-thirds (log 142;
  our ratio). Say "between about half and two-thirds", naming both. Most new enrollees below 400% were already
  eligible and joined because the help got bigger (log 154).
- **Who gets the new-enrollee money:** in CBO's 2024 letter, **69%** of the credit cost for additional
  enrollees goes to people below 400% of poverty, 31% above (log 158). ⛔ Never "31% of the total cost".
- **The comparator:** the tax break for job-based insurance is worth about **$240 billion** in FY2026,
  income tax alone, roughly **seven times** the yearly average cost of a permanent extension (our ratio;
  log 163). Per person it leans the other way: below 200% of poverty the Marketplace credit averaged
  $6,420 against $1,650 for the employer break; at 800% and above, $730 against $2,580 (log 165, 166).

**The documented fraud**
- **GAO's covert tests got through nearly every time:** 4 of 4 fake applicants approved for 2024; for 2025,
  19 of 20 initially approved and 18 of 20 still covered in September 2025 (log 225–227). GAO says this
  shows a vulnerability and **cannot be generalised** to how common fraud is (log 230).
- **Prosecuted cases are real and large:** a Florida scheme that sought over $233 million in subsidies;
  two executives sentenced to 20 years (log 236, 237); a brokerage agreeing to plead guilty and its former
  parent paying $107 million (log 238).
- Complaints of unauthorized enrollments and plan switches grew more than fourfold from 2023 to 2025
  (log 234). GAO found at least 160,000 applications in 2024 with likely unauthorized broker changes
  (log 231).

## Contested (the real fight)

**GREEN — "Bring it back: the cliff punishes the people in the middle."**
The lapse did what it was predicted to do, and it is measured. What people pay after credits rose 58%,
deductibles rose 37% as people traded down, and one in eight paid enrollees was gone by February (log 58,
66, 71). The cliff is where it bites hardest: people just above 400% of poverty were 3% of sign-ups and 27%
of the drop (log 84). For a 60-year-old just over the line, the extra cost runs to $22,000 a year in some
states, and the payment at least doubles in 46 states (log 174, 175); for a couple both 63 in West
Virginia, over $50,000 (log 176). The people near the line are older (about half are 50–64) and far more
likely to be self-employed (38%, against 7% of adults) (log 171, 172). And the help keeps the market
healthy for everyone: CBO says a permanent extension lowers *gross* premiums 7.6%, which helps people who
get no credit at all (log 136); HHS's own 2027 rule and the insurers' filings both say healthier people
leave when help shrinks (log 35, 122). **New Mexico is the test:** it put its own money in and was the only
state where paid enrollment grew (log 80). Against the cost: the tax break for job-based insurance is
about seven times larger, and it leans toward higher earners (log 163, 166).

**GOLD — "Don't bring it back as it was: it cost too much and paid the wrong things."**
It was sold as temporary pandemic relief, in a statute that says "Temporary" (log 16). A permanent
version costs about $350 billion over ten years (log 133). And most of the money doesn't buy new
coverage: in CBO's 2022 analysis fewer than half of new enrollees were newly insured (about two-thirds in its 2026 score of the three-year bill, log 142); the rest moved from
job-based or other plans, and employers drop coverage when the subsidy is permanent (log 148, 151, 152).
Most new enrollees below 400% were already eligible (log 154). The 2021–25 design had no income limit at
all (log 15), and 31% of the new-enrollee money went above 400% of poverty (log 158). The $0 plans opened
the door to broker fraud: GAO's fake applicants got through nearly every time, a Florida scheme sought
$233 million, and complaints quadrupled (log 225–227, 234, 236). Underlying prices are rising at 10% a
year with or without the help (log 121), and the one peer-reviewed study of tying subsidies to prices
found it raised them 1–6%, more where competition is thin (log 183). Even New Mexico's answer is fragile:
its fund may be insolvent by FY28 (log 96).

**The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:**

- *Gold at green:* **"More insured" is mostly "insured differently."** Only about half to two-thirds of CBO's new
  enrollees are newly insured (log 142, 151); H.R. 1834 moves 2.1 million off job-based plans (log 143).
  **The cliff hits few people:** about 7% of 2025 sign-ups were above 400% (log 170). **New Mexico is not
  a model at scale:** federal credits still pay most of its help, and its own analysts warn of
  insolvency (log 95, 96). **The uninsured effect is not yet measured** (log 118).
- *Green at gold:* **"Mostly fraud" is not what the record shows.** The drop came at the payment step,
  where people saw a real bill (log 74, 77); it fell hardest just above the cliff and among the young
  (log 84, 85); Paragon's own series says improper sign-ups barely fell (log 200); and CMS's big
  cancellation came six months after the February snapshot (log 217). **"Temporary" isn't an argument
  about value:** it describes the statute, not whether the help is worth it. **The job-based tax break is
  the same kind of subsidy and much bigger** (log 163). **Prices:** the one causal study is Massachusetts,
  not the 2021–25 credits, and CBO's model runs the other way (log 183, 185, 188).
- *Both at both:* **the fraud numbers.** Gold's are models built by HHS's own officials or an aligned
  think tank (log 189–201). Green's critics offer no corrected count (log 203, 212–214).

### The floor every setting shares

Every setting keeps the **base credit** that has existed since 2014: help on a sliding scale, worked out
as the benchmark plan's price minus a set share of your income (log 26–28). No proposal in the record
removes it, and the 2025 law's tightening of who qualifies (log 20–25) applies under every setting unless
Congress changes it. **The dial does not move this floor.**

### A note beside the dial: fixing prices instead of premiums (pitch Ruling 2)

A short beat, not a third dial: the record scores two price-side bills, and neither changes what care costs.
- **Funding cost-sharing reductions (ending "silver loading").** CBO and JCT have long found that paying insurers
  the CSR money directly **saves** the government money, because insurers stop loading that cost onto silver
  premiums, which shrinks every credit pegged to silver (log 281). For the House-passed 2025 provision, CRS
  reports CBO's estimate as about **$30.8 billion less spending and $2.8 billion more revenue over ten years**
  (log 282); it was **not enacted** (log 283). The catch: sticker prices fall, but subsidised bronze and gold
  buyers likely **pay more**, because their credit shrinks (log 284). ⛔ No premium percentage airs as CBO's
  (log 285); KFF's 300,000 more uninsured stays off air (log 162).
- **The HSA alternative, S. 3386.** Introduced Dec 8, 2025 by the Senate Finance chair (by role on air), it puts
  $1,000 a year (ages 18–49) or $1,500 (50–64) into a health savings account for bronze or catastrophic buyers up
  to 700% of poverty, for 2026–27 only, capped at $10 billion a year, and it also funds CSRs from 2027 (log
  286–289). Cloture failed 51–48 on Dec 11, 2025 (log 46). The sponsors say it saves money; **we found no
  independent score** (log 290).
- **"Only a third of the money reaches consumers."** The claim, corrected: **$1 to consumers for every $2
  elsewhere, about 34 cents on the dollar** (log 291). Its basis, said with it: a Joint Economic Committee
  majority brief, an interested source, resting on **one 2017 working-paper model of the original credits**,
  not a measurement of the 2021–25 credits (log 292, 293). Set it against CBO's model, in which the lapse
  **raises** gross premiums (log 185).
- **Provider prices, as context only.** Across regions, about half of the variation in private insurance
  spending is price, and monopoly hospitals charge about 12% more (log 294, employer plans); insurers' 2027
  filings cite provider contract increases (log 295). No option on the table was scored to change prices.

## The reframe

The shouting is **"bring back the help" against "it was fraud and waste"**, as if the question were yes
or no, and as if the fraud fight and the cost fight were separate. They are the same fight, in one place:
the **$0 plan**. The $0 plan is what made the extra help reach the lowest earners with no bill at all
(log 15). It is also where the broker fraud lived: CMS's warning signs include a $0 premium, GAO's fake
applicants went through brokers, and HHS's analysts saw half the people moved off $0 plans never pay a
first bill (log 195, 219, 225–227). And at the other end, the cliff decides who gets nothing (log 27, 84).

So the real decision is not "subsidies, yes or no." It is **two settings on one design: where the help
stops, and whether anyone pays nothing.** Each setting trades one documented failure for another: a
cliff that costs a couple thousands for one extra dollar, or a cost and a crowd-out that CBO measured; a
$0 plan that brokers can exploit, or a bill that some real people won't pay.

## Both sides quietly agree

**Each side's own sources concede the other side's key point.**
- **The integrity side's source says the drop wasn't mainly fraud falling away.** Paragon, whose 6.2
  million is the integrity side's biggest number, says in the same report that improper sign-ups fell
  only about 250,000 from 2025 to 2026 (log 200). On its own measure, the 2026 loss happened at the
  payment step, not at sign-up.
- **The skeptics' sources say the fraud was real.** KFF says fraud is real and the dispute is its scale;
  the insurer-commissioned actuary says improper enrollment likely raised no-claims rates; the Urban
  economist concedes real improper enrollment, clearest in Florida (log 203, 212, 213).
- **And the current administration's own rule makes the extension backers' market argument.** HHS's 2027
  Payment Notice expects healthier people to leave and costs to rise 1.7–2.4% for those who stay (log
  35), the same direction as CBO's model and the insurers' filings (log 122, 136). Nobody in the record
  disputes that less help means a sicker, pricier pool. The fight is over whether that is worth $35
  billion a year, and to whom it should go.

## Two short fact beats (pitch Ruling 3)

Each about 20 seconds, answering the two most-liked comments the council found (council card). Facts only.
- **Congress's own plans.** Members of Congress are **not exempt**: since 2014 the ACA has put Members and
  their office staff on an ACA exchange, DC Health Link's **small-business (SHOP)** side, and out of the
  federal employee plan (log 296, 297, 299). They do **not** get the premium tax credit: the government, as
  their employer, pays the ordinary federal-employee share, 72% of the average premium, capped at 75% of the
  plan's, and they pay the rest by payroll deduction, like most people with job coverage (log 298, 300).
  ⛔ Never "exempt", never "the same subsidy you get", never "free". Leave out the 2017 report's plan details
  and the committee-staff question (not re-checked as current; log 297's report is dated 2017).
- **The state-branded exchanges are Obamacare.** For 2026, 20 states and DC ran their own exchanges; for 2027
  it is **21 states plus DC**, Oregon the newest (log 301, 302). Kentucky's **kynect** (lowercase k), **Covered
  California** and Pennsylvania's **Pennie** are ACA marketplaces with state names on them, and the help on
  them is the same federal credit (log 303, 304). If a survey airs at all, only as *"a 2017 survey found about
  a third didn't know the ACA and Obamacare are the same law"*, dated on screen (log 306); no KFF figure on
  name confusion exists in the record (log 305).
