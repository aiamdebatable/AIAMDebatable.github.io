The verdict

What the record supports
Losing the help raised bills and cut paid coverage; that's measured. Whether bringing it back is worth the cost, and for whom, is a values question with agreed numbers. The size of the fraud is genuinely unmeasured.
What didn't hold up
Premiums didn't double; the measured rise is fifty-eight percent. The drop wasn't mostly fraud; the biggest estimate's own series says improper sign-ups barely fell. Nor was the fraud trivial; fake applicants got through, and executives got twenty years. Most of the money doesn't go to the rich. Your premium doesn't pay for anyone else's; your taxes do. And Congress didn't exempt itself.

Read the verdict as it aired

Cole This one doesn't resolve into a yes or a no, and neither team wins it. Three things from me: what's settled, what survived, and the dial that's yours.

Cole Both teams accepted the same record. The extra help ended on schedule; no law cut it. Since then, what people pay after the credit is up fifty-eight percent, and about one in eight paid enrollees is gone. You pay for the help through your taxes, borrowed unless it's paid for. And fraud through brokers on free plans was real.

Cole What survived from green. The cliff is real: one dollar over the line costs all of the help, and sign-ups fell hardest just above it. New Mexico replaced most of the lost help, and it was the only state that grew. And the job-based tax break is about seven times bigger than a permanent extension.

Cole What survived from gold. It's expensive and it's borrowed: about thirty-five billion dollars a year over ten years, for a permanent version. Only about half to two-thirds of new enrollees were uninsured before. The old design had no top income limit. And the brokers came in through its free plans.

Cole What fell. Premiums didn't double; the measured rise is fifty-eight percent. The drop wasn't mostly fraud; the biggest estimate's own series says improper sign-ups barely fell. Nor was the fraud trivial; fake applicants got through, and executives got twenty years. Most of the money doesn't go to the rich. Your premium doesn't pay for anyone else's; your taxes do. And Congress didn't exempt itself.

Cole So here's what the record supports. Losing the help raised bills and cut paid coverage; that's measured. Whether bringing it back is worth the cost, and for whom, is a values question with agreed numbers. The size of the fraud is genuinely unmeasured.

Cole That leaves two settings neither team can set for you. Where the help stops: at the cliff, the law now, which fails the person one dollar over; at a cap higher up, never tried or scored; or nowhere, with no one paying more than eight and a half percent of income, which costs the most. And whether anyone's plan costs nothing: free, the door the brokers used; a small minimum, which a court struck down; or a share of income, the law now, a bill on the lowest rung.

Cole Whatever you think, two things are worth doing. Check your twenty twenty-seven price, not last year's, on HealthCare dot gov or your state's exchange. And estimate your income carefully: there's no longer a cap on paying help back. Where should the help stop, and should anyone pay nothing? That one's yours.

The judge named no sides on this one, so there's no dial: we don't invent positions.
