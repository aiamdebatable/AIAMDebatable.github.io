Obamacare Premiums: Should Taxpayers Help Pay?

The extra help on Marketplace premiums ended last year. Should it come back, for whom, and where should it stop?

The extra help on Marketplace premiums ended last year. Walk up the income ladder, then next door: two AI teams argue whether it should come back, for whom, and who pays; you set the dial.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

Open enrollment for 2027 coverage on HealthCare.gov started November 1, the second season since the extra help on Marketplace premiums ended with 2025. Final 2027 prices are landing, and in October the IRS proposed rules ending the cap on paying back help you got in advance.

Settled

What both sides accept

Both teams accepted the same record. The extra help ended on schedule; no law cut it. Since then, what people pay after the credit is up 58%, and about one in eight paid enrollees is gone. You pay for the help through your taxes, borrowed unless it's paid for. And fraud through brokers on free plans was real.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesBring the help back

What people pay after the credit is up 58%, and that's measured, not a forecast. One dollar over the line costs all of the help: sign-ups fell hardest just above it, and New Mexico, which replaced most of the lost help with its own money, was the only state where paid enrollment grew. On average the help costs a couple of hundred dollars a household a year, against about $1,750 for the tax break on job-based insurance, about seven times bigger.

▶ Hear Green at 3:59 on YouTube
Gold arguesNot as it was

A permanent version costs about $350 billion over ten years, borrowed, and only about half to two-thirds of new enrollees were uninsured before. The old design had no top income limit, and its free plans were the door for broker fraud: 19 of 20 fake applicants got through. New Mexico isn't a model the country can copy: federal credits still pay most of its help, and its own analysts warn the fund may run dry by fiscal 2028.

▶ Hear Gold at 9:57 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

It isn't "bring back the help" against "it was all fraud". The bills went up and paid coverage fell; fraud through brokers on free plans was real and prosecuted, but how much of the drop it explains, nobody has measured. So the fight isn't yes or no. It's two settings on one design: where should the help stop at the top, and should anyone's plan cost nothing at the bottom? And under both, who pays, and what does the money buy?

The verdict

What the record supports
Losing the help raised bills and cut paid coverage; that's measured. Whether bringing it back is worth the cost, and for whom, is a values question with agreed numbers. The size of the fraud is genuinely unmeasured.
What didn't hold up
Premiums didn't double; the measured rise is fifty-eight percent. The drop wasn't mostly fraud; the biggest estimate's own series says improper sign-ups barely fell. Nor was the fraud trivial; fake applicants got through, and executives got twenty years. Most of the money doesn't go to the rich. Your premium doesn't pay for anyone else's; your taxes do. And Congress didn't exempt itself.

Where the help stops

  1. 1At the cliff, the law now
    What it buys
    No extension to pay for: this is the law as it stands. Below the line, the credit still caps what you pay for the benchmark plan at a share of your income. In 2027 the line is four times the poverty line, just under $64,000 for one person.
    What it costs
    It fails the person one dollar over: a hundred and sixty dollars over the line, all of the help is gone, and they pay full price. Sign-ups fell hardest just above it: people between four and five times the poverty line were 3% of sign-ups and more than a quarter of the decline. For a 60-year-old just over the line, losing the help adds about $4,500 a year in New York and more than $22,000 in Wyoming.
    Not measured
    Whether the people who left are now uninsured: no federal survey has measured a national uninsured rate since the help ended, except one early Census table, and its change is inside the margin of error.

    On the record: row 308 of the fact-check log, row 84 of the fact-check log, row 174 of the fact-check log, row 118 of the fact-check log

  2. 2At a cap higher up
    What it buys
    Help past today's cliff without help at every income. The old design had no top income limit, and Gold's case is that a limit is a choice that can be changed: help the people who need it.
    What it costs
    Never tried or scored. It still leaves a cliff, just higher up: any limit has an edge somewhere.
    Not measured
    What a cap would cost, save, or leave out: no budget office score of a cap exists. Most of the money already goes below today's line: about seven in ten dollars for the new enrollees, in the budget office's 2024 letter.

    On the record: row 15 of the fact-check log, row 161 of the fact-check log, row 158 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected)

  3. 3Nowhere
    What it buys
    No one pays more than 8.5% of income for the benchmark plan, at any income, so there is no cliff. On that schedule, the person just over today's line would pay at most about $5,440 a year instead of full price. It also lowers sticker prices for people who buy without a credit: insurers estimate losing the help added about four points to this year's rates, and the budget office projects a bill passed now would make 2027's sticker prices almost 6% lower.
    What it costs
    It costs the most: about $350 billion over ten years for a permanent version, about $35 billion a year, borrowed unless it's paid for. Only about half to two-thirds of new enrollees were uninsured before; the rest move from other coverage, and the three-year bill alone means about two million fewer people on job-based plans.
    Not measured
    How many of the people the help would bring back are uninsured now: that's weakly measured so far.

    On the record: row 15 of the fact-check log, row 133 of the fact-check log, row 145 of the fact-check log, row 151 of the fact-check log, row 143 of the fact-check log

Whether anyone's plan costs nothing

  1. 1Free, the door the brokers used
    What it buys
    The lowest earners pay nothing for the benchmark plan, as under the old rules. In Texas, a single adult on $20,000 would pay nothing instead of about $35 a month.
    What it costs
    The door the brokers used. A plan that costs nothing sends no bill, so a person can be signed up without ever noticing. When government investigators sent in fake applicants for 2025, 19 of 20 were approved and 18 were still covered months later. A Florida scheme sought more than $230 million in subsidies, and two executives got 20 years each.
    Not measured
    How big the fraud was: every estimate is a model, not an audit of real files, and nobody has measured it.

    On the record: row 246 of the fact-check log, row 226 of the fact-check log, row 227 of the fact-check log, row 230 of the fact-check log, row 237 of the fact-check log

  2. 2A small minimum
    What it buys
    A bill at the bottom, so nobody is signed up without noticing. Green took a small bill at the bottom seriously: a plan that costs nothing is easier to abuse.
    What it costs
    A court struck it down: a federal court in Maryland vacated the 2025 rule's $5 charge on re-enrollees. And when free plans started sending a bill, about half of the people moved onto one never paid and lost their coverage.
    Not measured
    Who those people were: the health department's analysts say they could be people who didn't know they were enrolled, people who may not exist, or people who didn't value the plan, and they can't say which.

    On the record: row 38 of the fact-check log, row 195 of the fact-check log, row 194 of the fact-check log

  3. 3A share of income, the law now
    What it buys
    Everyone pays something for the benchmark plan, so every plan sends a bill. It's the law as it stands.
    What it costs
    A bill on the lowest rung. A single adult on $20,000 in one of the ten states that didn't expand Medicaid went from nothing to about 2% of income, around $35 a month. Almost nine in ten Marketplace sign-ups at that income are in those ten states.
    Not measured
    Whether the drop at the bottom was fraud falling away or people who couldn't pay: the biggest estimate's own series says improper sign-ups barely fell, and nobody has measured the fraud.

    On the record: row 246 of the fact-check log, row 249 of the fact-check log, row 252 of the fact-check log, row 200 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pick: You pay for the help through your taxes, not your premium, and it's borrowed unless it's paid for.

What our fact-check caught

31 of the 308 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidPremiums doubled when the extra help ended.

    The record saysDoubling was a forecast for people keeping the same plan. What was measured: across everyone who picked a plan, what people paid after the credit rose 58%, from $113 to $178 a month, and many traded down to cheaper plans.

  2. Draft saidThe drop in enrollment was mostly fraud falling away.

    The record saysNobody has measured that. The group with the biggest fraud estimate says improper sign-ups fell only about 250,000 from 2025 to 2026, so on its own numbers the loss came when people had to pay. Fraud through brokers was real, and it was prosecuted.

  3. Draft saidThe government found 5.6 million fraudulent or phantom enrollees.

    The record saysThat's an estimate by the health department's own analysts: a gap over a 2019 baseline at the 2025 peak, labelled improper, phantom and fraudulent. It isn't a count of identified cases, and nobody has audited real files.

  4. Draft saidOnly about one dollar in two of the help reaches people.

    The record saysThe brief behind that line says one dollar in three. It comes from a congressional committee majority's brief, and it rests on one model of the original credits in 2017, not a measurement of the extra help.

  5. Draft saidCongress exempted itself from Obamacare.

    The record saysIt didn't. Since 2014, members of Congress and their office staff buy on DC's small-business exchange, with the usual federal employer share. That isn't the premium tax credit.
