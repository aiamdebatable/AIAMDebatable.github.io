Is Deer Meat Good for You?

Is venison really healthier than beef, and is it safe to eat?

Last changed 2026-11-04 · See every change

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

One deer, three people: the hunter, the person given the meat, and a parent. What's good in venison, what can make you sick, and what each should do before dinner.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

Every fall, states ask hunters to get their deer tested for chronic wasting disease. This year Wisconsin found its first case in Marinette County, and Michigan asked for deer heads from Kent, Montcalm and three neighbouring counties.

Settled

What the record has settled

Lean venison is as lean as chicken breast, with a lot more iron, but ground venison with added pork fat is a different food. No human case of chronic wasting disease has ever been reported, and it's not known if people could get infected. A "not detected" test makes infection less likely; it doesn't certify the meat. And lead from the bullet collects in ground meat.

What people get wrong

Whether deer meat is safe depends on which of three people you are: the hunter who shot it, the person given it, or a parent with a child under six at the table or someone pregnant. On the deer disease, the honest verdict isn't "it's safe" and it isn't "it's coming": it's not proven yet. And the risk almost nobody mentions is lead.

What to watch: Which of the three are you?

This episode was an explainer, so nobody argued sides and there's no dial to set. The verdict on good for you: yes, with limits. Lean venison is as lean as chicken breast, with a lot more iron, and the burger and the sausage are a different food. On safe, it depends on which of three people you are, and the verdict gave each one its own checks before dinner.

  1. If you shot it. In a CWD area, get it tested. It's free in all three states. Keep the meat separate and identifiable until the result. Never eat or share a positive deer: Wisconsin says landfill the meat and soak your knives in half bleach, half water. Eat before the result? The states differ, in writing: Minnesota's DNR says it's the hunter's choice, and Wisconsin's health department says don't eat it or give it away until the result says not detected. Whoever you share it with is relying on your choice.
  2. If you were given it. Ask two questions. Was it tested, and what came back? And could the sausage hold trim from other deer? Wisconsin warns that sausage and jerky may contain trim from other deer, which may or may not have been tested. Not detected means less likely, not certified: the test looks at two lymph nodes from the throat, not the meat, and it's weaker early in an infection. And cook it to 160 degrees, the federal chart's figure for venison.
  3. If you've already eaten from a deer that later tests positive. No agency we read says what to do. Their advice covers only the before.
  4. If there's a child under six at the table, or someone pregnant. Everything above, plus lead. Three state health departments say don't serve them lead-shot venison. Most fragments are too small to see, and ground meat is where they collect: Minnesota found lead in 26% of ground venison packages, against 2% of whole cuts. Whole cuts, or meat from a deer shot with copper, is the lower-lead plate. Nobody has linked venison lead to an illness, but nobody has measured health outcomes in the people who eat it, either, and the CDC says no safe blood lead level in children has been identified.

What our fact-check caught

34 of the 323 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidVenison is lower in cholesterol than beef or chicken.

    The record saysIt's about the same. Cooked lean venison runs close to chicken breast and beef, and a few venison entries run higher.

  2. Draft saidVenison is full of omega-3.

    The record saysWild deer meat has a good balance of omega fats, like grass-fed beef, but the amounts are tiny. Next to salmon, it's a trace.

  3. Draft saidCooking it well makes infected venison safe.

    The record saysCooking and freezing don't remove the prions. In one infected elk, medium-well grilling made them easier for a lab to find, and even the boiling water tested positive.

  4. Draft saidTwo hunters died from eating deer with chronic wasting disease.

    The record saysThat came from a 2024 conference poster. The one diagnosis was ordinary, sporadic CJD, the authors say causation is unproven, and there's no evidence the meat came from an infected deer.

  5. Draft saidMore than 600 Wisconsin hunters ate meat from infected deer and none got sick, so it's safe.

    The record saysThey said they ate it, or meant to. And a human infection could take ten years or more to show, so with that few people, zero cases is what you'd expect either way.
