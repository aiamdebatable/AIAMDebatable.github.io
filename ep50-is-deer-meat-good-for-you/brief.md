# Research brief — ep50: Is deer meat good for you, and is it safe to eat?

_The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video._

**Episode type: EXPLAINER.** No teams and no clash. The episode follows one deer to three people: the hunter
who shot it, the person it was given to, and a parent of a child under 6 or a pregnant person. It covers what
venison is, how each risk works, the measured numbers with a comparator a viewer can picture, and exactly where
the disagreement starts; then it hands each of the three people their own answer. Why an explainer: most of
this question is settled (the nutrition is measured; cooking does not destroy prions; lead fragments are real
and concentrated in ground meat), and the places the record is genuinely open (how big the CWD risk to people
is, what a "not detected" test can promise, and how much fragment lead matters in a body) are better shown as
"what each study measured" than argued as two sides.

## The question, in one line

**"Someone handed you deer burger. Is it good for you, and is it safe?"**

## The opening: three people, one deer

_The council's change, ruled by Lucas 2026-11-01. Premise before the object, feeling over figures, ≤ ~100
words, no numbers needed. The lines below are the shape and the facts, not the script; the house lines vary._

- **The premise first:** is deer meat good for you, and is it safe? The honest answer depends on which of three
  people you are.
- **The object:** a neighbour hands you a package in white butcher paper. Deer burger. You didn't shoot it. You
  don't know whether anyone tested it, or whose other deer went through the grinder with it. And there's a
  small child at your table.
- **Why it's real, not a device:** venison is shared food. In Michigan, 85% of successful hunters shared their
  venison, most often inside their own household (69%) (T3-31, SURVEYED, Michigan DNR's 2013 Deer Harvest Study).
  Three in four Michiganders have eaten game, including 59% of people who don't hunt (D42). So the person eating
  an untested deer is often not the person who decided whether to test it.
- **The journey line:** one deer, three people: the hunter who shot it, the person it was given to (a
  neighbour, a relative, a food-pantry client), and a parent of a child under 6 or someone pregnant. What's good
  in it, what can make you sick, and what each of the three should do before dinner.

**Then, straight after the town's rule line, the answer first** (the chair's change): lean, an iron win, not a
superfood; safe enough if you do a few things, and one of the risks is one almost nobody mentions. Then the peg
in one structural sentence (see Why now).

## The honest overall verdict, forked

**Good for you: yes, with limits** (the same for all three people). A lean venison steak is among the leanest
meats there is, in the same club as a skinless chicken breast and pork tenderloin rather than above it, and it
carries about four times the iron of chicken (A1–A5, A13). But it is not meaningfully lower in cholesterol (A8:
cooked lean cuts about the same as chicken and beef, some entries higher), its omega-3 story is a ratio of tiny
amounts (A21–A24), nobody has ever measured a health payoff from eating it (A29), and the burger, sausage and
jerky most families actually eat are a different food (A12, A30, A37).

**Safe: it depends which of the three you are.** What holds for everyone: no human has ever been shown to catch
CWD (B2-20); the newest monkey study moved the science from "no transmission seen" to "limited transmission, in
a lab" (B2-9, B2-12); the mad-cow precedent makes "not proven yet" the honest verdict, not "safe" (T2-28 to
T2-34); a "not detected" test makes infection less likely but is not a food-safety certificate (T1-22, T1-23,
B1-26); cooking doesn't remove prions (B2-35 to B2-37); cook venison to 160 °F (D11, D12); and the risk people talk
about least, lead fragments, is the one most clearly measured in the meat itself (C1–C3, C13), though how much it
matters in a body is small on average and unresolved (C18, C42).

1. **The hunter who shot it.** In a CWD area, test it (free in all three states: B1-22, B1-24, B1-27, B1-40). Until
   the result comes, keep the venison "separate and identifiable" (Minnesota DNR, T1-24) and have it processed on
   its own (CDC, B1-33). Never eat, share or feed to the dog a positive deer (B1-32, B2-19, T1-29); Wisconsin and
   Michigan replace the tag (T1-28, T1-29). **Whether to eat before the result** is a decision two state agencies
   answer differently in writing, shown side by side (see Beat 3), with the wait: about 9 days in Wisconsin, one to
   two weeks in Minnesota, and up to 6–8 weeks for Michigan's drop-box samples (B1-21, B1-25, T1-35). Whoever
   you share it with is relying on that decision.
2. **The person given it** (a neighbour, a relative, a food-pantry client). Ask two questions: **was it tested,
   and what came back?** and **was it processed on its own, or could the sausage hold trim from other, untested
   deer?** Wisconsin tells hunters to ask exactly that of processors (B1-37, T3-29); Minnesota's agriculture
   department tells processors to hold carcass or trim until results arrive (B1-39). "Not detected" means less
   likely, not certified (T1-22, T1-23). Cook it to 160 °F (D11). Donated venison: Wisconsin's program tests by
   county tier and lets the processor hold meat until results are known (B1-34, T1-32); Minnesota X-rays donated
   venison and throws out about one pound in ten (C38). **The honest gap:** no Wisconsin, Michigan, Minnesota, CDC
   or USDA page says what to do if you've already eaten from a deer that later tests positive, or tells the hunter
   to warn the people he gave it to; the agencies' advice covers only the before: wait, or keep it separate
   (T1-31). Say that plainly; invent nothing.
3. **A parent of a child under 6, or someone pregnant.** Everything above, plus lead: North Dakota, Minnesota's
   health department and Michigan's MDHHS say children under 6 (Michigan: 6 and under) and pregnant women
   (Michigan: women of childbearing age) should not eat lead-shot venison (C22, C24, C26); New York (October 2025)
   says pregnant people and children should choose other meats (C27). Ground meat is where the fragments
   concentrate, about a quarter of packages against 2% of whole cuts in Minnesota's screening (C13), and most are
   too small to see or feel (C23, C41). Whole cuts, or meat from a deer shot with copper, is the lower-lead plate
   (C9, C13, C32–C34). No safe blood-lead level in children is known (C21).

**Is it worth it?** (the explicit weighing the chair asked for; no pick on the eat-before question). The upside
is real but modest: lean meat and a lot of iron (A1–A5, A13). The steps that make it safer cost little: a free
test where CWD is, a thermometer, whole cuts or copper for the youngest plates. For the hunter it's a choice made
with both agencies' answers in hand; for the person given it, it's two questions; for the parent, the lead advice
is the one that changes what goes on the plate.

## The journey, as beats

_Town places are suggestions for the board (step 5b); every line gets a place._

### Beat 1 — Is it good for us?
- **Open on the label, not the myth.** One cooked quarter-pound patty is the 3-ounce serving every number
  here uses (A31). A venison loin steak: about 128 calories and 2 g of fat. A skinless chicken breast:
  about 140 and 3 g. Pork tenderloin: about 122 and 3 g. Lean beef sirloin: about 156 and 5 g (A1–A5;
  per-3-oz values are our arithmetic on USDA's per-100 g figures). **Venison is in the chicken-breast
  club, not above it.** [square chart, close framing]
- **The one real win: iron.** About 3.5 mg per serving, roughly a fifth of a day's iron, against about
  0.9 mg in chicken and 1.7 mg in sirloin (A13; DV from A20). About four chicken breasts' worth (our
  arithmetic). [store tag]
- **The myth that fails: cholesterol.** Cooked lean venison runs about the same as chicken and beef: loin 79,
  top round 85, tenderloin 88 mg per 100 g, against chicken breast 85, sirloin 82, pork tenderloin 73; the
  generic roasted entry and braised shoulder run higher (112–113). No advantage, not a penalty (A8; per 100 g
  cooked, FDC). [store tag, stamped]
- **The turn: what's actually in the package.** Plain ground venison has about 40% less fat than an 85/15
  beef patty (A37), but hunters' own grinding guides add 10–30% pork fat (A12), and with 20% fatback the
  burger lands in 80/20 territory (A12, A37). USDA's venison-sausage recipe entry has more fat per 100 g
  than raw 80/20 beef (A30). The person given a package rarely knows which it is. [butcher's counter]
- **Nobody measured the payoff.** No study has followed venison eaters for heart or cancer outcomes
  (A29); IARC's "red meat" means all mammalian muscle meat and simply doesn't name deer (A28).
  "Healthier than beef" is a reading of the label, not a result. **Hold that.**

### Beat 2 — Can it make us sick? (CWD, the science)
- **What it is.** A prion disease of deer, elk and moose. Prions are misfolded proteins; heat and freezing
  don't remove them (B2-35). For decades a Colorado–Wyoming problem in the wild (first noted 1967, first
  wild case 1981), then South Dakota whitetails in 2001 and Wisconsin in 2002 (B1-5, B1-6). Now 37 states
  and five Canadian provinces (USGS, June 2026, B1-1). [cloud map rise: USGS publishes official maps for
  "prior to 2000" and every year 2004–2025, B1-7]
- **Where it's established, it's common.** In Wyoming in 2025, about 1 in 5 adult male mule deer and 1 in
  3 adult male whitetails tested positive (B1-8). In Richland County, Wisconsin, 428 of 1,198 *submitted*
  deer were positive, about 1 in 3 (B1-10). [cloud map, then the stamp]
- **And you can't see it.** Most positive deer look healthy; the deer looks healthy "throughout most of the
  infection" (Minnesota DNR, B1-29, T1-33); a lab test is the only way to tell (B1-31). The Marinette buck that
  made this season's news is the exception: it was put down because it looked sick (B1-13).
- **The fear: the 2017 alarm.** In April 2017 a Health Canada internal note described unpublished results
  as CWD "transmitted" to macaque monkeys, including by feeding muscle (B2-16). Headlines followed.
- **The record: what the 2026 paper actually found.** The peer-reviewed study, nine years later, is titled
  "Limited transmission". 18 macaques were dosed (B2-1) and a few were examined years later. The standard
  test found no prions in any of them (B2-4). Ultra-sensitive amplification tests found faint seeding in
  6 of the 7 brains tested (B2-5). (⚠ "7 analysed, mostly 5–6 years after exposure" is row B2-2, which is
  APPROXIMATE because the paper itself says seven in one place and eight in another, and gives three time
  windows: keep that count and the "5–6 years" off air; "6 of 7 brains" is B2-5, CONFIRMED.) Tissue from one
  monkey that had CWD implanted in its brain, passed through mice and then voles, gave those rodents full prion
  disease (B2-8). The authors: the results "do not imply efficient transmission to primates" (B2-9), but the
  jump "cannot be excluded" (T2-28). [HQ research room]
- **Two serious labs disagree.** The NIH's Rocky Mountain Labs watched macaques for 11–13 years and found
  nothing by any test (B2-14, B2-15). The Calgary team points to its younger, female-only animals, muscle
  in the inocula and higher doses (B2-11). Outside experts split: one says leftover inoculum is a serious
  possibility; another says the prions look newly made (B2-12).
- **No human case.** "CWD infections in people have never been reported, and so far, it's not known if
  people could get infected" (CDC, B2-20). After the paper, CDC said it has found no evidence of CWD in
  people and that more years of watching are needed (B2-13). The hunter cohorts show nothing unusual so
  far, but the watch is short-sighted: Wisconsin's 642 hunters who ate or intended to eat positive venison
  are so few that zero cases is what anyone would expect (B2-32, B2-33), and human incubation is likely
  10 years or more (B2-21). [tower clock]
- **The 2024 "two hunters died" story** was a poster case report; the one diagnosis was ordinary sporadic
  CJD, and the authors say causation is unproven (B2-28). CDC told USA TODAY the cases were consistent with
  classic sporadic CJD (B2-29).
- **"They said mad cow couldn't cross either." The fair answer, both ways** (T2; the most-liked argument in the
  comments, 4,100 likes).
  - *What they actually said.* For ten years the UK government told the public there was "no evidence" BSE could
    reach people, that it was "most unlikely" to pose any risk, and that it was "safe to eat beef" (the BSE
    Inquiry's own summary, T2-1). The official line was "remote", never "impossible" (T2-2, T2-12); but the
    Chief Medical Officer said on TV in 1990 there was "no risk" (T2-7), and the Health Secretary agreed in
    December 1995 there was "no conceivable risk" (T2-8). In March 1996, ten young people had a new variant of
    CJD, probably from BSE (T2-15). vCJD has killed **233 people worldwide, 178 of them in the UK** (T2-16, T2-17),
    with a median age at death of 28 (T2-18). [the tower clock, then the stamp]
  - *Why they were wrong.* The 1989 reassurance was borrowed from a different disease: BSE probably came from
    sheep scrapie, and scrapie had not infected people in over 200 years (T2-3). The Inquiry's verdict: the
    government "did not lie", but its "campaign of reassurance was a mistake" (T2-9).
  - *Why CWD is not the same bet.* Today's reassurance rests on experiments with CWD itself. Fed by mouth, BSE
    infected **18 of 18** macaques given 5 g of cattle brain (T2-26); CWD at a similar dose (4 g in total) showed
    no clinical, pathological or biochemical sign in 11–13 years (T2-27, B2-15). (⛔ "18 of 18" is BSE, never CWD.)
  - *Where the precedent warns hardest.* In BSE cattle the infectivity sat in brain and spinal cord, which is why
    pulling the offal protected beef eaters; in CWD deer, infectious prions are in the muscle, the venison itself
    (T2-29). CWD comes in several strains, so "past experience does not necessarily predict current or future
    risk" (CIDRAP, T2-30). And a human CWD case might not be recognised quickly: it could be misfiled as ordinary
    CJD, which is not nationally notifiable in the US (T2-32). vCJD surfaced about 10 years after BSE was found in
    cattle (T2-33).
  - *The verdict line:* **not proven yet**, not "safe" and not "it's coming" (T2-28 to T2-34). One public-health
    scientist predicted around 2019 that human cases were "probable" "in the years ahead"; none has been
    documented so far, which is "not yet", never "he was wrong" (T2-14). [HQ research room, then the clock]
- **Cooking doesn't help.** In one infected elk, medium-well grilling made the prions *easier* for the lab
  to find, and even the boiling water was positive (B2-37). Bleach cleaned the grinders; dish soap didn't
  reliably clean cutting boards or steel grinders (B2-39). [the diner's grill] **Hold that.**

### Beat 3 — The test, and why people eat anyway
- **What the test looks at.** Not the meat: two lymph nodes from the throat (Minnesota DNR, T1-1; Wisconsin's
  lab tests the medial retropharyngeal nodes and runs no test on anything else, T1-2). That is the right tissue:
  in 4,430 Wisconsin deer tested both ways, 1 positive in 5 showed prions only in the lymph node and none only in
  the brainstem (T1-15). The answer comes back "detected" or "not detected" (T1-1); there is no "CWD-free"
  result (T1-20). [family-chat phone]
- **Good at the deer it can see.** On 1,300 Michigan lymph nodes, the screening test, the confirming test and
  the newest lab test disagreed on 7 (0.5%), and all 7 were false alarms, not misses (T1-10).
- **Weaker early on.** Every study and review says the test is less sensitive early in infection, when few
  prions have built up (T1-11, T1-13, T1-14). In a lab study, prions showed up in fawns' throat lymph tissue as
  early as 42 days after exposure (T1-16), so a deer infected in the weeks before the hunt can test clean. That
  last step is an inference: nobody has counted the miss rate in wild deer (T1-19).
- **So what "not detected" means.** Less likely, not certified. Virginia's wildlife agency puts it most clearly:
  the tests "are not food safety tests", early infections may sit below detection, and a negative makes
  infection "considerably less likely" without guaranteeing it (T1-23). Minnesota's DNR: "not a food safety
  test" (B1-26). Wisconsin's DNR has said a "not detected" "does not guarantee" the animal isn't infected, "it
  does make it less likely" (T1-22, via a 2019 reprint). And why a missed infection isn't a zero question:
  infectious prions have been found in the muscle of infected deer, including deer that showed no signs (T1-27).
- **If it comes back positive.** Don't eat it, and don't feed it to pets (Michigan relays CDC, T1-29). Wisconsin:
  landfill the carcass waste or packaged meat, soak equipment in 50/50 bleach, and a replacement tag arrives in
  2–4 days (T1-28). Michigan also replaces the licence (T1-29). Minnesota leaves the meat decision to the hunter
  but has processors handle a kept positive deer on its own (T1-30, B1-39).
- **Why people don't test, in their own words.** Testing is free in Wisconsin, yet about 1 in 19 deer gets
  tested (B1-22, T3-1); about 1 in 13 in Minnesota (B1-24). When North Carolina asked, hunters' top reasons for not
  testing were: CWD isn't in my area; I didn't know how to submit a sample; my deer looked healthy; I'm not worried
  about eating a deer that might have it (T3-5, SURVEYED, NC Wildlife Resources Commission 2022). Inside the
  surveillance zone, the number-one reason was not being worried about eating it (T3-6). Virginia: 93.5% of hunters
  said CWD hasn't changed how much deer meat they eat (T3-11, SURVEYED, Virginia DWR 2022, APHIS-funded). Money is
  a minority reason: in Kentucky 82.2% said the risk of paying to process a deer that tests positive was no barrier
  at all (T3-10), though 28.5% of those who wanted more deer named slow results (T3-9).
- **His question: "Is it because it's never had a problem before?" The sourced answer, with its limit.**
  The nearest measured thing: in Illinois, hunters who lived in CWD counties saw *less* risk to people and deer
  than hunters elsewhere, the opposite of what the researchers predicted (T3-18, SURVEYED, about 5,700 hunters,
  2012). Their suggested explanation, which they could not test: more than 98% of tested Illinois deer had come
  back negative, and hunters may take that, plus word of mouth, as evidence the risk is low (T3-19). **The limit,
  said out loud:** no study has measured whether hunters actually reason "it's never been a problem, so it won't
  be" (T3-39). And as a way of judging the risk, it is the reasoning the BSE Inquiry found wanting: 1989's
  reassurance leaned on 200 years of scrapie without a human case (T2-3). For CWD, no human case in over 50 years
  is real (B2-20), but zero is what today's small watched groups would show either way (B2-33), human incubation
  is likely 10 years or more (B2-21), and a first case might be misfiled (T2-32).
- **Fear did change behaviour once.** Wisconsin's gun-deer licence sales fell about one in ten the year CWD was
  found there, 2002 (T3-22, MEASURED), and a survey put about half of that year's dropouts down to CWD (T3-23).
  By an industry account, sales usually creep back (T3-25, APPROXIMATE: no figure on air).
- **Eat it before the result? Two state agencies, side by side. No pick from us.**

- **The question for the person given the meat: whose deer is in this?** The most-liked thread in the comments is
  a processor worker saying everyone's deer gets mixed (council card). The record: Wisconsin warns that sausage and

  (B1-37, T3-29); processors *may* choose to batch only not-detected deer (B1-37); Minnesota's agriculture
  department tells processors to hold the carcass or trim until results arrive, as guidance, not law (B1-39); CDC
  says ask for your deer to be processed individually (B1-33). So even a hunter who waited can be eating someone
  else's untested deer, and the person given the sausage has two questions to ask, not one. **Hold that.**

### Beat 4 — The risk nobody talks about (lead)
- **Follow one deer.** In a Wyoming study, 30 hunter-shot deer went to 30 different processors. Every
  carcass had metal fragments, a median of about 136 (C1, C5). The ground meat of 24 of the 30 deer had at
  least one fragment: about one package in three (74 of 234) (C2). 93% of the fragments analysed were lead
  (C3). [the X-ray at the food bank]
- **Why trimming doesn't solve it.** In Minnesota's sheep study, only about 30% of fragments sat within 2
  inches of the exit wound, and low lead turned up 18 inches away, the farthest point they sampled (C11,
  C12). The DNR won't name a safe trim distance (C12). Most fragments are too small to see or feel when
  chewing (C23, C41).
- **Ground is the lever.** Minnesota found lead in about a quarter of ground packages against 2% of whole
  cuts (C13, C14). Whole cuts aren't zero: loin steaks from 4 of 16 spine-shot deer had fragments (C7).
- **How much it matters.** North Dakota and CDC measured game eaters' blood lead about 0.3 µg/dL higher
  (C18), roughly a tenth of the level CDC uses to flag a child (3.5), and CDC says no safe level in
  children is known (C21). Nobody has linked venison lead to an illness (C13, C42), and no study has
  measured health outcomes in people who eat it either (C30): the same "absence isn't proof" rule we apply to
  CWD applies here. ATSDR called the hazard "indeterminate" (C29). ⚠ "A tenth of 3.5" is an absolute
  comparison; as a share of the average, 0.3 µg/dL is a sizeable relative gap (factchecker seat; our
  arithmetic, not for air without a row).
- **Kids and pregnancy: the third person's path.** North Dakota, Minnesota's health department and Michigan's
  MDHHS say children under 6 (Michigan: 6 and under) and pregnant women (Michigan: women of childbearing age)
  should not eat lead-shot venison (C22, C24, C26); New York's October 2025 notice says pregnant people and
  children should choose other meats (C27). It is current advice, not a 2008 relic. Wisconsin's page is the
  mildest (C25). [the clinic window]
- **The fix that works.** Copper and other non-exposed-core bullets left about 9 fragments against 82–141
  for lead types (C9). Studies across deer species, bear and boar find copper kills about as well (C32–C34).
  **Hold that.**

### Beat 5 — The everyday ones
- **Undercooking.** The federal chart puts venison, wild or farmed, at **160 °F** (D11); CDC says the 145 °F
  whole-cut rule excludes wild game (D12). Two real outbreaks: at a 2017 Wisconsin retreat, 9 of 11 who ate
  deliberately undercooked venison got toxoplasmosis (D2); of six sick Quebec hunters, five ate rare steak,
  and the two who ate it well done stayed well (D3). Outbreaks are rare (D49). [diner thermometer]
- **PFAS: about place and organ, not "wild deer".** Local advisories at Michigan's Clark's Marsh, two
  Wisconsin sites and four Maine areas (D13–D19). Mostly the **liver**: at Marinette, PFOS was in the liver
  of all 20 deer and the muscle of 1 (D15). Wisconsin tested livers from 32 counties and found PFAS in one
  (D18). Michigan says skip deer liver and kidneys statewide (D14). Maine is the outlier: no part is safe in
  its four areas and cooking doesn't help (D19). [the marsh / river edge]
- **EHD and screwworm, in the agencies' own words.** EHD: "no risk from handling or eating meat" from
  infected animals (North Dakota, D23); Michigan: venison from EHD areas "is safe for consumption", but
  don't eat a visibly sick animal (D24). Screwworm is a fly that lays eggs in wounds (D28); USDA's "not a
  food safety issue" is about inspected commercial meat (D27), and Texas tells hunters not to eat an
  infested animal (D29). **Hold that.**

### Beat 6 — Is it really free meat?
- One Maine hunter's buck: about 51 lb of boneless meat for a $155 processing fee, which he figured at
  about $3 a pound (D30, his figure) against $6.79 for ground beef (D33). (⚠ D30 is an APPROXIMATE row:
  the approximation is the dossier's licence-added $3.5–4.0/lb, which stays off air. The writer's own
  "roughly $3 per pound" is a verbatim quote and may be quoted as his, once, as one newspaper deer.)
- **Then the joke, told honestly (ruled: keep it).** The average US big-game hunter spent **$1,352** on trips and
  gear in 2022 (USFWS, D36). Spread that year over one average deer's 52 lb (D38) and it's about **$26 a pound**,
  nearly twice USDA Choice sirloin at $14.11 (D34). Say it as what it is: our arithmetic, "about", "if that's your
  one deer"; it's per hunter, not per deer; some hunters get several deer and many get none; and "big game"
  includes elk and bear (D36). [bank's number board]
- **The honest flip:** it is cheap meat *if* you'd hunt anyway. It isn't free. And for the person given the
  package, it's a gift that cost someone about that much. **Hold that.**

## Settled (not the debate)

### Nutrition (hand pass A)
- Lean venison cuts carry about 2–3 g of fat per 3 oz cooked; chicken breast 3.0 g; pork tenderloin 3.0 g;
  lean sirloin 4.9 g (A1–A5). Pork tenderloin has fewer calories than every deer cut (A5).
- Raw like for like, venison and chicken breast differ by 0.2 g of fat per 100 g, a tie; lean bison and
  lean grass-fed beef are as lean as venison (A6, A23).
- Iron: cooked deer cuts 4.09–5.01 mg/100 g vs sirloin 1.96, chicken breast 1.04, pork tenderloin 1.15 (A13).
- Zinc: beef is usually a bit higher than lean venison steaks (about 1.2–1.6x); braised venison shoulder
  beats beef (A14).
- B12: measured venison cuts carry 1.83–3.62 µg/100 g cooked, the same range as beef (A16).
- Cholesterol: cooked lean venison 79–88 mg/100 g vs cooked chicken breast, sirloin and pork tenderloin 73–85;
  the generic roasted and braised-shoulder entries 112–113 (A8). ⚠ Compare cooked with cooked: the raw beef
  rows (68–71) are not comparators for cooked venison.
- Ground venison (USDA, no stated added fat) has 7.13 g fat/100 g raw vs 20.0 for 80/20 beef (A9); about
  half the fat of USDA's generic raw ground beef (A11).
- Omega-6 : omega-3 ratio of about 2.3–2.6 in wild elk, mule deer and antelope (Cordain, A21) and farmed
  fallow deer (A25), like grass-fed beef (about 2:1) and unlike grain-fed (5:1 to 13:1) (A22, A23).
  EPA and DHA are a trace next to salmon's 1.44 g/100 g (A24, A26).
- Processed venison: USDA's venison jerky recipe entry carries 2,955 mg sodium/100 g, about 58 times raw
  deer's 51 mg; one 14 g strip is about 18% of a day's sodium (A30, our arithmetic). Its venison sausage
  entry has 25 g fat/100 g (A30).
- No venison-specific health-outcome study exists (one PubMed search, 110 records, A29). IARC classes red
  meat as "probably carcinogenic" (2A) on limited evidence, counts all mammalian muscle meat, and doesn't
  name deer (A28).
- Beef scale: 59.1 lb per person in 2024, retail weight (A36, D40).

### CWD (hand passes B1, B2)
- 37 US states and five Canadian provinces, wild and captive herds together (USGS, June 2026; the
  wild-only file alone also gives 37) (B1-1, B1-3). Michigan: 18 counties (B1-3, B1-16).
- Timeline: 1967 (Colorado research mule deer), 1981 (first wild case, Colorado elk), 2001 (South Dakota
  whitetails), 2002 (Wisconsin) (B1-5).
- Wyoming 2025: 21.6% of adult male mule deer, 32.1% of adult male whitetails, 2.4% of adult elk; CWD in
  35 of 37 mule deer herds (B1-8, B1-9).
- Wisconsin CWD year 2025, submitted deer: Richland 35.7%, Sauk 33.0%, Lafayette 31.0%, Columbia 27.4%,
  Iowa 23.5%, Dane 19.3% (B1-10). Saskatchewan: CWD in 66 of 83 zones (B1-11).
- Most CWD-positive harvested deer show no outward signs; lab testing is the only way to tell (B1-29,
  B1-31, T1-33).
- Cooking and freezing don't remove prions (B2-35, B2-36, B1-32); grilled and boiled elk meat kept prion
  seeding (B2-37); prions occur in the muscle of late-stage deer (B1-32), and infectious prions have been found
  in the muscle of infected deer, including deer with no clinical signs (T1-27).
- No human CWD case has ever been reported (B2-20, B2-22, B2-27).
- Every agency in the record says don't eat a positive animal: CDC (B2-19), Health Canada (B2-22), Sweden
  (B2-27), Wisconsin DHS (B1-32); Michigan relays CDC that pets shouldn't eat it either (T1-29).
- Testing is free in Wisconsin, Minnesota, and Michigan's five focus counties (plus free kits elsewhere in
  Michigan) (B1-22, B1-24, B1-27, B1-40).

### The CWD test (top-up T1)
- The hunter test is run on lymph nodes from the head and neck, not on meat; results are "detected" or "not
  detected" (T1-1, T1-2). Labs screen with ELISA and confirm positives with IHC (T1-3); Minnesota confirms only
  when the screen is positive (T1-4). RT-QuIC is not USDA-approved for deer (T1-9).
- Michigan's own 1,300 nodes: ELISA 184, RT-QuIC 178, IHC 176 positive; 7 disagreements (0.5%), all false alarms
  (T1-10).
- The test is weaker early in infection (T1-11, T1-13, T1-14, T1-16, T1-18); no measured miss rate exists for
  naturally infected wild deer in their first weeks (T1-19). A Bayesian estimate of about 91–92% sensitivity rests
  on 61 wild deer with wide uncertainty (T1-12): screen only, never "misses 1 in 12".
- No agency calls a negative a clean bill of health (B1-26, T1-22, T1-23).
- After a positive: Wisconsin (T1-28), Michigan (T1-29) and Minnesota (T1-30) steps are sourced; what to do about
  meat already eaten or shared is not (T1-31).

### The mad-cow precedent (top-up T2)
- UK officials told the public for ten years: "no evidence", "most unlikely", "safe to eat beef" (T2-1); "no
  risk" (CMO, TV, 1990, T2-7); "no conceivable risk" (Health Secretary, December 1995, T2-8). The Inquiry: the
  government did not lie; the reassurance campaign was a mistake (T2-9).
- vCJD: announced 20 March 1996 (T2-15); 178 UK cases to end-2023, all dead, last reported 2016 (T2-16); 233
  worldwide 1996–2023 (T2-17); median age at death 28 (T2-18).
- Infection was far more common than disease: about 1 in 2,000 archived UK appendixes carried abnormal prion
  protein (T2-21). About half a million infected cattle entered the food chain before the 1989 offal ban (T2-23).
- BSE fed to macaques: 18 of 18 infected at 5 g (T2-26); CWD at a similar oral dose: no classical disease at
  5–13 years (T2-27).
- BSE infectivity in cattle sat in brain and spinal cord; CWD prions are in deer muscle (T2-29).

### Why people take the risk (top-up T3)
- Testing uptake: about 5% of Wisconsin's harvest, a little over 7% of Minnesota's (T3-1, T3-2); free in both.
- Hunters' stated reasons for not testing (NC 2022): not in my area, didn't know how, looked healthy, not worried
  about eating it (T3-5, T3-6).
- Virginia 2022: 93.5% say CWD hasn't changed how much deer meat they eat; 7.8% had a deer tested in five years
  (T3-11, T3-3).
- Illinois 2012: hunters living with CWD perceived less risk (T3-18); the authors' explanation is a suggestion
  (T3-19).
- Venison is shared: 85% of successful Michigan hunters share, 69% within the household (T3-31).
- No study measures optimism bias or "never been a problem" reasoning (T3-39), or how much families depend on
  venison as food (T3-41).

### Lead (hand pass C)
- Wyoming, 30 deer: fragments in every carcass; ground meat of 24 deer (80%) and 74 of 234 packages (32%);
  93% of analysed fragments lead (C1–C3).
- Minnesota sheep study: ballistic tip 141 fragments, soft point 86, bonded lead core 82, slug 28, copper
  and non-exposed lead core 9 (C9); only ~30% within 2 in of the exit; lead found at 18 in, the farthest
  sampling point (C11, C12).
- Minnesota food-shelf screening: lead in 26% of 1,029 commercially ground packages vs 2% of 209 whole-cut
  packages (C13).
- North Dakota/CDC blood lead: geometric means 1.27 vs 0.84 µg/dL; adjusted gap 0.30 (95% CI 0.16–0.44)
  (C18). CDC's child reference value is 3.5 µg/dL, not a safety threshold; no safe level identified (C21).
- Health advice for children and pregnancy: North Dakota (C22), Minnesota MDH (C24), Michigan MDHHS (C26),
  New York (Oct 2025, C27), CDC on breastfeeding (C28); Wisconsin: non-lead is "the best way" (C25).
- Minnesota Agriculture discards about 10% of donated venison after X-ray screening (six-year average to
  2022, a news quote of the official, C38).

### Other risks, cost, scale (hand pass D)
- Toxoplasma antibodies in 36% of 914 US whitetails; live parasite from deer hearts in 11 states (D1).
- Venison: 160 °F on the federal chart (D11); CDC excludes wild game from the 145 °F whole-cut rule (D12).
- Freezing helps against Toxoplasma (D4) but doesn't reliably kill northern Trichinella (D9); deer were 2
  of 84 US trichinellosis cases in 2008–2012, bear 41 (D9).
- PFAS: Clark's Marsh 2018 (one deer of 20, 547 ppb PFOS in muscle, D13); Marinette 2020 (liver 20/20,
  muscle 1/20, D15); Stella 2025 (one meal a month of muscle, D16); Maine (Do Not Eat any part, four areas,
  D19); Michigan statewide: don't eat deer liver or kidneys (D14).
- EHD does not infect people (D23, D24). Screwworm is wound-borne (D28).
- National harvest 2024–25: 6,194,659 deer (National Deer Association, a hunting group, D37). Ohio's
  measured yield: 52.19 lb of venison per deer (D38). 11.5 million big-game hunters, about 1 in 23 adults
  (D41).
- Wisconsin's deer donation program: over 100,000 deer and over 4 million lb of ground venison since 2000
  (B1-34, D45).

## Where the disagreement starts

### 1. Can CWD reach people? Say what each study measured, never "can" or "cannot".

| study | what it measured | what it found | row |
|---|---|---|---|
| Race et al. 2014, 2018 (NIH Rocky Mountain Labs) | macaques dosed by mouth (4 g of brain in total) or into the brain; seven examined 11–13 years later | no clinical, pathological or biochemical evidence of transmission; squirrel monkeys, a more distant species, were susceptible (the 2014 counts are APPROXIMATE, B2-14: off air) | B2-14, B2-15, T2-27 |
| Hannaoui et al. 2026 (Calgary), *Science Advances* | 18 macaques dosed; a subset examined years later (seven or eight: the paper says both, B2-2); standard tests plus ultra-sensitive amplification | standard tests negative; faint seeding in 6 of 7 brains tested; full disease only in rodents several passages from one brain-implanted monkey; "limited transmission"; the jump "cannot be excluded" | B2-1 to B2-10, T2-28 |
| Transgenic mice, organoids | mice carrying human prion protein; human brain organoids | not susceptible; no propagation at 180 days | B2-18 |
| Benavente et al. 2025 (Texas, in CDC's journal) | one positive elk, grilled and boiled | seeding survived cooking; no conversion of human prion protein in a single-round test | B2-37, B2-38 |
| CDC hunter cohorts (preliminary, via the National Deer Association) | prion deaths among hunters in Colorado, Wyoming, Wisconsin | nothing above expected; Wisconsin's group too small to expect any case | B2-32, B2-33 |
| Oneida County, NY, 2005 feast | dozens of people exposed to a positive deer (81 at baseline, 29 known to have eaten it: APPROXIMATE, B2-34, the count stays off air) | no prion disease reported to date | B2-34 |
| *For contrast:* Holznagel et al. 2013, BSE fed to macaques | 5 g of BSE cattle brain by mouth | 18 of 18 infected (counting infection at any stage); illness after a median 4.6 years | T2-26 |

**What the agencies conclude from the same record:** CDC: not known whether people can be infected (B2-20).
Norway's VKM: eating risk "seems to be very low" (B2-25). Sweden: zoonotic potential uncertain, especially
for Nordic variants (B2-27). EFSA: the risk "cannot be directly assessed", and eaters of infected meat
"will be exposed" (B2-24). Canada's public-health agency review: no evidence of transmission, but it "cannot be
entirely ruled out" (T2-34). The hunting side does not claim zero either: the National Deer Association
writes the risk is "low, but it's not zero" (B2-41).

**Where the record lands (no false balance):** no human case in more than 50 years of the disease; one
recent primate study finds a faint, contested signal that the earlier, longer primate studies did not; the
surveillance that would catch a rare, slow human spillover is admittedly thin (B2-21, T2-32). The mad-cow
precedent cuts both ways: CWD has been tested directly in primates where BSE was not before it crossed, but deer
prions sit in the meat and come in several strains (T2-26 to T2-30). That is exactly why every agency says test
where CWD is and never eat a positive. It is not "CWD can infect people", and it is not "CWD can't": it is "not
proven yet".

### 2. What does a "not detected" test promise?
- **What's measured:** close agreement between the tests on the deer they can see (T1-10, T1-11); lower
  sensitivity early in infection (T1-13, T1-14, T1-16); a model estimate of about 91–92% sensitivity from a small
  sample (T1-12).
- **Where it splits:** the strongest reassurance on record is a public-health scientist's opinion that "not
  detected" makes prions "highly unlikely", in the same testimony that predicts human cases (T1-26: pair the two
  if either is used); the agencies settle on "less likely", "not a food safety test" (T1-22, T1-23, B1-26).

### 3. How much does fragment lead matter?
- **What's measured:** fragments in the meat (C1–C3, C13), a small blood-lead gap in game eaters (C18), a
  bigger rise in four pigs fed fragment venison (C6, pigs, not people), and later studies abroad that mostly
  find small or no differences, with no health outcome measured (C30).
- **Where it splits:** health agencies advise caution for under-6s and pregnancy because no safe level is
  known (C21–C27); the gun industry's 2008 critique called the 0.3 gap "insignificant" (wrong: the
  confidence interval excludes zero) and cited a superseded CDC threshold of 10 (C31). The honest middle:
  the gap is real, small on average, and nobody knows how much fragment lead a body absorbs (C42).

## Both quietly agree

**The hunting side and the health agencies give the same practical advice on CWD.** CDC, Health Canada,
Sweden and Wisconsin's health department: test where CWD is present and never eat a positive (B2-19,
B2-22, B2-27, B1-32). The hunters' own National Deer Association, which pushes back hard on alarm ("this
sketchy report"), still writes "The risk is obviously low, but it's not zero" (B2-41). Neither side says
zero; neither says eat a positive. The real split is narrower than the headlines: whether to eat **before**
the result comes back (Minnesota DNR: hunter's choice; Wisconsin DHS: wait) (B1-26, B1-32).
⚠ The record does not hold a hunting group's own "test your deer" instruction in its words; the NDA line is
about risk, not about testing. (The Rocky Mountain Elk Foundation page in T1-22 is a reprint of the Wisconsin
DNR's words, not RMEF's own advice.) If the script wants "hunting groups say test", it needs a top-up row first.

**And on the test itself:** Minnesota's DNR, Wisconsin's DNR and Virginia's wildlife agency all say a negative
is not a food-safety certificate (B1-26, T1-22, T1-23). Even the agency that leaves the eat-before decision to
the hunter says so.

**A second, smaller one on lead:** Minnesota's DNR, which ran the fragment study, also prints in capitals
that lead in venison "HAS NOT BEEN LINKED TO ANY ILLNESSES" (C13), and North Dakota itself said wild game is
not the most important lead risk (C20). The agencies that found the fragments are not claiming harm they
haven't measured.
