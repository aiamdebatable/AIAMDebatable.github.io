Open questions this episode could not settle

  • How often a "not detected" CWD test misses a deer infected only weeks before the hunt: the test's accuracy is studied (T1-10 to T1-18) and every source says it is weaker early in infection, but no one has measured that miss rate in wild deer (T1-19). "Not detected" makes infection less likely; it is not a food-safety certificate (T1-22, T1-23).
  • What to do if venison was already eaten or given away before a result came back positive: no agency page read gives after-the-fact advice (T1-31).
  • Whether a human CWD case would be recognised quickly: probably not; it could be misfiled as ordinary CJD (T2-32).
  • Whether CWD can infect people at all, and at what rate: the primate studies disagree (B2-9 to B2-15), and the human surveillance may be too slow to see a rare spillover (B2-21).
  • How much lead a body absorbs from fragments, and whether venison lead has ever caused harm (C42).
  • How many families eat their deer before the CWD result arrives (the only figure is one small social-media sample, T3-4), how many hunters eat a deer that tested positive (27–28% vs "most", T3-14, T3-15), and how much household venison is ground, sausage or jerky. None is properly measured.
  • Why hunters skip the test or eat early, beyond what they say in surveys: no study measures optimism bias or "it's never been a problem" reasoning (T3-39), and no study measures how much families depend on venison as food (T3-41).
  • Whether wild venison could ever be called "organic", and whether "gamey" signals spoilage: no source.
  • Bovine TB in northeast Michigan deer as an eating risk: not researched.
  • The Lithuanian wild-vs-farmed red deer comparison (A27), the 2017 press numbers (B2-17), "WHO 1997" (B2-23), Norway's cull size (B2-26), the Maine liver figures (D21) and the pounds destroyed in the 2008 pantry recalls (C39): unresolved, not on air.
