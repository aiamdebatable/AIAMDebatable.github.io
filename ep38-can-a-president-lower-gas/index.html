Gas Prices: Why So High If America Makes Its Own Oil?

If America makes its own oil, why is gas over $4 a gallon, and what can the government actually do about it?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in October 2026, regular gas averaged $4.35 a gallon, $1.23 more than a year earlier, after fighting effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz. On October 5 the President signed an order on dyed diesel, the untaxed fuel that farms and job sites use off the road.

Settled

What both sides accept

America makes more oil than it uses, but sells it, and the fuel, at the world price. About half your gallon is the oil, and the tax is a set number of cents. In 2026, the refinery's slice was about double a normal year.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesPull the levers

Doing nothing costs about $570 a year for a typical car, and households under $40,000 cut their gas use by about 7%. In a real shortage, release the reserve and pause the tax where the pause reaches drivers, as Georgia's reached them at 64% in 2026. And keep a light on the refinery's slice, which about doubled.

Gold arguesKeep the cushion

The levers built for the pump are worth cents, and each comes with a bill. The reserve is at its lowest since 1982, and a federal tax holiday is Congress's call and would come out of road money. Keep the cushion for a shortage that empties the pumps, and tell drivers the truth: the price is the world's.

What the shouting gets wrong

Two common claims fell: that it's our oil so it should be cheap, and that a president can't do anything. The levers are real, they're just measured in cents, and the biggest promise, a federal gas-tax holiday, was never the President's to keep. Judge any promise with four questions: which slice of the gallon, by when, paid for with what, and who actually holds that lever.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: Which levers would you pull, and what would you pay?

The judge named these levers and declared no winner. You could pull more than one. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Pull nothing
    What it buys
    No bill comes due: the reserve stays a cushion for a real shortage, and no road money is spent.
    What it costs
    About $570 a car this year, and lower-income drivers pay it in gas they go without: households under $40,000 cut how much gas they used by about 7%.

    On the record: row 13.2 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 13.7 of the fact-check log, row 4.17 of the fact-check log, row 4.18 of the fact-check log

  2. 2Release the reserve
    What it buys
    A few cents to a few tens of cents. Two past releases moved crude about $2 a barrel, roughly 5 cents a gallon. The big exception was Libya in 2011: up to about 30 cents. A president holds this lever.
    What it costs
    It comes from a cushion at its lowest since 1982, and the barrels lent out this year have to come back. Government auditors found the reserve can pump out only about 60% of what it was designed to.
    Not measured
    Nobody has measured what this year's release of 172 million barrels did at the pump.

    On the record: row 6.11 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 6.12 of the fact-check log, row 4.18 of the fact-check log, row 6.25 of the fact-check log, row 5.6 of the fact-check log

  3. 3Pause the tax
    What it buys
    Through your state, in days: Georgia's pause reached drivers at 64% this year, in the same shock. Through Congress, up to 18 cents a gallon, nearer 12 at the pass-through that's been measured.
    What it costs
    Paid from road money. The federal tax holiday is Congress's, and it has never passed. And how much of a state's pause reaches you varies: in 2022, Maryland's reached drivers at 83%, New York's at about zero.
    Not measured
    A federal pause has never happened, so what it would do is a model, not a measurement.

    On the record: row 7.6 of the fact-check log, row 7.1 of the fact-check log, row 7.8 of the fact-check log, row 2.7 of the fact-check log, row 16.41 of the fact-check log

  4. 4Loosen the fuel and dye rules
    What it buys
    At most 24 cents on a gallon of dyed diesel, the untaxed red fuel farms and job sites use, about 4% of a $6.20 gallon. The IRS dropped its own federal penalty for road use on 14 October.
    What it costs
    The diesel order asks to postpone the federal tax, not cancel it, on dyed fuel only, and the states keep theirs. A trucker who crosses a state line can fall back under a ban. The owner-operator truckers' group called it minimal relief.
    Not measured
    Nobody has measured what loosening the fuel rules does at the pump.

    On the record: row 16.33 of the fact-check log, row 16.4 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 17.33 of the fact-check log, row 16.30 of the fact-check log, row 8.3 of the fact-check log

  5. 5Lean on the refiners
    What it buys
    A light on the slice that changed most: this year the refinery's slice is about double a normal year. Federal enforcers urged state attorneys general to investigate, so your state's attorney general and watchdog are levers that exist.
    What it costs
    Worth nothing measured so far. Nobody has found wrongdoing, and per barrel this spring's margins were below 2022's. Last year was a weak one, so double starts from a low base.
    Not measured
    Whether any of the doubled slice is wrongdoing: there's no finding either way so far, and nobody has finished looking.

    On the record: row 15.8 of the fact-check log, row 17.19 of the fact-check log, row 17.4 of the fact-check log, row 17.6 of the fact-check log, row 17.18 of the fact-check log

  6. 6Keep fuel at home
    What it buys
    The promise is cheaper fuel at home, but the record found no modelled benefit. A diesel export limit was talked about in September and two House bills were filed; neither has passed.
    What it costs
    It's Congress's call. Industry analysts who modelled a fuel export ban found refiners would cut output and America would end up importing gasoline. And keeping our oil at home would mostly widen the refiner's margin.
    Not measured
    We found no government model of what a fuel export ban would do at the pump.

    On the record: row 15.29 of the fact-check log, row 15.30 of the fact-check log, row 15.32 of the fact-check log, row 15.27 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pull: We sell our oil, and the fuel, at the world price. Every lever has a holder, a worth and a bill, and the levers are real, they're just measured in cents.
