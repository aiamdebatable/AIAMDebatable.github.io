## The research · It's our oil, so why the world price?
**Ryan:** Now the question every driver asks at the pump.
**Mara:** It's our oil, and we don't depend on the Middle East, so why does a war over there set my price?
**Ryan:** Half of that is true. In twenty twenty, America began selling more oil and fuel abroad than it buys, for the first time since at least nineteen forty-nine. This May set a record. And very little of our oil comes from the Persian Gulf: about eight percent of crude imports last year, and about two percent by June.
**Mara:** Wait, we still import oil?
**Ryan:** Still more crude than we export. Most of it comes from Canada, about two-thirds this year. Many American refineries were built for heavier crude than American wells produce, so we sell light oil abroad and buy heavier oil from next door.
**Mara:** Fine, but it's still ours, so why does it cost the world price?
**Ryan:** Because it can be sold to the world. A producer can ship crude abroad, and a refiner can ship gasoline and diesel abroad, so they charge Americans about what a buyer overseas would pay. The government's statisticians call gasoline a globally traded commodity, and they found the world price drives it, even in the Midwest.
**Mara:** Has anyone actually tested that?
**Ryan:** History did. From twenty eleven to twenty thirteen, American crude was much cheaper than the world's. The pump followed the world price anyway.
**Mara:** So what did the war actually do?
**Ryan:** Military action involving Iran began on the twenty-eighth of February. The Strait of Hormuz, which carries nearly a fifth of the world's oil, was effectively closed. World crude went from about seventy-one dollars a barrel to a hundred and thirty-eight by early April. A June agreement took it back near seventy, and renewed attacks pushed it over a hundred again.
**Mara:** Did our crude go up as much?
**Ryan:** Less. The statisticians credit high American stockpiles and government action here. But fuel sells at the world price, so the pump climbed anyway.
**Mara:** Couldn't we just keep our oil at home?
**Ryan:** America tried that. It banned most crude exports until twenty fifteen. When the government's statisticians modelled lifting the ban, they found gasoline would be unchanged or slightly cheaper, because the pump follows the world price. Keep crude at home, and American crude gets cheaper, but the gain goes mainly to refiners, not to you.
**Mara:** And what about keeping our diesel at home?
**Ryan:** A diesel export limit was talked about in September, and two House bills were filed. Neither has passed. We found no government model of a fuel export ban. Industry analysts who modelled one found refiners would cut output, and America would end up importing gasoline.
**Mara:** So we make our own oil, and we still pay the world price. Hold that.
