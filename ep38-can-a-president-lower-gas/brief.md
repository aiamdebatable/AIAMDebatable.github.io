## The pitch, in one line

**America makes more oil than it uses, but it sells oil and fuel at the world price, so a war that shut
the world's busiest oil strait reaches your pump anyway.** Your $4.35 is about half the barrel, about a
third the refinery (twice its normal slice this year), and 52 cents of tax. Against that, the levers a
president, Congress or your state can pull are worth cents, and the one the President pulled this week
runs straight into state law.

## The viewer's moment, and the question in his words

You pull in on a Sunday in October. The sign says $4.35. Fifteen gallons is about **$65**; the same fill a
year ago was about **$47**, so **about $18 more** (log 13.10; 15 gallons is our example, not a statistic).
And the thought every driver has (Lucas's pitch, nearly verbatim):

> **"It's our oil. If US oil is about $90 a barrel and we don't depend on the Middle East, why am I paying
> over $4 a gallon, and what can the government actually do about it?"**

The episode answers every clause of that, from the record:
1. **"US oil is about $90."** Right on one measure, a bit low on the other: **about $90 to $100 a
   barrel** this fall (below).
2. **"We don't depend on the Middle East."** True for the barrels, false for the price (below).
3. **"Why over $4?"** Because a gallon has four slices, the biggest two are priced on a world market, and
   one of them, the refinery's, is about double its normal size this year (below).
4. **"What can the government do?"** Less than the speeches say, and the lever often sits with someone
   other than the person promising to pull it (the drawer).
5. **"Is it all just show?"** Answered from the record, lever by lever: who holds it, what it's worth,
   and whether it actually happened (below).

## Why now

**Hot peg: the dyed-diesel order (5 October 2026).** On **2026-10-05 the President signed an executive
order, "Emergency Tax Relief on Diesel Fuel"** ([text](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/10/emergency-tax-relief-on-diesel-fuel/);
log 16.2). What it actually does, in plain words (log 16.4–16.9):
- **It does not remove any tax.** It tells Treasury to **decide whether** it may **defer** (postpone, not
  cancel) the 24.4-cent federal tax owed when **dyed** diesel, the untaxed "off-road" fuel that farms,
  job sites and furnaces use, is burned on the road, for fuel used **5 October to 31 December 2026**. The
  legal hook is a disaster-and-military-action provision (26 U.S.C. 7508A), so Treasury must also find a
  "qualifying event". Cancelling the deferred tax is something Treasury is told only to "explore,
  including legislation" (log 16.5, 16.8).
- **It has the IRS announce that it won't impose the federal dyed-fuel penalty** ($1,000 or $10 a gallon,
  whichever is greater) for road use in that window (log 16.6, 16.10).
- **As of 8 October neither step had happened.** Treasury's determination and the IRS announcement were
  due within five days (about 10 October); neither was posted (log 16.43).
- **Update 14 October:** the IRS dropped its federal penalty, retroactive to 1 October and through 31 December; the
  postponement of the tax itself awaits further IRS guidance (IR-2026-122, log 17.33). The script line says so ("On the fourteenth of October, it did"; the dates are on screen).
- **Ordinary clear diesel still pays its 24.4 cents at the terminal.** The order changes no EPA rule;
  EPA's only diesel waiver that week was for Texas (high-sulfur diesel in older equipment, at the state's
  request), not a national dye waiver (log 16.12).
- **And it cannot touch state law.** States set their own diesel taxes and their own dyed-fuel bans.
  South Dakota's revenue department, 8 October: "the state cannot waive taxes set in state law", and
  outside farm use dyed fuel still owes state and municipal sales tax (log 16.19). Iowa, Kentucky and
  North Carolina eased their **own** penalties by their **own** orders, mostly for farm loads (log 16.24–16.26).
- **Worth:** at most **24.4 cents of a $6.20 diesel gallon, about 4%**, deferred, and only for buyers of
  dyed fuel in states that also allow it (log 16.32, 16.33). The owner-operator truckers' group: "minimal
  relief" (log 16.37). No one has estimated an effect on shelf prices (log 16.39).

## The answer, in plain words (the new spine)

### 1. "US oil is about $90." About $90 to $100, depending on which price you read.

- **On air: "US oil has been selling for about $90 to $100 a barrel this fall."** True on either measure
  (log 17.30). ⛔ Never one live number ("US oil is $91", "$97").
- **On screen: one dated EIA spot figure, with its name:** "Cushing WTI spot, EIA: $96.24 (6 Oct 2026)",
  or the September average, $97.31 (log 17.28, 15.1). EIA spot ran $96.13–99.77 a day from 29 September
  to 6 October. If a futures figure is ever shown, label it "futures" and date it.
- **Lucas's $91 (oilprice.com, 8 Oct) is the front-month futures price**, from unofficial sources: $91.05
  on 8 Oct, $88.28–92.87 from 29 Sep (log 17.27). EIA no longer publishes daily futures (log 17.29). So
  "$91 now vs ~$97 in September" is partly a real fall (futures were $105.83 on 15 Sep) and partly two
  different prices: since 28 September EIA's spot has run about $6.80 above futures every day, and nothing
  in the record explains why (log 17.30). ⛔ **Explain nothing about the spot–futures gap on air.**
- ⛔ **Name the benchmark every time:** "US crude, WTI". "$90" also fits March ($91.38) or the climb
  through August ($83.90) on the spot measure (log 15.1).
- **The world benchmark is higher.** Brent spot averaged **$114.16 in September**, about $17 above WTI;
  on 6 October it was $125.44 against WTI's $96.24 (log 15.2). In 2025 the gap was only $2–5. ⚠ **Spot vs
  futures:** Brent futures (about $102 in September) show a much smaller gap (about $6). Air one, and say
  which (log 15.2, 4.14).
- **Why US crude rose less:** EIA (April): Brent "is more exposed to global crude oil market conditions";
  WTI rose less "because of above-average domestic inventories and policy interventions in the United

### 3. ★ Margin, profit, and blame

**This revises the earlier brief twice:** first E1 gave the "it's the oil companies" camp margin data; now
hand pass F (Ruling 8) gives it the refiners' own earnings and the record of who has looked. Told with the
record, not as an accusation.

**In one breath (on air):** refiners' slice is about twice its usual size this year; their own filings
show spring profits several times last year's but below 2022's; the companies and EIA blame a war that
cut world fuel supply and left inventories low; Washington says it's watching and California is checking
some stations; nobody has found wrongdoing.

#### 3a. The slice: the refinery's margin over crude is about double normal this year
- **The refiner's gasoline margin over US crude** (Gulf Coast gasoline minus WTI, our arithmetic on EIA's
  cells): **2015–2025 annual averages ran from 19 cents (2020) to 69 cents (2022); 2025 was 46 cents. In
  2026 (January–September) it averaged 97 cents, and in September $1.42** (log 15.8). About double a normal
  year, and in September about triple.
- **Diesel's margin is higher still:** $2.57 a gallon over WTI in September; retail diesel set a weekly
  record of $6.529 on 21 September, above 2022's $5.81 (log 15.9).
- **EIA confirms it pushed September up:** "The increase in retail prices was driven by higher crude oil
  prices in addition to rising crack spreads", and expects the gasoline margin to "remain above seasonal
  averages through the beginning of 2027 due to low U.S. inventories" (log 15.10).

#### 3b. The profit: what refiners actually earned (their own SEC filings)

⚠ **A company's "refining margin" is not our crack spread.** Our $1.31 is one product (Gulf Coast
gasoline) at one hub minus WTI. A company's margin averages every barrel it makes (gasoline, diesel, jet,
asphalt, coke) at the crude it actually bought, and each company defines it differently (log 17.8).
Valero's $23.62 a barrel is about 56 cents a gallon, not $1.31. ⛔ **Compare each company only with
itself over time; never one company with another, never a company margin with the crack.** Companies are
named because they are the authors of their own filings.

- **Spring 2026 (Q2, April–June) margins per barrel were about double spring 2025** (log 17.1):

  | company (own definition) | Q2 2026 | Q2 2025 | Q2 2022 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Marathon Petroleum, R&M margin | $36.33 | $17.58 | $37.54 |
  | Valero, refining margin per barrel of throughput | $23.62 | $12.35 | $30.01 |
  | Phillips 66, realized margin | $24.08 | $11.25 | $28.31 |
  | PBF, gross refining margin excl. special items | $23.40 | $8.38 | $30.41 |

  Ratios 1.57x–2.79x across five filers (HF Sinclair +57%) (our arithmetic). On air: "about twice".
- **Profit grew faster than the margin,** because running costs barely moved: Valero's operating profit
  per barrel $16.56 vs $4.78, **about 3.5x**; Marathon's segment EBITDA per barrel $24.84 vs $6.79, about
  3.7x (log 17.2). Per gallon at Valero: about 39 cents of operating profit in spring 2026, 11 cents in
  spring 2025, 54 cents in spring 2022 (log 17.11, APPROXIMATE, our arithmetic; all products).
- **Company-wide, the four (Marathon, Valero, Phillips 66, PBF) made about $13.6 billion of net income
  in Q2 2026,** vs about $2.8 billion in Q2 2025 and about **$14.9 billion in Q2 2022** (log 17.3,
  APPROXIMATE: our sums of confirmed cells; Marathon and Phillips 66 include pipelines and chemicals, PBF
  2026 includes fire-insurance gains). Air rounded, labelled as our sum.
- ⛔ **Not a record.** Per barrel, spring 2026 margins were 77–97% of spring 2022's; Valero's per-barrel
  profit $16.56 vs $22.71 (log 17.4). The four's profit was about 90% of Q2 2022.
- **Q1 2026 (January–March) was ordinary;** the jump came in spring (log 17.5). **2025 was a weak year**
  (Valero and PBF lost money in Q1 2025), so "double 2025" starts from a low base (log 17.6).
- **Q3 (July–September, when September's crack peaked) is not filed yet;** releases normally come late
  October (log 17.7). Re-check before publish if they land.
- **A margin is not a profit:** out of it come running the plant, shipping the fuel, maintenance
  shutdowns, equipment wear, then interest and tax (log 17.9), and renewable-fuel credits, whose cost
  more than doubled by Valero's market reference ($13.78 vs $6.14 a barrel; log 17.10).

#### 3c. Why the margin is wide, by who says it

1. **The companies: the war cut world fuel supply.** Refineries and export terminals damaged, shipping
   disrupted, so inventories are low (log 17.12). Attribute it: "the companies say".
2. **EIA: low inventories, especially diesel.** East Coast diesel inventories were **32% below** their
   five-year seasonal average in September; EIA expects the gasoline margin to stay above normal into
   early 2027 "due to low U.S. inventories" (log 17.13, 15.10). Crack spreads roughly doubled (log 17.14).
3. **EIA: less US refining.** About 400,000 b/d closed in 2025 (Houston, Los Angeles) and Benicia in early
   2026; the effect is largest on the **West Coast** (log 17.15).
4. **Exports at record highs** pulled US fuel abroad (EIA cells; California's watchdog); the size of the
   effect on margins is not established (log 17.16, 15.19).
5. **US refiners buy US crude and sell fuel at the world price.** When US crude is cheaper than world
   crude, the pump doesn't fall to match; the difference shows up in the refiner's margin. EIA (2015): "a
   wider Brent-WTI spread will provide higher margins as refined product prices continue to move with
   global crude prices" (log 15.27). In September, about 40 cents of the $1.42 was that discount
   (margin over Brent $1.01); this week about 55 cents ($1.31 over WTI vs about 75 cents over Brent) (log
   15.11). ⚠ **In the companies' own books the discount is smaller:** Valero's reference puts Brent
   futures only **about $4 a barrel** over WTI in Q2 2026 ($3.85), though the physical world barrel
   (Dated Brent) ran about $8 over Brent futures (log 17.17). The mechanism is real; its size in company
   profits is not established.
6. **Fuel itself is scarce worldwide** (the same story as 1–3, in the market cells). ⛔ Even measured
   against the world crude price, the margin is about **double 2025's** (75 cents this week and $1.01 in
   September, against a 2025 average near 37 cents; our arithmetic). Hand pass E1's summary called that
   "ordinary"; its own rows show otherwise, so it is corrected (log 15.11).
7. **Wrongdoing (collusion, withheld supply):** no official finding exists either way (log 17.18).

### 4. "We don't depend on the Middle East." True for the barrels, not for the price.

What's true:
- **The US has been a net petroleum exporter since 2020,** the first time since at least 1949, and net
  exports hit a **record in May 2026** (about 5.9 million barrels a day) (log 15.12, 15.13). Production is
  about 13.8–14.0 million barrels a day (log 15.14). Exports of crude and fuel rose about 13% in 2026 (log
  15.19).
- **Very little of our oil comes from the Persian Gulf:** about 8% of crude imports in 2025, and **about 1.9%
  by June 2026** after the strait closed (log 15.17).

What's also true:
- **The US still imports more crude than it exports:** 6.28 vs 4.56 million barrels a day, January–July
  2026 (log 15.15). The net-exporter title comes from crude and fuels together.
- **Most imports come from Canada** (62–68% of crude imports in 2026), because **many US refineries are
  built for heavier crude than US wells produce** (log 15.16, 15.21).
- **And it all sells at the world price.** "Gasoline is a globally traded commodity" (EIA 2014); Brent, not
  WTI, is the stronger driver of US gasoline prices "in all four regions studied, including the Midwest"
  (log 15.22, 15.23). A US refiner can ship its gasoline or diesel abroad, so it charges Americans about
  what buyers overseas would pay. **The last time US crude was much cheaper than world crude (2011–2013),
  the pump followed the world price** (log 15.24).

**The answer, in one line: "We make our own, but we sell it at the world price."** (log 15.18)

⚠ **Comparator (APPROXIMATE, nominal):** in every past month with US crude at $85–100 (back to 2007),
the pump ran 76 cents to $1.75 above the barrel; in September 2026 it was $2.04, the widest of them (log
15.33). Not inflation-adjusted (log 15.34); if it airs, use the 2022–2024 months, which are closest in time.

### 5. "Couldn't we just keep our oil at home?"

- **Crude:** the US banned most crude exports until 2015. EIA modelled lifting it and found US gasoline
  "would be either unchanged or slightly reduced", because "petroleum product prices throughout the United
  States have a much stronger relationship to Brent prices than to WTI prices" (log 15.26, 9.10). The flip
  side: **keeping crude at home makes US crude cheaper, and the gain goes mostly to refiners' margins,**
  because fuel still sells at the world price (log 15.27).
- **Fuel (the 2026 talk):** ⚠ **trade press only, label it.** A diesel-export limit was publicly considered
  in September, and two House bills were filed on 17 September (a ban until January 2027; a trigger at $5
  diesel); bill text unopened, not enacted (log 15.29). The President: "I've said let's not send out the
  diesel … It could have a little bit of an effect on regular automobile gasoline". The Interior
  Secretary: the administration "would consider an export ban if we thought that actually might lower
  prices, but that's not the case" (log 15.29).
- **What the models say it would cost (APPROXIMATE, industry and think tanks, no government model):** an
  S&P Global analysis (September 2026) found a diesel export ban would force refiners to cut runs by about
  1.9 million barrels a day, about 12%, cutting **gasoline** output by up to 750,000 b/d and turning the US
  into a net gasoline importer (log 15.30). A 2022 bank note modelled a product-export ban as raising fuel

  imports by sea, so a ban would trap Gulf Coast supply (log 15.31). **No EIA, CBO or DOE model of a
  product-export ban was found** (log 15.32): say "industry analysts", never "studies show".

## Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

**What's in a gallon**
- **Crude is the biggest slice, about half.** EIA's latest official breakdown, **May 2026**, at $4.48: crude
  52%, refining 22%, distribution and marketing 15%, taxes 12% (log 1.1). This week, at US crude prices,
  about 53% (our arithmetic, log 15.4). Over 2016–2025 about 52% (log 1.5).
- **Taxes are fixed cents:** 18.4 cents federal on gasoline (24.4 on diesel), unchanged since **October
  1993** (log 2.1, 2.2), plus about **33.8 cents of state tax on average, about 52 cents in all** (EIA, 1
  July 2026); California 73.6, Alaska 9 (log 2.3). **Only Congress sets the federal tax;** no federal
  suspension has ever been enacted (log 2.7).
- **The refinery's slice is about double normal in 2026** (log 15.8; above), and so were the big
  refiners' spring margins in their own filings, against a weak 2025 and below 2022 (log 17.1, 17.4).

**From the barrel to the pump**
- **Each sustained $1 a barrel puts about 2.4 cents a gallon of pressure on gasoline** (EIA, 2019) (log
  3.1). About half shows up within two weeks, about 80% within four, faster on the way up (log 3.2).
- **US fuel follows the world price, not the US crude price** (EIA 2014, 2015; log 15.22, 15.26).
- **Most of the swing in gasoline prices comes from the world oil market and refining** (Kilian; direction
  only, log 3.9). In 2012, 36 of 39 economists agreed a decade of gas-price changes was "predominantly due
  to market factors rather than U.S. federal economic or energy policies"; none disagreed (log 10.1).

**The 2026 shock** (EIA's wording: "military action in the Middle East that began on February 28")
- **The Strait of Hormuz, which carries nearly 20% of world oil supply, was "effectively closed"** from 28
  February (log 4.1, 4.2). Flows fell from **21.6 to 4.9 million barrels a day** (August STEO; log 4.7).
  Shut-in production peaked at 10.9 million b/d in May and was 4.8 in September (log 4.11, 4.13).
- **Brent (EIA spot):** about **$71 on 27 February** → **$138.21 on 7 April, the year's high** → about **$69
  on 2 July** after the June memorandum → **$105 on 23 July** → **$114 average in September** (log 4.3–4.9).
- **The pump:** $2.80 in early January, a 2026 high of $4.50 on 11 May, **$4.35 now**, up $1.23 on a year
  (log 4.17, 12.1). Still below 2022's record $5.01; in today's money, 2026's worst month is about a fifth
  below June 2022 (log 12.2).

**The reserve**
- **283 million barrels, lowest since 1982** (log 4.18), down from 415 million at the end of February (log
  5.11). GAO (May 2026): drawdown capability **61% of design**, **more than a quarter of the crude
  unavailable** (log 6.25, 6.26).

**What the levers did the last time**
- **The 2022 reserve release:** Treasury modelled 13–31 cents US-only, **17–42 cents with partners**,
  assuming full pass-through, and said pass-through was "unclear" (log 6.1, 6.5). No after-the-fact
  estimate exists (log 6.22).
- **History, measured:** about **$2 a barrel** (≈5 cents) in 1990–91 and 2005; **$10–13** (≈24–31 cents)
  for Libya in 2011 (log 6.10–6.12).
- **Tax holidays reach drivers mostly, not fully:** Maryland 83% in 2022, New York about zero; **2026,
  Georgia 64%, Indiana 40–76%** (log 7.1–7.3, 7.6).
- **No agency has measured a pump effect for any fuel waiver or Jones Act waiver** (log 8.3, 8.7).

## Contested (the real fight)

The record does **not** support a fight over whether presidents control the pump price: it leans hard
toward *mostly not*. The genuine fight is narrower: which levers are worth their bills, and does anything
aimed at the refinery slice help?

**GREEN — "Pull them: small relief, at the right moment, is real relief."** The reserve exists for a
physical supply disruption, and 2026 is the textbook case. Treasury put the 2022 release at 17–42 cents
with partners (log 6.1); Libya 2011 was worth $10–13 a barrel (log 6.12); a 10-million-barrel release
temporarily cuts spot prices 2–3% (log 6.9). The 2026 US release runs as exchanges repaid with premium
barrels (log 5.8). Tax holidays reach drivers within days where they are designed well: Maryland 29 of 36
cents almost immediately (log 7.2); Georgia 64% in 2026 (log 7.6). A federal holiday at measured
pass-through is modelled at about 12 cents a gallon (log 7.8), and 51% of the March 2026 economists' panel
agreed it would deliver substantially lower prices (log 7.18). The diesel order is a real instrument, used
where a President's power ends; USDA estimates about $640 million for farms if states join (an agency
estimate, no method published; log 16.36).

**The "it's the oil companies" strand (green carries it, from the record only).** The refinery slice is
genuinely large this year (log 15.8), and the refiners' own filings show it: spring 2026 margins per
barrel about double spring 2025, operating profit per barrel about 3.5x at Valero, and the four largest
independents' Q2 net income about $13.6 billion against $2.8 billion a year earlier (log 17.1–17.3). Part
of the margin is US refiners buying cheaper American crude and selling at the world price (log 15.27,
17.17). Federal enforcers themselves called on states to investigate (log 17.19). So green may argue that
a margin this size, in a shock, is worth public scrutiny, and that state oversight (California's watchdog,
state attorneys general) is the lever that actually exists. ⛔ Green may not claim wrongdoing, a record or
a finding (log 17.4, 17.18).

**GOLD — "They're mostly theater, and the bill comes later."** The headline number is a model that assumed
full pass-through and admitted it didn't know (log 6.4, 6.5). Measured history is "more modest than
sometimes is thought", about $2 a barrel (log 6.11, 6.13). The reserve is at a 1982 low, degraded (log
6.25, 6.26). Tax holidays leak (New York about zero) and cost road money: a federal pause May–September
2026 about $17 billion (advocacy estimate via CRS; log 7.14). The federal one is Congress's lever and has
never passed (log 2.7, 16.41). The diesel order defers a tax that is "still owed", applies only to dyed
fuel, needs Treasury to find a qualifying event, and stops at every state line (log 16.5, 16.19, 16.30).
And the refinery lever cuts the wrong way: keeping crude at home mostly fattens refiners' margins (log
15.30). On the oil companies: the profits are below 2022's and follow a weak 2025 (log 17.4, 17.6); the
companies and EIA alike point to world supply, low inventories and lost US refining (log 17.12–17.15);
the only finding on record, California's, says its prices rose in line with the country's (log 17.22);
and California shelved its own margin cap (log 17.23).

**The holes each team gets to poke — earned, not asserted:**
- *Gold at green:* **Treasury's 42 cents is a ceiling built on full pass-through;** the one big success
  (Libya) is one case of four; a release is a loan against the next crisis; **tax relief is proportional to
  gallons** (log 7.9); **the diesel order's "tax-free for any reason" is not what the signed text says**
  (log 16.7); **a margin is not a profit, spring 2026 was not a record, and no investigation has found
  wrongdoing** (log 17.4, 17.9, 17.18–17.23).
- *Green at gold:* **"modest" is not "zero"** (log 6.10); **the 2026 releases are unmeasured either way**
  (log 6.22); **Georgia's 2026 holiday passed through 64%** within the same shock (log 7.6); **doing nothing
  has a price too,** the full $1.23, paid by lower-income households in lost trips (log 13.7); **about 40–55
  cents of this year's refinery slice is the US crude discount staying with refiners** (log 15.11), which is
  a real, measurable gap between what US oil costs and what US drivers pay; **the refiners' own filings
  show the margin turned into profit**, about 3.5x per barrel at Valero (log 17.2).

**Where the record leans, said plainly.**
- On "can a president set the pump price": no. Crude and fuel are priced in a world market.
- On "it's our oil, so it should be cheaper": the US makes more than it uses, but sells at the world price;
  the only reliable route to a cheaper pump is a cheaper world barrel.
- On "can a president move it at all": yes, at the margin, in cents. The decisions that move dollars
  (conflict, sanctions, diplomacy) are never made for gas prices.
- On "is it all just show": no, but the actions a president can take alone are small, and the biggest
  promise, a federal tax holiday, needs Congress.

## The reframe

The shouting asks **who** to blame. The record answers **which slice, how many cents, by when, and who
holds it**.

1. **"It's our oil" is the wrong frame.** We do make it. We sell it, and the fuel, at the world price.
2. **The refinery slice is the surprise.** About double normal this year, part US-crude discount, part
   tight world fuel, and it shows up as profit in the refiners' own filings (about double 2025, below
   2022). That's a real finding, and neither a crime nor nothing.
3. **The asymmetry is the real finding.** A president's largest influence on the barrel runs through
   conflict, sanctions and diplomacy, which move dollars, both ways, and are never made for gas prices.
4. **And the blame is asymmetric too.** Whether people think a president "can do a lot" flips with the
   party in the White House: Democrats 71% → 42%, Republicans 54% → 69%, 2006 vs 2012 (log 11.4). The
   question returns at every spike, to every president.
5. **Where the reframe does not hold:** presidents are not powerless; a reserve release is worth real cents
   in a real shock. Say "cents, not dollars, from the levers built for the pump", never "nothing at all".
