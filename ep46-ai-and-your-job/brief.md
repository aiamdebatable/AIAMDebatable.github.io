# Research brief — AI Jobs: Will AI Take the Work Away, or Just Change It?

_The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video._

**Episode type: DEBATE.** Two teams argue at full strength; the verdict declares no winner and hands the
viewer one question to ask about their own field. Why a debate and not a design episode: the record
through October 2026 settles what has happened (no economy-wide job loss yet) but cannot settle what
happens next, and that forward question is where the two sides genuinely split (Contested, below).

## The pitch, in one line

**Will there still be a way in, for me or my kid? The data shows no mass unemployment. What it shows is
fewer people being hired at the bottom rung of the most AI-exposed jobs, and nobody can yet say AI is the
cause.** Unemployment has sat between 4.1% and 4.3% since March, though it is up from 3.4% in April
2023 (C-B2, C-B21), and layoffs run at about 1% of jobs a month (C-B9). The one measured alarm is young
workers, 22 to 25, in the most AI-exposed jobs: 19% below where they would be had they kept pace with
less-exposed peers, and it works through fewer hires, not more firings (C-C1, C-C4). Its own authors
call it descriptive, not proof that AI caused it, and the rival causes are real (C-C8, C-C9, C-B31). And
when a company says "AI", nobody can yet tell whether AI is doing the work: the counts that name AI
record what the employer chose to say (B-B14, A-C3), and California's claims tracker, which bypasses the
employer, finds no statewide surge and says it cannot show cause (T1-3, T1-9, T1-4). **The real fight is
not about the record so far. It is about speed, and about who is still being let in the door.**

## The viewer's moment

**It starts with a question, not a memo: "will there still be a way in, for me or my kid?"** That is the
gut question the council found in 2,066 viewer comments. The episode answers it first, plainly, in the
cold open: the data shows no mass unemployment; the measured signal is fewer people being hired at the
entry level in the most AI-exposed jobs; that signal is descriptive, and its cause is disputed (C-B2,
C-B9, C-C1, C-C4, C-C8). Then the question goes to three viewers, each with one door and one number.
(Invented and unnamed. Each is built only from figures in the log.)

- **The graduate, 22 to 25: the way in.** The door is the first job. The number: in payroll data,
  22–25-year-olds in the two most exposed groups of jobs fell about 11% in number from November 2022 to
  June 2026, while the same age group in the three least exposed grew about 10% (C-C6). It shows up as
  hiring that didn't happen, not as firings (C-C4). It is not only a coder's problem: the gap holds with
  tech firms and computer jobs left out (T3-36), and the sharpest rises in young graduates' unemployment
  include business operations and finance (T3-35). ⚖ The rivals are real: some of this began before
  ChatGPT (C-C9), and remote work explains much of the earlier rise in young graduates' unemployment
  (C-B31).
- **The mid-career worker, 45: the way out.** The door is keeping the job you have. The number: layoffs
  run about 1% of jobs a month, 1.6 million in August, a rate that "changed little" (C-B9). California,
  sorting unemployment claims by how much of the claimant's old job AI could do, finds no statewide surge
  from the most exposed jobs (T1-9). ⚖ Don't promise more than that: the young-worker study measures
  hiring and says nothing about older workers already in the job (C-C4), and the telephone operators
  already on the switchboard were worse off a decade later (D-B5). The memo this viewer may get, and the
  word on it ("AI", "technology", or nothing), is a later beat (Make it Matter, beat 5).
- **The parent: the way in, ten years from now.** The door is whether there will be a first rung for a
  child still in school. The number: BLS still projects software developer jobs to grow 10% from 2025 to
  2035, "much faster than the average", but that projection has fallen from 17.9% to 15% to 10% across
  three editions (T4-9, T4-10, T4-11; the windows differ, and nothing read gives BLS's reason). The
  honest answer for the parent is an open question: is the way in closing, or moving somewhere else? (Is
  the way in moving?, below). History's picture: when dial phones arrived, the door may have closed for as
  much as 10–15% of a generation of young women, while later cohorts found work in other sectors (D-B6,
  D-B4).

Two more lenses, not on screen as people:
- **The freelancer** (writer, translator, designer). On Upwork, freelancers in exposed work saw about 2%
  fewer new contracts and about 5% lower earnings within six to eight months of ChatGPT, and the hit was
  hardest on the experienced, higher-priced ones (D-D1, D-D2). Writing and translation demand on a large
  platform fell 20–50% against trend (D-D5). This is where the earliest measured drops are, and it is
  platform data, not national employment.
- **★ The hook nobody expects: the job that never gets posted.** Salesforce's line on its support staff
  was not "we laid them off" but "we no longer need to actively backfill" (B-B34). Several Fed
  policymakers said AI uncertainty was "keeping both hiring and firing low" (C-B14). A job that is never
  posted appears in no layoff count. The new laws are only now starting to ask about it (A-A10, A-B29,
  A-B36).

All three viewers leave with the same check they can run themselves: is the bottom rung in my field, or
my kid's, still being hired?

## The question

**Will there still be a way in, for me or my kid?** It sits under the title's question, **will AI cause
mass unemployment, or just change the jobs?**, and the record forces two more: **when a company says
"AI", is AI doing the work or is it the excuse; and is the door into your field quietly narrowing, or
moving somewhere else?**

## Why now

_Peg hunt run 2026-10-26 (peg-hunt skill), then every peg fact re-checked at the primary by hand passes A
and B. Re-run the hunt before scripting and after the 2026-11-06 jobs report._

**The structural peg (script): California decided to count it.** SB 951 (Sen. Eloise Gómez Reyes,
"Employment: technological displacement: notice") was approved and filed on **2026-09-30** as Chapter
860, Statutes of 2026, and is set to take effect **2027-01-01** (A-B1, A-B2, A-B14). It is not a standalone
AI-layoff law: it amends the existing California WARN rules (A-B3). When a covered mass layoff,
relocation or closure is "caused in whole or in substantial part by an AI system or other automated
technology", the 60-day notice (unchanged) must add (A-B4, A-B6):
- the number, occupation and location of layoffs "substantially due to" AI or automation (A-A2);
- the job functions that will be automated (A-B7);
- the type of technology (A-B8);
- the line "This notice is for a technology displacement" at the top (A-B9).

A covered establishment has 75 or more employees (now or in the past year); a mass layoff is 50 or more
in 30 days (A-B5). The state's Employment Development Department publishes a **summary** of the notices
and a **quarterly statewide summary** of what employers report (A-A1, A-B10). It adds no new penalty: the
existing up-to-$500-a-day penalty attaches to failing to give the required notice, and is waived if back
pay is paid within three weeks; whether a notice that leaves out the AI items counts as a failure is
untested (A-B13, A-A8, A-A9). "In substantial part" and "other automated technology" are
not defined (A-B11, A-C1, A-C2). It also orders a one-time report on **AI's effect on hiring** by
2028-01-01, including recommendations on a notice for employers engaged in "technological cessation in
hiring" (A-A10, A-B12). The governor issued no signing message for it (A-B14).

**Has Washington touched it? Not as of late October (top-up T2).** Washington wants to override state AI
rules, and the Justice Department has a task force to sue states, but nothing found has touched
California's layoff-notice law. The task force exists "to challenge State AI laws" (T2-6) and its one
court action found is joining xAI's suit against Colorado's AI Act (T2-7); no suit or threat against SB
951 or its companion bills was found (T2-8). Commerce's list of "onerous" state laws, due 2026-03-11, was
not found published, and a list issued on time could not have named a law signed in September (T2-1 to
T2-5). The White House asks Congress to preempt state AI laws that "impose undue burdens" (T2-10); the
one preemption bill in play is an un-introduced draft aimed at AI **development**, not employer use
(T2-12). No statute or court order suspends the January start (T2-13). Risk: low, not zero. Say SB 951
"is set to" start in January. ⚠ Whether the White House framework's carve-out for "laws of general
applicability" would spare a Cal-WARN amendment is a legal judgement nobody has made (T2-11): never say it
does. Court dockets were not searched.

⚠ **Corrected by hand pass A, three ways:**
1. The state publishes a **summary** of what employers report, not a **count**, and there is no
   verification step (A-A1).
2. The statute says "substantially **due to**", not "substantially attributable to" (that is a law
   firm's paraphrase) (A-A2, A-A3).
3. **California is not first.** Connecticut's Public Act 26-15, Sec. 26 (an omnibus online-safety act,
   approved 2026-05-27) has required, since **2026-10-01**, that an employer filing a federal WARN notice
   tell the state Labor Department whether the layoffs relate to "artificial intelligence or another
   technological change" (A-A4, A-B24, A-B25). New York has asked on its layoff form since March 2025,
   with no statute behind it (A-A6, A-B22). California's is the most detailed: the first found that
   requires counts, occupations, job functions and technology type, and makes the state publish a
   summary. Say "the most detailed", never "first" (A-A4). The release's "first-in-the-nation" was a
   union leader describing a package of four bills (A-A5).

**New York's zero, said the way the record allows.** New York's labor department **told reporters** that
as of the end of January 2026 no employer had marked technology or automation as the reason for a
layoff; a spokesperson confirmed it to Bloomberg Law in March, and a law firm's May review of 160-plus
companies found the same (A-B16, A-B17, A-B18). Over that stretch, more than 750 notices from 162
employers covered nearly 28,300 workers (A-B19). The state publishes no tally of its own, so this is the
state's word to reporters, not state data (A-B16). Per reporters (the form itself was not seen), the
AI answer is an option on a required reason question, not a "voluntary checkbox" (A-A6). What a zero means is itself the argument: filers may treat
the answer as optional, and the form does not say whether AI must be the sole cause or a contributing one
(A-C4, A-C5); the governor's office says the department follows up on every filing (A-A11).

**The hot peg (description): October's cuts, and the two labels.**
- **Barclays (2026-10-22, Q3 results)** is the clear case of management crediting AI in investor
  documents: about 5,000 full-time roles, about 5% of employees, under "Organisational simplification and
  technology change / efficiency driven by AI" (B-B49). The CFO said "fewer developers as AI makes our
  people more productive" (B-B50). The CEO's prepared remarks: "AI is the icing on the cake, not the
  cake" (B-A2). ⚠ The 60% "support functions" figure applies to second-half actions only (B-A4). UK
  bank: not US jobs evidence (B-C4).
- **Klarna** is the other filed case: its SEC F-1 says its headcount fell (about 5,527 in 2022 to 3,422 in
  2024) through a decision "to reduce our overall headcount and drive operational efficiency by
  leveraging AI" (B-A11, B-B37).
- **Kroger (memo of 2026-10-12) is the AMBIGUOUS case.** About 3,000 of about 8,200 corporate roles
  (about 37%) over three years, details "in the coming months" (B-B43). Kroger confirmed the memo by
  sharing it with Grocery Dive (B-A8). Every quoted line says "technology", never "AI"; the AI link is
  Reuters' and AP's framing, three weeks after Kroger announced an AI push (B-A6, B-A9, B-B46). The full
  memo is unpublished, so whether it names AI anywhere is UNRESOLVED (B-A15). Kroger says the cuts will
  help it lower prices and invest in its associates: a stated plan, not money moved (B-A7). No exit-cost
  8-K was filed around it (B-B48).
- **Barclays' AI tools, as plain facts at equal weight (top-up T5; optional, the Barclays beat stands
  without it).** On 2026-10-01 Anthropic announced, quoting two Barclays co-COOs, that Barclays is
  **expanding** its use of Claude and **expects** half its developers to be using Claude Code, an AI
  coding tool, by the end of 2026: a target, not a measured rate (T5-1, T5-3). In June 2025 Barclays
  announced Microsoft 365 Copilot for 100,000 colleagues (T5-11). Claude is one of at least two AI vendors
  at the bank, not "its AI" (T5-12). What Barclays did not say: which tools, if any, the cuts came from.
  The results speech names no vendor, and the Claude announcement never mentions jobs (T5-8, T5-9); the
  cost link in the press is Bloomberg's own context (T5-10). ⛔ Never imply either tool caused the 5,000
  cuts or the "fewer developers", and never set them side by side in a way that suggests it (Script
  traps).

**The backdrop.** September 2026: payrolls +29,000, unemployment 4.2%, inside a 4.1–4.3% band since
March; July and August were revised down 60,000 combined (C-B1, C-B2, C-B3). The release never mentions
AI (C-A13). August JOLTS: layoffs and discharges 1.6 million, a 1.0% rate; hires 3.3% (C-B8, C-B9).

## Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

**The aggregate record: no economy-wide job loss yet**
- **Unemployment 4.2% in September, in a 4.1–4.3% band since March; payrolls +29,000** (C-B1, C-B2).
  Weak hiring, low firing: JOLTS layoffs 1.0%, hires 3.3%, openings 7.1 million (C-B7, C-B8, C-B9).
- **Weak payroll growth is partly arithmetic.** Fed staff economists (their own views, not the Fed's)
  estimate the job growth needed to hold unemployment steady fell from 155,000 a month in 2023–24 to about
  85,000 in 2025 as immigration slowed, and **could** fall to nearly zero (C-B11, C-A8, C-A9).
- **Productivity shows no AI-era break:** 2.1% a year since late 2019, the same as the long-run rate since
  1947 (C-B56). Q2 2026: +1.4% (C-B55).
- **Fed policymakers, July 2026:** "several" said feared AI layoffs "had not materialized to date", and
  all recognized "significant uncertainty" (C-B12, C-B13).
- **The null studies agree.** Yale Budget Lab: no discernible disruption 33 months after ChatGPT, the
  occupational mix shifting about 1 point faster than in the internet era (C-B15, C-B16); a May 2026
  follow-up finds no significant effect on employment or wages so far (C-B18), while warning its survey is
  underpowered for recent graduates and this "could change quickly" (C-B20). SIEPR: unemployment rose
  0.77 points in the most-exposed fifth of workers and 0.85 in the least (C-B22). EIG: 0.30 against 0.94
  (C-B24). Fairlie and Wu: no summer-2026 spike for recent graduates (C-B26). Denmark: chatbot adoption
  had precisely estimated zero effects on earnings and hours (C-B52).
- **Firms mostly report no headcount change from AI.** In Census data cited by SIEPR, about 5% of firms
  report any employment impact, gains and losses equally (C-B23). UK: of businesses using AI to improve
  operations, 63% report no headcount change, 6% a decrease, 1% an increase (C-A12).

**Where something IS moving: the bottom rung**
- **22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed jobs are 19% below where they would be had they kept pace with
  less-exposed peers** (ADP payroll data to June 2026), up from 15% at the July 2025 data (C-C1, C-C2).
- **Through hiring, not firing,** and concentrated where AI substitutes for tasks (C-C4, C-C5).
- **Its own authors:** "no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement", and the results are
  descriptive, not causal (C-C7, C-C8). Its weak points are theirs too (Contested).
- **Not only a coder's problem.** The gap persists with tech firms and computer jobs left out (T3-36).
  The fall in postings for people with three years' experience or less is concentrated in AI-exposed
  jobs, and the sharpest rises in young graduates' unemployment are in tech, business operations and
  finance (Burning Glass Institute, July 2025; T3-34, T3-35).

**Occupations move both ways (BLS OEWS, May 2022 → May 2025, our arithmetic)**
- **Falling:** computer programmers 132,740 → 92,230 (−30.5%); customer service representatives
  2,879,840 → 2,595,750 (−9.9%); technical writers −6.4%; graphic designers −6.6% (C-B41, C-B42, C-B43).
- **Not falling:** software developers 1,534,790 → 1,687,890 (+10.0%); paralegals +13.8%; interpreters
  and translators about flat (−0.2%); all occupations +5.1% (C-B43, C-A6).
- **BLS itself discourages reading these as a time series,** and they exclude the self-employed (C-B38,
  C-B40). Industry payrolls: computer systems design −5.1% from December 2022 to September 2026 while all
  jobs rose 3.0% (C-B44).

**How AI layoffs are counted**
- **Challenger, Gray & Christmas counts announced cuts by the reason the employer gives.** Through
  September 2026, AI was cited in 120,136 of 573,195 announced US cuts, about 21%, the leading reason
  (B-B1, B-B3). In September alone it was fifth, 3,961 (B-B2). In 2025 the share was about 4.5% (54,836 of
  1,206,374) (B-B6).
- **The jump is part wording, part denominator.** No recoding is documented; Andy Challenger says the
  messaging "swung from hedging to aggressively citing it" (B-A13). 2025's total was swollen by 308,167
  government cuts; excluding government, AI's 2025 share was about 6.1% (B-B9, B-B10; our arithmetic).
  Total cuts are down 39% from 2025 but only 15% excluding government (B-B3, B-B4).
- **Every count that names AI records what the employer chose to say:** Challenger's reason codes, New
  York's form, Connecticut's yes/no, California's notice from 2027 (B-B14, A-B24, A-C3). Management
  lawyers advise keeping the stated reason consistent with public messaging (A-C3). The one counterweight:
  New York's governor's office says its labor department follows up on every filing (A-A11, APPROXIMATE).
- **One count bypasses the employer, and it still cannot tell cause (top-up T1).** California counts
  layoffs from the jobs AI could do best, every month since June, and finds no statewide surge. Even its
  builders say it cannot tell whether AI caused a single one. It is the **California AI-Unemployment
  Tracker** (the Governor's Office, EDD and the California Policy Lab, launched 2026-06-25; T1-1, T1-2):
  unemployment-insurance claims sorted by how AI-exposed the claimant's last job was, which the claimant
  picks from a drop-down; nobody is asked whether AI caused the layoff (T1-3, T1-5). Statewide, no surge
  from highly exposed jobs since ChatGPT, through May 2026 (T1-9). What did rise: claims from master's and
  PhD holders in highly exposed jobs, 13,000 a month in November 2022 to 16,000–22,000 since mid-2023,
  with the Bay Area and professional services also elevated (T1-10); the information sector spiked and
  is back to pre-AI levels (T1-11). Its makers: "a descriptive signal, not causal evidence" (T1-4, T1-15).
  It is blind to the young-worker channel by design: a worker who was never hired files no claim (T1-14).
  One of its two exposure measures is built from Claude usage, whose users skew toward tech jobs (T1-7):
  air it as a plain fact. ⚠ The latest monthly figures are not seasonally adjusted and the update's own
  headline is seasonally adjusted: re-read before quoting a month (T1-12, APPROXIMATE).
- **Layoff notices see a sliver of layoffs.** Federal WARN covers employers of 100+, events of 50+ at a
  site, and requires no reason (A-B42, A-B43, B-B19, B-B20). WARN layoffs are about 1–6% of all layoffs
  nationally and 2.2% in Texas over 2012–23 (B-B22, B-B23). The government's monthly layoff count lumps in
  firings for cause, so it cannot isolate AI either (B-B26).
- **No federal AI-displacement statistic exists or is close.** S.3108 (Hawley, with Warner; later Kaine
  and Blackburn) has had no action since 2025-11-05; its House twin, H.R. 9352, since 2026-06-18 (A-A7,
  A-B32, A-B33). BLS's proposed time-use questions, from January 2027, measure AI **use**, not job loss
  (A-B37, A-B38).

**Exposure is not job loss**
- **The exposure scores' own authors say so.** Eloundou et al. measure potential impact "without
  distinguishing between labor-augmenting or labor-displacing effects" (D-C1); Microsoft's authors call
  inferring job loss "a mistake" (D-C5); the ILO: they capture "what AI could do… not what will happen"
  (D-C10). Anthropic's own index finds no systematic unemployment rise for highly exposed workers since
  late 2022 (D-C9).

**History: jobs changed, and someone paid**
- **Telephone operators.** Dial switching began in the late 1910s; 32% of Bell phones were on dial by
  1930, 60% by 1940, and it took almost 60 years, to 1978 (D-B1, D-B2). Young women's operator jobs fell
  50–80%, about 2% of that group's employment (D-B3). Later cohorts found work in other sectors (D-B4);
  the operators already in the job were more likely, a decade on, to be in lower-paying work or out of
  work (D-B5). **It may have closed entry-level openings for as much as 10–15% of peak cohorts** (D-B6).
- **ATMs.** Tellers per urban branch fell from 20 to 13 (1988–2004) while urban branches rose 43% (D-B7).
  BLS now projects tellers down 13% from 2025 to 2035 as branches close (D-B8, D-B9).
- **New work:** roughly 60% of 2018 employment was in job titles that did not exist in 1940 (D-B10), but
  automation's job-eroding effect has intensified over the last four decades (D-B11).
- **1966:** a national commission concluded "technology eliminates jobs, not work", and still recommended
  about 500,000 public-service jobs and an income floor (D-B12, D-B13).

**One more number the engine left out**
- **The labor share of output hit 52.8% in Q2 2026, the lowest since records began in 1947,** and real
  hourly pay fell 3.3% (annualized) that quarter (C-A14, C-A15). Nothing in the record ties it to AI; it is
  context for "who gets the gains", never evidence of displacement.

## Contested (the real fight)

The record settles the past. The fight is about speed, about what the young-worker gap is, and about what
the word "AI" in a layoff means.

**Side A — "It's coming, faster than the record shows."** (Steelmanned from primaries.)
- **The capability argument.** Amodei's January 2026 essay: AI "could displace half of all entry-level
  white collar jobs in the next 1–5 years" (D-A9), because it is "a general labor substitute for humans"
  rather than a substitute for particular jobs (D-A11), adoption "is growing at rates much faster than any
  previous technology" (D-A12, his assertion; the essay gives no adoption data), and its weaknesses "get
  addressed quickly — often, within just a few months" (D-A13). In May 2026 he framed it as a choice:
  firms can do the same with fewer people, "and that leads to things like layoffs", or do more with the
  same people (D-A14, as Fortune reported it). In 2025 he warned unemployment could reach 10–20% within one
  to five years, **in Axios's words, a scenario, not a forecast** (D-A7, D-A8).
- **The bank's number.** Goldman Sachs Research (June 2026): with a 15% productivity lift, about 9% of US
  workers, roughly 15 million, displaced over about a decade (D-A1), up from its 6–7% baseline as late as
  March 2026 (D-A5). Fortune reports its October note adds that automation accelerates in downturns and
  slower hiring weighs on employment by another 3% (D-A3; the note itself is client research and was not
  opened, D-A4).
- **Acemoglu is not on the "nothing happens" side.** His model caps the 10-year productivity gain at about
  0.66% (D-A15), but in June 2026 he expected "a small net negative impact on labor over the next five
  years", larger later, and his model widens the gap between capital and labor income (D-A19, D-A18).
- **The measured signal is growing.** The young-worker gap widened from 15% to 19% in a year (C-C2), is
  concentrated where AI substitutes for tasks (C-C5), and survives an interest-rate control (C-C10).
  Junior software-developer vacancies fell 14–15% against senior ones after ChatGPT, with little sign of an
  earlier break and no such fall in mechanical engineering (C-C18, C-C19). Several Fed policymakers tie low
  hiring partly to AI uncertainty (C-B14).
- **Where AI substitutes, jobs are falling.** Computer programmers −30.5% in three years; customer service
  representatives −9.9% (C-B43). Freelance writing and translation demand down 20–50% against trend
  (D-D5); Fiverr's active buyers down 21.9%, with the company citing "persistent weakness across
  categories most exposed to AI automation" (D-D8).
- **Companies say it in filings, not just press releases:** Barclays' investor slide and Klarna's F-1
  (B-B49, B-A11). Salesforce's CEO said support went from 9,000 to about 5,000 "because I need less heads"
  (B-B33, his stated view).
- **Cheap, so-so automation brings no jobs boom.** Technology that replaces workers but makes things only
  a little cheaper sets off no surge in demand to absorb them; Acemoglu and Restrepo's example is
  automated customer service (T4-6, T4-7). And translation, made nearly free by machine translation, did
  not grow its jobs: about 28,000 translator jobs the trend predicted were never created (T4-17). Full
  case under "Will cheaper work make more jobs?", below.
- **History's sting:** the operators already in the job lost out, and the entry door may have closed for
  as much as 10–15% of peak cohorts (D-B5, D-B6); automation's eroding effect has grown, not faded
  (D-B11).

**Side B — "It changes the jobs, as it always has."** (Steelmanned from primaries.)
- **Every aggregate series and every causal study so far finds no economy-wide displacement** (Settled):
  unemployment in a 4.1–4.3% band since March, layoffs at 1%, productivity on its long-run trend (C-B2,
  C-B9, C-B56); Yale, SIEPR, EIG, Fairlie-Wu, Denmark (C-B15–B26, C-B52). If AI were already replacing labor at scale, productivity
  should be accelerating, and it isn't (B-C3, Oxford Economics via Fortune).
- **The young-worker gap has rivals its own authors concede.** Exposed and unexposed jobs diverged before
  ChatGPT (C-C9); adding the jobs' college share halves the estimate, to significance only at 10% (C-C11);
  the payroll sample's gap is far larger than the Census survey's for 2022–24 (C-C12). Remote work can
  explain 64% of the rise in young graduates' unemployment from 2017–19 to 2022–24 (C-B31). The NY Fed
  finds the postings divergence began before 2022 and AI "is not the main driver of the slowdown in
  hiring" (C-B34, C-B35), and, on the same vendor's data as the junior-software paper, no junior-vs-senior
  split within exposed jobs (C-C20).
- **Software postings peaked in February 2022, before ChatGPT** (C-A10). ⚠ But the claim is weaker than it
  sounds: at ChatGPT's launch they were still 42% above their 2020 level, and the fall below it came after
  (C-A11).
- **Occupations reshuffle.** Programmers fell, but software developers grew by more, and the two together
  grew 6.75%, a bit faster than all jobs (C-B43, C-A6; our arithmetic). That fits work moving between job
  titles (an inference from the arithmetic, not a finding).
- **"AI" in a layoff is often a label.** Amazon's CEO said a 14,000-role cut was "not even really
  AI-driven, not right now at least" (B-A10). TCS's CEO called its cut a skills mismatch (B-B41).
  Salesforce's spokesperson reframed its cut as not backfilling plus "hundreds" redeployed (B-B34). Klarna's
  CEO said AI service gave "lower quality" and it re-hired people (B-B38). IBM's CEO said hiring rose
  elsewhere (B-B39). Oxford Economics "suspect[s]" firms dress up layoffs as good news (B-B42); a Wharton
  professor says firms announce the AI they hope for, not work done (B-C2); Challenger says naming AI "can
  win over investors" (B-B17). Even Barclays' CEO says AI is "the icing on the cake, not the cake" (B-A2).
- **History says new work arrives:** about 60% of today's jobs have titles that did not exist in 1940
  (D-B10); tellers per branch fell but branches multiplied (D-B7); the operators' successors found work
  elsewhere (D-B4); "technology eliminates jobs, not work" (D-B12).
- **The forward case: cheaper work, more demand, more jobs.** When looms made cloth cheap, people bought
  far more of it and the number of weavers rose (T4-3). BLS used the same logic for software: AI "may
  lower prices and increase demand for software products, thus boosting employment demand for software
  developers" (T4-8), and it still projects developer jobs growing "much faster than the average" (T4-11).
  Nine years after "stop training radiologists", radiology offered a record number of residency positions
  (T4-13, T4-14). Full case, and where it breaks, below.
- **Even the forecasters are cautious.** Goldman's August 2025 view: "skeptical that AI will lead to large
  employment reductions over the next decade" (D-A6); its 9% scenario still has unemployment rising by
  less than one point in any year (D-A2).

### Will cheaper work make more jobs? Side B's forward case, and where it breaks (top-up T4)

The most-liked argument in the viewer comments is the "Jevons" case: make work cheaper, people buy more
of it, and jobs grow. At full strength, from primaries:
- **Weavers.** Nineteenth-century looms cut the labour needed per yard of cloth. Cheaper cloth sold far
  more yards, so the demand for weavers rose (Bessen, 2015; T4-3).
- **New work.** Most of today's employment is in job specialties introduced after 1940 (Autor and
  co-authors; T4-1, D-B10).
- **BLS's software projections.** BLS wrote the argument into its own projections (T4-8) and still
  projects software developers, QA analysts and testers at +10% for 2025–35, "much faster than the
  average", naming AI among the sources of demand (T4-11).
- **Radiology.** Geoffrey Hinton said in 2016 that people should stop training radiologists (a secondhand
  transcription by an economist who was in the room; T4-13, APPROXIMATE). In the 2025 Match, radiology
  programmes offered a record 1,451 residency **positions** (trade press quoting the Match; T4-14,
  APPROXIMATE). Positions, not employment: whether radiologist employment grew since 2016 cannot be said
  from the record (T4-16).
- **Inside one AI company,** staff estimate that 27% of their AI-assisted work would not have been done
  at all without it: self-reported, at one company, Anthropic's own study (T4-31).

Where it breaks, also measured:
- **Demand fills up.** Bessen's own later work, on two centuries of US textile, steel and auto data:
  automation added jobs while demand was elastic, then cut jobs once people had enough of the product
  (Bessen, 2019; T4-4). His conclusion: not mass unemployment, but "disruptive transitions to new
  industries" (T4-5).
- **New work grows where technology helps a job, and shrinks where it replaces the job's tasks,** and the
  two tend to arrive together (T4-2).
- **So-so automation.** Technology that replaces workers but makes things only a little cheaper sets off
  no demand boom; the example is automated customer service (Acemoglu and Restrepo; T4-6, T4-7).
- **Translators, the counter-example.** Machine translation made translation nearly free, and
  cross-border trade grew (T4-21, APPROXIMATE). But where Google Translate use rose, translator employment
  growth fell: about 28,000 fewer translator jobs were created, 2010–2023, than the trend implies (Frey and
  Llanos-Paredes; T4-17); wage growth slowed, then recovered from about 2016 (T4-18). BLS's count fell from
  58,870 (May 2019) to 52,060 (May 2025), about −12%, while all jobs grew about 6% (T4-19). ⚠ That count
  leaves out the self-employed, and BLS puts 28% of translators in that group, so part of the drop may be
  a move to freelancing (T4-19). Most of it came before 2022: May 2022 to May 2025 alone was about flat
  (C-B43; our arithmetic from the two rows). BLS projects +2% for 2025–35 (T4-20).
- **BLS's own software projection is falling:** +17.9% (2023–33), +15% (2024–34), +10% (2025–35). The
  windows differ, and nothing read gives BLS's reason (T4-9, T4-10, T4-11). BLS also wrote that "there is
  no clear evidence" AI productivity gains will outweigh demand (T4-9).
- **For software, nobody has measured it.** No source found gives a measured price elasticity of demand
  for software, or shows cheaper software production raising software employment; BLS calls the effect
  "highly uncertain" (T4-12, UNRESOLVED).

**Net, said plainly: "sometimes, for a while, when demand is hungry."** Not "always". Whether software is
a weaving case or a translation case is unmeasured.

### "AI will write 90% of the code": said vs measured (top-up T4)
- **Said.** At the Council on Foreign Relations on 2025-03-10, Dario Amodei, who runs Anthropic, a company
  that sells coding AI, said AI would be "writing 90 percent of the code" in "three to six months", and
  "essentially all of the code" in twelve (T4-24, T4-25). In the same answer he said programmers would
  still specify and design for now, and that "eventually all those little islands will get picked off"
  (T4-26). It is a forecast of AI taking more of the work, not a Jevons claim. The popular retelling, "by
  the end of the year" and "within enterprises", is wrong (T4-27, CORRECTED).
- **Measured.** Company claims, from companies that sell the tools: more than a quarter of new code at
  Google, reviewed by engineers (October 2024; T4-28); "maybe 20 to 30 percent" in some Microsoft projects
  (April 2025, via The Register; T4-29, APPROXIMATE). Anthropic's own study of its staff reports
  self-reported use of Claude in 60% of their work and 67% more merged pull requests per engineer per day,
  but no share of code written by AI (T4-30). The only independent count found: about 29% of US Python
  functions were AI-written by the end of 2024, with output up 3.6% and experienced programmers capturing
  nearly all of the gains (Daniotti and co-authors; T4-32). Nothing independent found shows 90% across the
  industry by late 2025; his later "70, 80, 90%" was for Anthropic alone, via Fortune (T4-27).

### "More code is more to maintain" (top-up T4)
Partial support, mostly correlational, and no randomized study of defects:
- **Google's DORA surveys** (correlation, not cause): in 2024, 25% more AI adoption went with an estimated
  7.2% drop in delivery stability and 1.5% in throughput (T4-36), and with better code-quality and
  documentation ratings (T4-37). In 2025, AI adoption went with higher throughput but still lower
  stability (T4-38).
- **GitClear,** a vendor that sells code-quality tools: more duplicated code and more short-term churn,
  2020–2024, a trend over time rather than AI against human line by line; only its landing page was read
  (T4-39, APPROXIMATE).
- **The one randomized trial (METR, early 2025)** measured speed, not defects: 16 experienced developers
  on their own mature projects took 19% longer with AI allowed, while expecting to be 24% faster and
  believing afterwards they had been 20% faster (T4-33, T4-34). Its 2026 follow-up could not produce a
  reliable number because developers would no longer work without AI; METR says they are likely faster
  now, but not by how much (T4-35).
- **Security:** in a security vendor's lab test, AI-written code carried a flaw in 45% of tasks (T3-31).

### Where the record lands, said plainly (no false balance)
- **On "is AI causing mass unemployment now?" the record leans hard one way: no.** Every aggregate series
  and every null study agrees, and so do the alarm side's own best sources (Both agree, below). A team that
  argues mass unemployment is here today is arguing against its own witnesses.
- **On "is AI narrowing the entry door?" the record is genuinely split.** The 19% gap is real and widening;
  its causation is not established, and two careful postings studies disagree (C-C1, C-C18, C-C20).
- **On "will it be faster this time?" the record cannot answer.** No source measures AI adoption against
  the PC or the internet; Amodei's speed claim is asserted, not measured (D-A12; hand pass D, correction
  10). This is the genuine 50/50, and it is where the teams should spend their time.
- **On "will cheaper work make more jobs?" the record says sometimes, for a while, when demand is
  hungry.** Weavers yes, translators no; for software, nobody has measured how much more people will buy
  as it gets cheaper (T4-3, T4-4, T4-17, T4-12).
- **On "is the way in moving into business roles?" the record cannot say.** Building with AI tools is
  spreading beyond software jobs inside one company's own data; no source measures the jobs moving, and
  postings data leans against it (Is the way in moving?, below).
- **On "when a company says AI, is it true?" nobody can tell yet.** The new laws will mostly record what
  management chooses to say (A-C3, B-B14), and California's claims tracker, which bypasses the employer,
  cannot show cause either (T1-4).

## Is the way in moving? (an open question, top-up T3)

One more possibility the record has to face: what if the first rung is not closing but moving, out of
junior developer and IT jobs and into business teams (customer service, sales, operations, finance) whose
own people now build tools with AI, with IT's job turning to fitting what they build into everything else
and keeping it secure? This airs as an **open question, with what would answer it**, never as a trend.

**What is measured: building is spreading into non-software jobs, but only inside one company's data.**
- Anthropic studied about 400,000 sessions of its own Claude Code tool, from about 235,000 people (October
  2025 to April 2026), and had a model guess each user's job from the session. After software and maths
  jobs, the largest groups are business and finance, then arts and media, management, and the sciences
  (T3-1); the fastest-growing non-software groups are management, sales and legal (T3-2). Non-engineers
  succeed about 26% of the time against about 30% for engineers (T3-5).
- Say whose data it is, plainly: Anthropic's study of the users of its own product. It publishes no share
  of non-engineers (T3-4, UNRESOLVED); its users already chose a coding tool; the job could be guessed for
  only about 70% of sessions (T3-3); and it "cannot measure … whether code written in a session is
  actually used or discarded" (T3-7). Anthropic itself treats "software production … part of ordinary work
  in every field" as something to watch for, not a finding (T3-8).

**What is measured: the load on IT is real.**
- Among breached organisations, the share whose breach or incident involved "shadow AI" (staff using AI
  without approval) more than doubled in a year: 1 in 5 in IBM's 2025 report, 43% in its 2026 report (T3-30,
  T3-30b). Both are shares of breached organisations IBM's researchers interviewed, not of all companies, and
  neither separates pasting data into a chatbot from tools the business built. Never "43% of companies".
- In a security vendor's lab test, AI-written code carried a security flaw in 45% of tasks, with no
  improvement as models got better at writing working code (Veracode; T3-31).
- Among a tool vendor's own customers, 60% had built something outside IT oversight in the past year, and
  44% test thoroughly before deploying (Retool, a vendor survey of its own users; T3-28, T3-29).
- The risk is measured; a change in what IT jobs are is not.

**What is not measured, and the postings data leans against it.** No source measures business units
hiring beginners to build what junior developers used to build.
- When a business job mentions AI, it mostly means **using** it. Indeed counts AI words in postings, not a
  requirement to build (T3-15), and says the non-tech job titles carrying "AI" (63% of such US titles)
  mostly mean using AI-assisted software or advising on it (T3-17, T3-18, CORRECTED). The skill that is
  spreading is AI literacy, up 70% in a year in LinkedIn's count (T3-23); AI-engineering skills sit at
  about 1–2% of LinkedIn members in operations, sales, finance and customer support (T3-21).
- In business and finance postings that ask for AI skills, **coding is asked for less**, not more:
  computer science, data science and coding languages appear less often than in 2022 (Lightcast data
  charted by EAB, to March 2025, before the latest coding agents; T3-20, CORRECTED).
- **The business door is narrowing for beginners too.** The entry-level postings drop is concentrated in
  AI-exposed jobs, and the sharpest rises in young graduates' unemployment include business operations and
  finance (T3-34, T3-35); the Stanford gap holds outside tech (T3-36).
- Of the 52% of US workers who use AI at work, about 1 in 6 use it for help with code (Gallup, Q2 2026;
  T3-24, T3-25).
- The idea is not new: in 2009 Gartner predicted business users would build at least a quarter of new
  business apps by 2014, with IT moving to "deeper architectural concerns"; nobody measured whether it
  came true (via IT Pro; T3-37, APPROXIMATE).

**What would answer it:** postings or payroll data showing business and finance teams hiring beginners
and asking them to build software; a count of who builds with AI tools across the whole workforce, not
inside one product's users, and of whether what they build is kept; and a measure of IT roles being
rewritten around integrating and securing it. None exists yet.

**Said plainly:** the skill of building is spreading into ordinary jobs; nobody has shown it has become a
new way in. For the parent and the graduate: the way in may be knowing a job really well and then building
for it, rather than starting as a junior coder. That is a hope with early signs, not something anyone has
measured.

## The reframe

**The shouting frames it as "mass unemployment vs nothing happens". The record points somewhere
quieter: the hiring door.** So far, AI isn't pushing many people out: layoffs are low, and unemployment
has risen only slowly (C-B9, C-B2, C-B21). Where anything shows, it is at the **hiring** door: fewer
young people are getting in to exposed jobs, through fewer hires rather than more firings (C-C4, C-C6).
That shows up in nobody's layoff count, because no layoff happened. It is also where the alarm side's own
warning points: Amodei's is about entry-level jobs (D-A9).

**It has happened before.** When dial phones replaced operators, the women already on the switchboard were
hurt (D-B5), and the door may have closed on entry-level openings for as much as 10–15% of peak cohorts,
while later cohorts found work in other sectors (D-B6, D-B4). The record does not say which effect was
bigger.

**And the new laws already know it.** California's SB 951 counts layoffs, but orders a study of AI's effect
on **hiring** and of a notice for "technological cessation in hiring" (A-A10, A-B12). New York's pending
bill would count "positions previously filled" that an employer decides not to fill because of AI (A-B29),
and its sponsors' memo names "discouraging future hiring through attrition" (A-C7). The stalled federal
bill counts the same unfilled positions (A-B36).

**The second mistake is treating the word "AI" as evidence either way.** Firms have reasons to overclaim
(to please investors, B-B17) and reasons to underclaim (naming AI can push current and prospective employees away, and Challenger
expects regulation to make tracking "more opaque", B-B17, B-B18). Today,
"is AI doing the work?" mostly cannot be answered from the record.

**So the useful question for a viewer is not "will I be laid off by AI?" It is "is the bottom rung in my
field still being hired?"**

## Both sides quietly agree

Even the loudest voices on the alarm side agree that AI is not causing economy-wide job loss today:
- **Amodei** wrote that he agrees AI is "likely not" displacing jobs right now (D-A10).
- **Goldman's** 9% scenario has unemployment rising by less than one point in any given year (D-A2).
- **The Stanford "Canaries" authors**, whose 19% gap is the alarm side's best measured evidence, find "no
  evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement" (C-C7).
- **Anthropic's own exposure index** finds no systematic unemployment rise for highly exposed workers
  (D-C9).

And both sides agree on three more things. If an effect comes, it shows first for young, entry-level
workers in exposed jobs, through hiring rather than firing (C-C4; D-A9 frames his warning around
entry-level jobs). Exposure scores are maps of where work could change, not job-loss forecasts, and their
authors say so (D-C1, D-C5, D-C10). And current data cannot cleanly separate AI from interest rates, the
pandemic unwind, remote work and immigration (C-C9, C-C11, C-B31, C-B11). **The real disagreement is about
speed and the future, not the record so far.**

## Verdict framing

**No winner.** The judge says what the record settles (no mass unemployment yet; something moving at the
entry door; the label "AI" unverifiable) and hands the viewer **one question: "Is the bottom rung in your
field still being hired?"** The judge may name how to check it. The judge never answers it for the viewer.

Three ways to check, each with a real instrument behind it and its documented weakness:
1. **Watch the door, not the exit.** Junior postings and new-hire rates in your field: Indeed's software
   postings index (C-B36), the NY Fed's recent-graduate data, updated in November (C-B30). Weakness:
   postings are not hires, and two careful studies of the same postings data disagree on junior vs senior
   (C-C18, C-C20); the software peak came before ChatGPT (C-A10).
2. **Read what your employer tells investors, not just the memo.** Barclays' slide and Klarna's F-1 credit
   AI; Kroger's memo says "technology"; Amazon's CEO said "not right now at least" (B-B49, B-A11, B-B45,
   B-A10). Weakness: the label runs both ways, and naming AI can be a pitch to investors (B-B17).
3. **Read the state's numbers.** Already, every month: California's AI-Unemployment Tracker, which sorts
   unemployment claims by how AI-exposed the claimant's old job was (T1-1, T1-3, T1-12). Weakness: it
   shows exposure, not cause, and cannot see a job that was never filled (T1-4, T1-14). From 2027:
   California's EDD will post a quarterly summary of AI-tagged layoff notices; Connecticut already
   collects a yes/no (A-B10, A-B24). Weakness: New York's form drew a zero, the state told reporters
   (A-B16, A-B17), it is self-reported, and layoff notices cover a sliver of all layoffs (B-B22).

## Make it Matter — the journey, one door at a time

A suggested beat order for the desk (the writer owns the words; every fact below has a row above):
1. **The gut question, answered first.** "Will there still be a way in, for me or my kid?" In the first
   seconds, plainly: no mass unemployment in the data; the measured signal is fewer people hired at the
   entry level in the most AI-exposed jobs; it is descriptive, and the cause is disputed (C-B2, C-B9,
   C-C1, C-C4, C-C8). Then the hook: the job that never gets posted (B-B34, C-B14).
2. **Three viewers, three doors, one number each.** The graduate (the way in: about 11% fewer 22–25s in
   the most exposed jobs, about 10% more in the least, C-C6); the mid-career worker (the way out: layoffs
   about 1% of jobs a month, no statewide surge in California's claims, C-B9, T1-9); the parent (the way
   in, ten years out: BLS still projects developer jobs +10%, down from 17.9% two editions ago, T4-9,
   T4-11).
3. **What the record says (the way out).** Unemployment up only slowly, layoffs at 1%, productivity on
   trend (C-B2, C-B21, C-B9, C-B56); California's tracker finds no surge and says it can't show cause
   (T1-9, T1-4). The fear against the record. "Hold that."
4. **The bottom rung (the way in).** The 19% gap, fewer hires not more firings, the rivals, and not only
   coders (C-C1, C-C4, C-C9, C-C11, C-B31, T3-36). Follow the graduate.
5. **The label on the memo.** Two companies, one month, two labels: Barclays writes "AI" on the slide and
   calls it the icing; Kroger says "technology" (B-B49, B-A2, B-B45). About 1 in 5 announced US cuts this
   year cite AI, but that is what employers said, and the jump is part wording (B-B1, B-A13). "Wait, so
   'AI' is just what they wrote on the form?" New York asked, and got zero (A-B16). Optional, at equal
   weight and never as the cause: Barclays expects half its developers on Claude Code by end-2026, and in
   2025 announced Copilot for 100,000 staff; it never said which tools, if any, the cuts came from (T5-3, T5-11,
   T5-9).
6. **The switchboard.** The operators: the door may have closed on the next generation (D-B6). Picture
   it.
7. **Is the way in moving?** For the parent: building with AI is spreading into ordinary jobs (in one AI
   company's own data), but job ads don't show employers hiring beginners for it, and the business door
   is narrowing too (T3-1, T3-5, T3-18, T3-20, T3-34). An open question, with what would answer it.
8. **The laws** (on screen or in the description, not a wall of bill numbers): California will count the
   exit, and asks about the door; New York's bill would count the unfilled job; the federal bill is stuck;
   nothing from Washington has touched California's law as of late October (A-A10, A-B29, A-B32, T2-8,
   T2-13).
9. **The forward fight** goes to the teams: speed (Amodei, Goldman) against the record and history, and
   Side B's "cheaper work, more jobs" against the translators (T4-3, T4-17). "Sometimes, for a while, when
   demand is hungry."

**Pictures, not ledgers (town):** the jobs board for payrolls and unemployment; the square's chart for the
young-worker gap (headline chart, close framing); three doors for the three viewers; the store window or
office tower for the Barclays and Kroger memos; the counting room or the state office for the layoff
notice and its header line; a cloud map for California, Connecticut and New York; a switchboard or the
phone fixture for the operators; a loom beside a translator's desk for the forward fight.

## Comparators a viewer can picture

- **Payroll growth:** +29,000 in September against a Fed-staff estimate that the break-even pace was about
  85,000 a month in 2025 and could fall to nearly zero (C-B1, C-B11, C-A8). Weak hiring, not collapse.
- **The bottom rung:** the two most exposed fifths of jobs for 22–25s fell about 11% while the three least
  exposed grew about 10%, same age, same years (C-C6). That is the cleaner picture than "19%".
- **Programmers vs developers:** about 40,500 fewer programmers and about 153,100 more developers over
  three years (C-B41, C-B42; our arithmetic).
- **The switchboard:** 50–80% of young women's operator jobs went, about 2 in every 100 jobs that group
  held (D-B3).
- **The bank branch:** tellers per urban branch 20 → 13, branches up 43% (D-B7).
- **Goldman's 15 million** is spread over about a decade, with unemployment rising by under a point in any
  year (D-A1, D-A2).
- **Weavers vs translators:** cheaper cloth, more weavers (T4-3); nearly free translation, about 28,000
  translator jobs the trend predicted that never appeared (T4-17).
- **"90% of code" vs the count:** predicted for mid-to-late 2025 (T4-24); the only independent count
  found put it near 3 in 10 US Python functions by the end of 2024 (T4-32). Say "Python functions" on
  screen.
- **BLS's software projection:** +17.9% → +15% → +10% across three editions, still "much faster than
  the average" (T4-9 to T4-11).

## Script traps

**The peg and the laws (hand pass A)**
- ⛔ **"Summary", never "count".** "The state will post a quarterly summary of what employers report"
  (A-A1).
- ⛔ **On screen, the statute's words only:** "caused in whole or in substantial part", "substantially due
  to". Never "substantially attributable" (A-A2, A-A3). Never quote the governor's one-line summary
  ("caused by an AI system") as the law (A-D1).
- ⛔ **Never "first" or "first-in-the-nation"** for SB 951. "The most detailed" or "the first to make the
  state publish it". Connecticut's took effect 2026-10-01 (A-A4); the release's "first-in-the-nation" was a
  union leader on four bills together (A-A5).
- **New York:** "New York's labor department told reporters…", never "state data shows" (A-B16). "A
  question on New York's layoff form", never "voluntary checkbox" (A-A6). 750+ notices and 162 employers
  are different units: never "750 companies" (A-B19).
- **Penalty:** "the law adds no penalty for getting the AI answer wrong"; never "it only penalizes failing
  to file" as a settled reading (A-A8). If "$500 a day" airs, add that it is waived if back pay is paid
  within three weeks (A-A9).
- **SB 951's reach:** only layoffs already covered by Cal-WARN (75+ establishment, 50+ in 30 days), 60 days'
  notice, unchanged (A-B5, A-B6). Never "90 days".
- **Connecticut** is an online-safety act with one section on this: never "Connecticut's AI layoff law"
  (A-B25).
- **New York's pending bill:** by number (S.8706-B / A.9581-B), never "AI Labor Information Act" (A-B26);
  "more than fifty" employees, never "50+" (A-B23); passed both chambers in June, **not yet sent to the
  governor** as of 2026-10-26 (A-B27, A-B28).
- **Federal bill:** S.3108, introduced 2025-11-05 by Hawley with Warner, never "Warner–Hawley, December
  2025" (A-A7).
- **Start date:** SB 951 "is set to" start in January, never "will start, unchallenged". As of late
  October nothing federal has touched it (T2-8, T2-13), but the task force exists and the risk is low,
  not zero. ⛔ Never say the White House framework spares it (T2-11). Re-check before "starts in seven
  weeks" airs (court dockets were not searched).
- **Warsh is the Fed Chairman** (A-B39); his task forces are advisory, not data series.

**Companies and counts (hand pass B)**
- ⛔ **Never "Kroger tied its cuts to AI".** "Kroger's memo cited technology; the press linked it to AI"
  (B-A9, B-A6). The savings line: "Kroger says the cuts will help it lower prices and invest in its
  associates", a stated plan, never "moved money to store staff" (B-A7).
- **Barclays' "icing" line:** quote the investor-call version, attributed to the results call (B-A2).
  "The bulk of the efficiency gains" comes from earlier technology spending is the CEO's media-call quote
  as ITV reports it: attribute it to that, never to the prepared remarks (B-A1, B-A3). 60% is of
  second-half actions (B-A4). "Subject to consultation", no country (B-A5). ⛔ No staff base (93,000) and
  no "1,000 already gone": press only (B-B55, B-B54).
- **Jassy:** always with his hedge, "not right now at least" (B-A10).
- **Klarna:** its own filing credits AI; never "mostly attrition and a hiring freeze" (B-A11).
- **IBM:** its annual report credits "automation and AI-driven efficiencies" for productivity, not
  headcount; never "IBM doesn't attribute it to AI"; "hundreds" of HR roles, never "about 200" (B-A12,
  B-B39).
- **Amazon:** "about 30,000 corporate roles", no percentage (B-A14, B-B31).
- **Challenger:** "AI was cited in about 1 in 5 announced US cuts this year": always "cited" or "named by
  employers", never "caused" (B-B1). Never "Challenger recoded" (B-A13). If "down 39%" airs, add "15%
  excluding government" (B-B4). Never "peaked at 24%": 24% through July, 22% through August, 21% through
  September (B-B12). Challenger counts US-based employers' announcements only (B-C4).
- ⛔ **Never divide one instrument by another.** Challenger's 120,136 are announcements over nine months;
  JOLTS's 1.6 million are realized separations in one month. Never "AI is X% of layoffs" from the two.

**The measured record (hand pass C)**
- **Breakeven:** "Fed staff economists estimate it could be near zero", never "the Fed says" (C-A8, C-A9).
- **The 19% gap:** "19% below where they'd be had they kept pace with less-exposed peers"; it was 15% at
  the July 2025 data. ⛔ Never set it against the older 13% or 16% headlines: those were a different
  measure (C-C1, C-C2, C-C3). Always "descriptive, not proof AI caused it" (C-C8).
- **"About 17 points"** of the gap's fall carry it below its pre-pandemic baseline, out of about 30 points
  of decline; never "17 of 18" (C-A7).
- **Indeed:** "postings peaked in early 2022, before ChatGPT" only with "but fell below their pre-pandemic
  level after it" (C-A10, C-A11).
- **Remote work's 64%** covers the rise from 2017–19 to 2022–24, not 2025–26, and the authors say AI may
  matter more going forward (C-B31, C-B33).
- **IZA and the NY Fed disagree** on junior vs senior postings: air both or neither (C-C18, C-C20).
- **All occupations +5.1%**, not 5.2% (C-A6). Programmers + developers is our arithmetic, and the
  "reclassification" reading is an inference (C-B43).
- **Customer service:** ⛔ never "BLS says it isn't AI". BLS projects less demand as tasks "continue to be
  automated" and gives no cause for the past fall (C-A4, C-A5).
- **Software publishers' +12.9%** led 30 selected service industries, not "every industry" (C-A1).
- **UK:** "of businesses using AI to improve operations, 63% saw no change" (C-A12).
- **20–24-year-old unemployment jumped to 8.0% in September** (from 7.1%; 9.2% a year earlier). One month
  is noise: ⛔ never air it as an AI signal (C-B6).
- **The labor share record low** is not attributable to AI: context only (C-A14).
- **Recent graduates:** about 5.7% unemployed against about 4.1% for all workers, June 2026 (C-B28); the
  data update in November, so re-check (C-B30).

**The forward cases, history and creative work (hand pass D)**
- **Goldman's 9% and "under a point a year" are June 2026 research,** never "Goldman's October note"
  (D-A1, D-A2). "One in 11" restates the 9%, it does not include the extra 3% (D-A3). The recession-timing
  mechanism is Fortune's account of an unopened note (D-A4).
- **Amodei:** "in the next one to five years", never "within about five years"; the 10–20% is Axios's
  paraphrase and a scenario (D-A7, D-A8). His essay uses "displace" in one place and "disrupt" in another:
  quote one verbatim, never merge them (hand pass D, correction 10). His speed claim is his assertion
  (D-A12).
- **Acemoglu:** "slow gains, and labor still loses a little": never file him under "nothing happens"
  (D-A19).
- **Dial switching began in the late 1910s and took almost 60 years, to 1978**; never "1920–1978" (D-B2).
- **Freelancers:** ⛔ no "image freelancers −3.7% jobs, −9.4% income": the authors say the image effect was
  identical to text (D-D3). In writing and translation the biggest drop was for **experienced** workers,
  never "novices fell generally" (D-D6).
- **Fiverr:** "expects revenue to fall 14–17% this year", never "cut guidance 14–17%" (D-D9).
- **WGA 2026:** written notice if the studio licenses writers' work for AI training, and the Guild "can
  request discussion"; no pre-deployment meetings (D-D14). Blizzard: bargaining over AI, never a "60-day
  layoff notice" (D-D16, D-D17).
- **Indian IT:** air only TCS's own headcount series, 613,069 → 582,163 → 598,056 (D-E1, D-E2); no
  "net 7,389", no "fresher offers cut" (D-E3, D-E4).

**The top-ups (T1 to T5)**
- **California's tracker:** "California counts layoffs from the jobs AI could do best, every month, and
  finds no statewide surge. Even its builders say it cannot tell whether AI caused a single one" (T1-9,
  T1-4). ⛔ Never "California counts AI layoffs" or "the tracker shows AI isn't costing jobs": it shows
  exposure, not cause (T1-4, T1-15). Never "every count is the employer's word" (T1-15). The
  graduate-degree rise is "since mid-2023" in highly exposed jobs, not an AI finding (T1-10).
- ⛔ **Is the way in moving (T3):** never "entry-level coding jobs are moving into the business" as fact:
  no source measures jobs moving (T3-4, T3-7). Never "half of AI jobs are now outside tech" without
  "mostly using AI, not building" (T3-18, T3-19). Never "business jobs now want coders": the one direct
  measure found the opposite (T3-20). Never "IT's job is becoming securing what the business builds" as a
  measured trend: the risk is measured, the job change is not (T3-30, T3-31). Retool's figures only as
  "a vendor's survey of its own customers" (T3-27 to T3-29). No Gartner forecast (neither was read at
  Gartner, and both are forecasts). Don't turn one employer's experience into a trend. The Claude Code
  study airs as "Anthropic's study of its own coding tool's users", plainly, with no callout and no share
  (T3-1, T3-4).
- ⛔ **"Cheaper work, more jobs" and code (T4):** never "Amodei predicted 90% by the end of the year" or
  "in enterprises" (T4-27); never "AI writes 90% of code" as a fact. Never "radiologist employment rose
  since 2016": only residency positions are shown (T4-14, T4-16). Never DORA or GitClear as proof that AI
  **causes** bugs: both are correlational, and GitClear is a vendor (T4-36 to T4-39). Never METR's 19% as
  today's effect: METR says it is likely out of date (T4-33, T4-35). Never "economists have measured that
  cheaper software creates more developer jobs": no such estimate was found (T4-12). Translators' BLS drop
  always with the self-employed caveat (T4-19). Hinton's line is "as an economist who was there recalls
  it" (T4-13). The spreadsheet-made-more-accountants story is unchecked (T4-23): not on air.
- ⛔ **Barclays and Claude (T5):** Barclays "expects" half its developers to be using Claude Code by the end
  of 2026: a target, never "half its developers use" (T5-3). Never "Barclays' AI runs on Claude" or "its
  AI is Claude": it has at least two vendors, and Microsoft 365 Copilot (100,000 colleagues, announced
  2025) airs beside Claude at equal weight (T5-11, T5-12). ⛔ Never say or imply that Claude or Copilot
  caused, or is behind, the 5,000 cuts or the "fewer developers": Barclays named no tool, and the CEO
  called AI "the icing on the cake, not the cake" (T5-9, B-A2). Never put the two in one sentence or one
  shot that reads as cause ("after rolling out Claude Code, Barclays cut developers"); if the tools air,
  the line ends on what Barclays did not say. How the bank gets Claude (direct or through a cloud
  provider) is not stated (T5-7). The Claude fact is optional: drop it if time is tight.
