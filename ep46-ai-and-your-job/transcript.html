The verdict

NiaNeither team wins this one. From me: what's settled, what survived from each side, and one question that's yours to check.

NiaSettled: so far there's no mass unemployment. Layoffs run about one percent of jobs a month, unemployment has held a narrow band since March, and productivity is on its long-run trend. Even the loudest warning on the alarm side agrees it isn't happening at scale yet.

NiaWhat survived from green. Fewer young people are being hired into the most exposed jobs, nineteen percent behind and widening. Where AI does the task, some whole jobs are shrinking. And history says the people already in the job, and sometimes the next generation at the door, pay.

NiaWhat survived from gold. Nobody has shown AI caused the gap, and its own authors say so. Work has moved between job titles before, new kinds of jobs arrived, and the national numbers show no break.

NiaThree claims didn't survive. That AI is causing mass unemployment today. That the word AI on a layoff proves AI did the work, or that it didn't. And that cheaper work always makes more jobs: sometimes it does, for a while, when demand is hungry.

NiaSo the question isn't 'will I be laid off by AI?' It's 'is the bottom rung in my field still being hired?' First way to check: watch the door, not the exit, in junior job postings and new-hire numbers. Job ads only show openings, not hires, and two careful studies of the same data disagree.

NiaSecond: read what your employer tells investors, not just the memo, knowing the label can run either way. Third: read the state's numbers. California's tracker shows exposure, not cause, and from January its quarterly summary of layoff notices will show what employers chose to say.

NiaIf the bottom rung in your field is still hiring, that's the best signal the record can give you today. If it isn't, that's worth knowing before anyone writes it on a memo. Whether that door is closing or moving is yours to watch.
