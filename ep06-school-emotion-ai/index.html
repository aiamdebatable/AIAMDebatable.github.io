AI in Schools: Should Emotion-Recognition Be Banned, or Bound?

The question: Should schools be prohibited from using AI emotion-recognition surveillance on students?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

The science says it can't do what it claims — so what do we do about it?

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in August 2026, emotion-reading AI had already been pointed at classrooms, and its market, roughly three billion dollars in 2024, was projected to more than triple by 2030. Europe had banned it in schools, but enforcement was young and the tech kept rebranding.

The fight

Green arguesGuardrails, Not Bans

The emotion reading on offer isn't reliable. But a blanket ban sweeps up things worth keeping: safety uses, and tools built with and for autistic students. Even Europe kept a medical and safety exception. Draw a hard line at emotion inference on kids, keep the narrow uses, and enforce it with consent, transparency and audits.

Gold arguesBan the Mind-Reader

The tool doesn't reliably work, and it's aimed at children who must sit in front of it and can't opt out. It scored Black faces as angrier for the same expression, and students under the cameras began policing their own faces. Britain's warning didn't stop it spreading. Europe banned it outright.

What both sides quietly agree on

The science is unusually one-sided. A large review found you can't reliably read emotion off a face, the tools misfire across skin tones, and they're aimed at kids who can't opt out. Neither team defended the idea that a camera reliably knows what a child feels.

What the shouting gets wrong

The usual fight is whether it works, and on the science that's nearly settled: the versions on offer mostly don't. The trouble is that it's sold and deployed anyway. So the real question is the remedy for a shaky tool aimed at kids who can't say no: ban it outright, or write hard rules that allow a few narrow uses.

The verdict

Watched it already? Read the verdict as it aired

Ryan So where does that leave us? Start with what is not in dispute.

Ryan The science here is unusually one-sided. A large review found you can't reliably read emotion off a face, the tools misfire across skin tones, and they're aimed at kids who can't opt out. Both sides accepted all of it.

Ryan And notice what both sides agree on. Neither one defends the idea that a camera reliably knows what a child feels. Nobody in this debate is actually selling the mind-reader.

Ryan So the fight isn't about the science. It's about the remedy. Prohibit it, or bind it?

Ryan The case for guardrails says a blunt ban sweeps up real safety and accessibility uses, that the line is genuinely hard to draw, and that transparency beats a prohibition you can't enforce, though it leans on a narrow exception that has a habit of widening.

Ryan The case for a ban says an unproven tool aimed at captive kids doesn't earn a narrow door, that guardrails go unenforced, and that a major government already prohibited it, though a ban may sweep up genuine clinical and accessibility tools along with it.

Ryan And here's what both sides quietly conceded, without ever meaning to: the current technology doesn't work, and children can't consent. They only disagree on whether that calls for a wall, or a fence.

Ryan So here's the charge, and you're the jury. That the science is shaky and the kids are captive is settled. Whether the answer is a ban or a boundary, that is yours to weigh. You've heard both cases. Now you decide.
