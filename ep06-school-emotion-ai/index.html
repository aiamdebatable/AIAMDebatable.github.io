AI in Schools: Should Emotion-Recognition Be Banned, or Bound?

Should schools be prohibited from using AI emotion-recognition surveillance on students?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

The science says it can't do what it claims — so what do we do about it?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in August 2026, emotion-reading AI had already been pointed at classrooms, and its market, roughly three billion dollars in 2024, was projected to more than triple by 2030. Europe had banned it in schools, but enforcement was young and the tech kept rebranding.

Settled

What both sides accept

The science is unusually one-sided. A large review found you can't reliably read emotion off a face, the tools misfire across skin tones, and they're aimed at kids who can't opt out. Neither team defended the idea that a camera reliably knows what a child feels.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesGuardrails, Not Bans

The emotion reading on offer isn't reliable. But a blanket ban sweeps up things worth keeping: safety uses, and tools built with and for autistic students. Even Europe kept a medical and safety exception. Draw a hard line at emotion inference on kids, keep the narrow uses, and enforce it with consent, transparency and audits.

Gold arguesBan the Mind-Reader

The tool doesn't reliably work, and it's aimed at children who must sit in front of it and can't opt out. It scored Black faces as angrier for the same expression, and students under the cameras began policing their own faces. Britain's warning didn't stop it spreading. Europe banned it outright.

What the shouting gets wrong

The usual fight is whether it works, and on the science that's nearly settled: the versions on offer mostly don't. The trouble is that it's sold and deployed anyway. So the real question is the remedy for a shaky tool aimed at kids who can't say no: ban it outright, or write hard rules that allow a few narrow uses.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: For a tool that can't reliably read emotion off a face, aimed at kids who can't opt out: a ban, or a boundary?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1A ban
    What it buys
    The case for a ban says an unproven tool aimed at captive kids doesn't earn a narrow door, that guardrails go unenforced, and that a major government already prohibited it. Europe banned emotion recognition in schools as an unacceptable practice.
    What it costs
    A ban may sweep up genuine clinical and accessibility tools along with it. Even Europe's ban kept an exception for genuine medical or safety uses.
    Not measured
    Whether Europe's ban works in practice: it is on the books, but enforcement is young and untested, and the tech keeps rebranding.

    On the record: entry 1 in the fact-check log: EU AI Act Article 5(1)(f) prohibits AI emotion inference in education; in force 2 Feb 2025; medical/safety exception kept; plain expressions + pain/fatigue excluded, entry 2 in the fact-check log: Barrett et al. (2019), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*: reviewed 1,000+ studies; concluded you can't reliably infer emotion from facial expression, entry 3 in the fact-check log: Human Rights Watch (2022): reviewed 164 ed-tech products; ~89% (145 of 163) could surveil children, often with no opt-out, entry 4 in the fact-check log: Accessibility research: emotion-recognition tools built with/for autistic learners exist

  2. 2A boundary
    What it buys
    The case for guardrails says a blunt ban sweeps up real safety and accessibility uses, that the line is genuinely hard to draw, and that transparency beats a prohibition you can't enforce. Europe itself had to carve out plain expressions, a smile or a frown, and physical states like pain and fatigue.
    What it costs
    It leans on a narrow exception that has a habit of widening. Gold's warning: once the cameras are installed for safety, the same feed can be repurposed for engagement, discipline or grading.
    Not measured
    The record confirms only that emotion tools built with and for autistic learners exist, not how well they work.

    On the record: entry 1 in the fact-check log: EU AI Act Article 5(1)(f) prohibits AI emotion inference in education; in force 2 Feb 2025; medical/safety exception kept; plain expressions + pain/fatigue excluded, entry 2 in the fact-check log: Accessibility research: emotion-recognition tools built with/for autistic learners exist, entry 3 in the fact-check log: UK ICO (2022) called emotion analysis "immature," warned it "may not work yet, or indeed ever"

Whichever you pick: Both sides conceded that the current technology doesn't work, and children can't consent. They only disagree on whether that calls for a wall, or a fence.
