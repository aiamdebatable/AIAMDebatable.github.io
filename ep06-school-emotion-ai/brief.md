# Research brief — "AI emotion-recognition in schools: ban it, or bound it?"

_The full research behind the episode. Every figure traces to `sources.md` and is verified in
`factcheck-log.md`. The EU prohibition is in force but enforcement is young — treat status as current._

## The question
Should schools be legally **prohibited** from deploying AI that claims to infer students' emotions,
attention, or engagement from faces/voices/behavior — or should such systems be **permitted under
narrow rules**?

- **Side A — "Guardrails, Not Bans":** a blunt ban sweeps up genuine safety and accessibility uses;
  write precise rules and enforce them.
- **Side B — "Ban the Mind-Reader":** an unproven tool aimed at children who can't opt out should be
  prohibited outright — as the EU already did.

## Topic-shape verdict (honest — and asymmetric)
This is **not symmetric on the science**: the strongest expert review concludes you cannot reliably
read a specific emotion off a face, so the "does it work" question leans hard one way. That means the
honest debate is **not** "does it work" — it's the **policy** question: does invalid-but-deployed tech
aimed at captive minors warrant an *outright prohibition* or *bounded rules*? Both sides largely concede
the science is weak; they disagree on the remedy and on where to draw the "emotion recognition" line.

## Settled facts (not the debate)
1. **What it is / where it's deployed.** Emotion/affect recognition claims to infer inner states from
   biometric signals. In education: engagement monitoring (e.g. Intel + Classroom Technologies on
   Zoom), exam proctoring (gaze/movement flags), and classroom cameras (one Chinese school scanned
   faces ~every 30 seconds into ~7 categories). The emotion-AI market was ~$2.7B in 2024, projected to
   more than triple by 2030.
2. **Who's affected.** Minors in compulsory settings who can't meaningfully consent. Human Rights Watch
   reviewed 164 ed-tech products and found ~89% could surveil children, often with no opt-out. Emotion
   AI also misreads by skin tone — commercial tools scored Black faces as angrier/more contemptuous
   than white faces, even when smiling.
3. **The science that cuts deepest.** Barrett et al. (2019), a review of 1,000+ studies in *Psychological
   Science in the Public Interest*, concluded facial movements are **not** sufficiently reliable or
   specific to diagnose emotional state. The UK ICO called emotion analysis "immature" and warned it
   "may not work yet, or indeed ever."
4. **Two loaded terms.** *Emotion recognition vs. engagement detection* — vendors rebrand emotion
   inference as neutral "attention analytics," but a face/voice biometric standing in for an inner
   state carries the same flaw. *Accuracy vs. construct validity* — a system can be 95% accurate at
   classifying a facial *configuration* and still be measuring the wrong thing (a smile ≠ happiness).

## The contested core (both steelmanned — as POLICY)
**Guardrails, Not Bans:** even the EU's ban keeps a medical/safety exception and excludes plain
expressions and pain/fatigue — proof the concept is hard to define and a blunt ban overreaches. Real
accessibility tools (built with/for autistic learners) would be swept up. The honest tool for immature
tech is consent + transparency + audits (the ICO pointed to data-protection rules, not a criminal ban).
And a ban here just exports the practice to places with no rules. *Caveat: leans on a narrow safety
exception that tends to widen.*

**Ban the Mind-Reader:** the tool doesn't reliably work, full stop; it's aimed at captive minors who
can't opt out (~89% of ed-tech can already surveil); it misreads by skin tone (discrimination,
automated); it chills behavior (students self-policed expression under classroom cameras); guardrails
go unenforced; and a major government already banned it as an *unacceptable* practice (fines up to
€35M). *Caveat: a blunt ban can sweep up genuine clinical and accessibility tools.*

## The reframe
Not a debate: whether current face/voice emotion-*inference* is scientifically valid (the review says
no). The real fight is the **policy remedy** for invalid-but-deployed tech on captive minors — outright
prohibition vs. bounded rules — and the definitional knife-edge of where "emotion recognition" ends and
"engagement analytics" or clinical/accessibility tools begin.

## Both quietly agree
Neither side defends "a camera reliably reads a 14-year-old's true feelings." Both concede the current
tech isn't reliable, both accept the demographic bias is real, and both want consent/transparency/limits
on surveillance of minors. The whole disagreement collapses to one word: **prohibition vs. regulation**
— a wall, or a fence. They're arguing about the lock, not whether the mind-reader currently works.

## Verdict framing (no winner)
The science jury is largely in: today's emotion-from-face inference can't reliably do what it claims,
especially across skin tones — and one major jurisdiction has already outlawed it in schools. The open
question for the viewer: when a technology is *both* scientifically shaky *and* aimed at children who
can't opt out, is the right answer a hard **prohibition** (that risks sweeping up genuine safety and
accessibility tools) or **bounded rules** (that risk letting a discredited "mind-reader" in through a
side door labeled "engagement")? **You're the jury.**
