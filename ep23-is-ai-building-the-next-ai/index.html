Intelligence Explosion: Is AI Building the Next AI — and Would We Know?

Is AI building the next AI, and would we know?

The labs say AI now does most of their code and some of their research. Every number counts effort. Would we know if it counted results?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in October 2026, 22 researchers from OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft and universities had just warned, on September 28, that AI was on track to automate most of the work of AI research within a few years. They added it could happen without anyone outside being able to see it.

Settled

What both sides accept

Inside the labs, AI was already doing much of the routine work, the code, the runs and the experiments, while people still chose the direction. No lab says the loop is closed. And no outsider has checked any lab's internal number.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesCount the results

Every lab number counts effort, like OpenAI's three agent workdays per human workday or Anthropic's four lines of code in five, and no outsider has checked them. Where results are measured, the chess engine and a famous maths record showed no speed-up. And in a 2025 trial, developers felt faster with AI while taking 19% longer.

▶ Hear Green at 4:20 on YouTube
Gold arguesWatch the curve

The one clock no lab controls, the length of task an AI agent can finish, doubles about every four months and has run past its ruler. On a set research problem, AI agents closed almost all of a gap two researchers closed a quarter of. Results move last, so treat the slope as the warning.

▶ Hear Gold at 9:29 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

Asking whether AI is building AI, yes or no, is the wrong question. The numbers both sides quote count effort, nobody disputes the effort, and the fight is about results nobody publishes. No rule anywhere makes a lab publish how much of its research AI does, so we'd know only what the labs choose to show.

The verdict

What the record supports
Is AI building AI, yes or no, is the wrong question. The numbers both sides quote count effort, and nobody here disputes the effort. The fight is about results nobody publishes.
What didn't hold up
Green's comfort: no results measure is published, so nothing has bent is partly nobody can look. Gold's alarm: the outside estimate came with no evidence shown and the lab's own edits, and the jobs record has other suspects. Remote work, interest rates, the hangover from pandemic hiring.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: Which number would change your mind, and who's grading it?

The judge named four numbers and declared no winner. You could watch more than one. Open one to see what the record says it offers, its catch, and what nobody has measured. The judge's second dial is yours alone: in your own job, is the part that's direction growing, or shrinking? Nothing is saved or counted.

  • METR's time horizon
    What it offers
    An outsider runs it, and it's the one clock no lab controls. It measures how long a task an AI agent can finish half the time, and since 2023 that has doubled about every 4 months.
    The catch
    Its ruler has run out: the latest measurement is past the 16 hours METR says its tasks can reliably measure. And it measures how long a task an agent can finish, not whether research is getting faster.
    Not measured
    Whether longer tasks mean faster research: nobody publishes a measure of what the labs' research actually produces.

    On the record: “Time-horizon doubling ~4 months; latest horizon” in the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), “METR limitations” in the fact-check log, “METR's independence” in the fact-check log

  • A lab's own share of research done by AI
    What it offers
    The numbers that would tell us are inside the labs. Anthropic says Claude now leads about a quarter of its AI research tasks, up from under 1% in February. And OpenAI itself says labs should be required to track their progress publicly.
    The catch
    Graded by the lab: the judge deciding what counts as leading is another Claude model. Every headline lab number counts effort, like agent hours and lines of code, and OpenAI calls numbers like these relatively easy to gather, but hard to interpret.
    Not measured
    No outsider has checked any lab's internal number, and we found no rule anywhere that makes a lab publish it.

    On the record: “Claude "leads" 26 % of Anthropic's AI R&D” in the fact-check log, “Any outside audit of Anthropic's figures” in the fact-check log, “Compute confound; call for mandatory public tracking” in the fact-check log, “"Easy to gather, but hard to interpret"” in the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), “What rules require” in the fact-check log

  • Records nobody controls
    What it offers
    Honest: no lab controls the scoreboard. So far, the leading open-source chess engine shows no sign of acceleration, and neither does a famous maths record. Reported security flaws in one widely used program did jump, from 9 to 36.
    The catch
    Slow: waiting for results means waiting for the last signal to move. And the labs publish no results measure, so a flat record is partly that nobody can look.
    Not measured
    What the labs' research actually produces: nobody publishes it.

    On the record: “METR discoveries note” in the fact-check log, “AI Scientist evaluation; open-ended case study” in the fact-check log

  • The job postings in your own field
    What it offers
    Closest to home. The bottom rung shrank most in the jobs AI can do: on Indeed, only about 4.5% of software job postings were entry-level in the first three months of this year, and employment of 22 to 25 year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs fell about 11%, while the same age group grew about 10% in less-exposed jobs.
    The catch
    Nobody can yet say why they move. The jobs record has other suspects: remote work, interest rates, the hangover from pandemic hiring. Economists at the New York Fed found junior and senior postings moving together, and Yale's Budget Lab says the overall data shows no clear disruption from AI.
    Not measured
    Whether AI is the cause: the payroll studies say their patterns aren't proof of it.

    On the record: “Indeed: entry-level 4.5 % of software postings, senior…” in the fact-check log, “Stanford Canaries: 19 % below counterfactual; −11 %…” in the fact-check log, “NY Fed postings: no junior/senior divergence; remote work…” in the fact-check log, “EIG "young people of all types"; Yale "does…” in the fact-check log

What our fact-check caught

9 of the 52 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidMETR's latest measure put a top Claude model at tasks of about 4 hours 49 minutes.

    The record saysThat figure had been replaced, and it wasn't the latest. The latest measurement, an early version of Anthropic's Mythos model, came out at about 17 hours.

  2. Draft saidAnthropic now produces 8 times more code.

    The record saysThat figure mixes three different measures on one page, and Anthropic itself says it almost certainly overstates the real gain in productivity.

  3. Draft saidIndeed reported entry-level software job postings down 28%.

    The record saysThat number isn't in any Indeed post. Indeed's figure is that about one software posting in twenty-two was entry-level in early 2026.
