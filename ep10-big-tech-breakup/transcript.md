## The research · Three countries, three answers, right now
**Kate:** For about twenty years the line has been that somebody should break up Big Tech. In the last two years, courts actually started answering.
**Ryan:** Answering how? Did anyone win?
**Kate:** The government won on liability, twice. A federal judge found Google's search business an illegal monopoly, and a different judge found its ad tech business one too.
**Ryan:** So they lost. That's the headline.
**Kate:** That's the liability. The remedy is a separate fight, and that's where it turns. In September last year the search judge refused to break anything off.
**Ryan:** Refused what exactly?
**Kate:** The government asked him to force Google to sell Chrome, with Android held in reserve. He declined both, and ordered behaviour changes instead — no exclusive deals, and Google has to share parts of its search index and user data with competitors.
**Ryan:** And the other case?
**Kate:** Two months later a third judge threw the Meta case out entirely. Instagram and WhatsApp stay where they are. The agency has appealed.
**Ryan:** So three big cases, no breakups.
**Kate:** And the ad tech remedy still hasn't come. Closing arguments finished in November of last year. It's been pending more than nine months.
**Ryan:** That's the American story. Is anyone doing it differently?
**Kate:** Two other places are running two other answers, right now. Europe wrote one rulebook for everyone. Britain does something else entirely — it names a specific company and writes rules for that company alone.
**Ryan:** Hold on. Three different countries, three different methods, all running at the same time? That's not a debate any more, that's an experiment.

## The research · "Break them up" is three different policies
**Kate:** It is. And the ground that genuinely isn't in dispute starts with the phrase itself.
**Kate:** Break them up isn't one thing. It's at least three, and they have almost nothing in common.
**Ryan:** Three?
**Kate:** One: divestiture. Force the company to sell something — the ad exchange, or Instagram. Two: conduct rules. Let it keep everything, but tell it how to behave. Three: structural separation. You may run the marketplace or sell on it, not both.
**Ryan:** Different costs, different evidence, different ways of failing.
**Kate:** Completely different. And almost no public argument about this says which one it means.
**Ryan:** Alright. What's the evidence on whether any of them work?
**Kate:** The most-cited case in the whole argument is the breakup of the phone company in nineteen eighty-four. And it is quoted by both sides, because it says two things at once.
**Ryan:** Go on.
**Kate:** After the breakup, total American telecoms patenting went up about nineteen percent. But that increase came from companies that had nothing to do with the old phone company.
**Ryan:** And the pieces of the old company itself?
**Kate:** Their own patenting fell. Same paper, same table. If you're counting the industry it worked, and if you're counting the company it didn't.

## The research · It depends entirely on where the cut falls
**Ryan:** So we've got one famous case that answers both ways. Is there anything systematic?
**Kate:** There is, and it's the finding I'd put in front of anyone arguing about this. The competition regulator studied its own divestiture orders — eighty-nine of them, across six years.
**Ryan:** And?
**Kate:** Divestitures that handed over a whole working business generally succeeded. Divestitures that handed over a package of assets generally struggled.
**Ryan:** Say that again, because that sounds like it decides something.
**Kate:** Selling a going concern works. Carving a piece out of a machine and hoping it runs on its own mostly doesn't.
**Ryan:** So apply that. Spinning off Instagram is —
**Kate:** A whole working business. Its own users, its own staff, its own revenue. Whereas the ad exchange the government wants sold is a component wired into a stack.
**Ryan:** Those are completely different operations, and everyone calls both of them a breakup.
**Kate:** Which is the whole problem with the phrase. And I have to flag the limit of that study honestly: it's about unwinding mergers, not about breaking up a standing monopoly.
**Ryan:** So it's not evidence about the thing we're actually arguing.
**Kate:** It's the best systematic evidence anyone has, and it is not evidence about this. Both of those are true, and neither side gets to spend it as if only one were.
**Ryan:** That's the most useful thing anyone's said about this argument so far.

## The research · The rulings don't say what the argument says
**Kate:** Last of the settled ground, and it's the part that changed my mind about the whole file.
**Ryan:** Which is?
**Kate:** That the rulings don't say what everybody says they say. We read both opinions end to end — the search one runs two hundred and thirty pages.
**Ryan:** And what's in them?
**Kate:** The search judge didn't rule that breakups are bad policy. He ruled the government asked for the wrong thing. His words: the plaintiffs overreached in seeking these assets, which Google did not use to effect any illegal restraints.
**Ryan:** Meaning Chrome wasn't the weapon.
**Kate:** Right. The violation was in the distribution contracts. He wouldn't take assets that weren't the instrument. That's a scope holding, not a verdict on breaking up companies.
**Ryan:** And the Meta case?
**Kate:** That one's even narrower. The agency has to prove a company is violating the law now, or about to. It argued past harm that's still doing damage should count. The judge said that might be a sensible scheme, but it is not the one that Congress passed.
**Ryan:** So it lost on the statute. Not on whether buying Instagram was bad for competition.
**Kate:** Not on that at all. And both sides quote these rulings as proof that breakups don't work. They're proof of what these plaintiffs proved, under which law.
**Ryan:** That's a completely different claim, and I'd never have known from the coverage.

## The debate · Team Green · A BET NOBODY CAN PRICE
**Andre:** I want to argue about time, because everything on the other side of this is a claim about a future nobody has seen.
**Andre:** Start with the thing my opponent needs. The phone company breakup raised industry patenting about nineteen percent. I accept that. It's the best historical evidence there is.
**Andre:** Now the half that gets left off. The broken-up company's own research output fell. Same paper, same table. So the honest version is that the industry gained and the firm lost, and which of those you call success is a choice, not a finding.
**Andre:** Second: irreversibility. You cannot un-break a company. A divestiture takes years, the appeal takes more, and if it was the wrong cut there is no putting it back.
**Andre:** Third, and this is the one I'd sit with. The judge who spent two hundred and thirty pages on this said the remedy question asked him to gaze into a crystal ball and look to the future, and that this was not exactly a judge's forte.
**Andre:** That's not a technicality. That's the person with the most evidence anyone has assembled saying the counterfactual is not knowable.
**Andre:** Fourth: there is a cheaper answer already running. Last week one of these companies agreed to roughly seventeen billion dollars and rebuilt its product for every minor in the country. No structure was touched.
**Andre:** And I'll say the weakness in that myself. That was a child-safety case, not an antitrust one. Different law, and I'll own that.
**Andre:** But it does show that a company's behaviour can be changed enormously without cutting it into pieces.
**Andre:** My opponent will say conduct rules are a subscription you pay forever. She's right that they need policing.
**Andre:** I'd rather pay for supervision I can adjust than make one irreversible cut in the dark.

## The debate · Team Gold · STRUCTURE IS THE CURE
**Mara:** Let me grant his best point first, because I think he's right about it. You cannot un-break a company, and the counterfactual is genuinely unknowable.
**Mara:** My argument is that we are not choosing between a known thing and an unknown one. We are choosing between two bets, and only one of them has already been run.
**Mara:** Start with Europe, because it's the cleanest result in the file. Twenty-six months of a rule requiring that rival browser engines be permitted on the iPhone, and zero have shipped.
**Mara:** I'll say the honest part myself. The people documenting that campaign for the ban to end, so read their explanation with that in mind. But the fact isn't disputed by anyone, including the company.
**Mara:** Second: a conduct rule needs somebody watching it forever. The search remedy comes with a technical committee overseeing compliance. That's not a fix, that's a subscription — and it expires.
**Mara:** Third, and this is where the structure argument actually bites. When one firm runs the exchange, the ad server and the buying side, no rule about fair behaviour removes the conflict. The conflict is the org chart.
**Mara:** And I'll take the phone company case, including the part that hurts me. Yes, the broken-up firm's own patenting fell.
**Mara:** But total industry patenting rose about nineteen percent, and it rose because of companies that had nothing to do with the old monopoly. That is exactly what unlocking a market is supposed to look like.
**Mara:** If you only count the incumbent, you will never count the thing a breakup is for.
**Mara:** And Britain is now testing the middle. It names the company and writes rules for it alone — including on browser engines, the precise thing Europe's general rule failed to move.
**Mara:** I'm not asking for a blind cut. I'm asking that we stop pretending the careful option has no results yet, when one of them has twenty-six months and nothing to show.

## The debate · Team Green responds
**Andre:** The browser engine result is her best card. Twenty-six months and nothing shipped is a real number.
**Andre:** What it shows is that one badly-designed conduct rule failed. It doesn't show that structure would have worked, because nobody ran that arm of the experiment.
**Andre:** And Britain proves my point better than hers. That's a third conduct regime, started this year, aimed at the same target. If conduct were hopeless, nobody serious would be building a new one.
**Andre:** She's right that a supervised remedy has to be watched. I'd rather watch something than break something.
**Andre:** But I have to concede her strongest line. If one firm runs all three sides of a market, no rule about fairness dissolves that conflict. On that specific structure, I think she's got the better argument.

## The verdict
**Nia:** This one doesn't resolve, and I want to be precise about where it stops rather than call it a draw.
**Nia:** Both teams accepted the same historical evidence, including the half that hurt them. Industry patenting rose about nineteen percent after the phone company breakup, and the broken-up firm's own research output fell. Neither debater disputed either number.
**Nia:** So the disagreement isn't about what happened. It's about what you are counting — the industry, or the company. That is a values question wearing an evidence question's clothes.
**Nia:** Both also conceded their weak flank. Green conceded that where one firm runs every side of a market, no fairness rule dissolves the conflict. Gold conceded the counterfactual is unknowable and a cut cannot be undone.
**Nia:** The case for waiting rests on irreversibility, and on the judge with the most evidence saying the future was not his to see.
**Nia:** The case for cutting rests on twenty-six months of a conduct rule that produced nothing, and on a market where the conflict is the org chart.
**Nia:** And the thing both cases are downstream of is a phrase that hides three different policies. The best systematic evidence says a whole business survives being sold and a component usually doesn't — so the answer depends on where the cut falls, and almost nobody says where.
**Nia:** So the question we're handing you isn't whether to break up Big Tech. It's which cut you'd actually make, and whether you'd accept being wrong about it permanently. That one's yours.
