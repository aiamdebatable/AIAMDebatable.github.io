✅ CONFIRMED — the pattern the episode rests on

2. US v. Google (search) — monopoly found, Chrome divestiture refused

CONFIRMED, with the date now exact. Judge Amit Mehta issued a 230-page remedies ruling on 3 September 2025. He denied the government's request to force divestiture of Chrome — and of Android — holding the proposed divestitures went beyond the scope of a liability ruling focused on search distribution agreements. He imposed behavioural remedies instead: no exclusive distribution contracts, plus mandated sharing of user-interaction data and the search index with competitors. ⚠ One brief detail refined: the brief says he "rejected a blanket ban on Google's payments to Apple." That is consistent with reporting — the remedy bars exclusivity, not the payments themselves — but the "would harm partners and consumers" characterisation is a paraphrase and should be quoted from the opinion, not from coverage, before it is spoken.

SourcesCRS LSB11362 (Congress.gov) · Hughes Hubbard client alert · Public Knowledge

3. FTC v. Meta — dismissed, and the quote is verbatim

CONFIRMED, with the date now exact. On 18 November 2025, after a six-week bench trial, Judge James Boasberg found the FTC failed to prove Meta currently holds monopoly power in personal social networking, citing competition from TikTok and YouTube. The brief's quoted phrase — "changed markedly" — is verbatim from the opinion as reported. Instagram and WhatsApp stay. The FTC appealed in January 2026.

SourcesCRS LSB11379 (Congress.gov) · Skadden · Sullivan & Cromwell · CNBC

4. US v. Google (ad tech) — liability found, remedy still pending

CONFIRMED. Judge Leonie Brinkema (E.D. Va.) found Google unlawfully monopolised publisher ad servers and ad exchanges. Remedies testimony ran late September 2025; closing arguments concluded 21 November 2025. DOJ sought divestiture of AdX (and DFP if that proved insufficient). A targeted search on 2026-08-30 surfaced no ruling — it remains pending, ~9 months after closings. ⚠ The brief's "leaning away from a breakup" is reporting on courtroom remarks, not a holding. Keep it flagged that way or drop it — this is the same class of claim as the Penske remarks below.

SourcesDigiday · AdExchanger · NatLawReview · Public Knowledge

The FTC and 17 state AGs allege Amazon holds durable monopoly power in an "online superstore" market at an estimated ~82% share, plus a second "online marketplace services" market. ⚠ It is the FTC's pleaded figure, not a found fact — nothing has been tried. CONFIRMED, and the brief's figure is incomplete. 7. The DMA browser-engine null result

CONFIRMED. More than 26 months after Apple's Article 5(7) obligation took effect on 7 March 2024, no browser vendor has shipped its own engine on iOS in the EU. Cited barriers: vendors must ship an entirely separate app (abandoning existing EU users), and Apple has not confirmed security patching for EU users travelling outside the EU beyond 30 days. ⛔ DISCLOSE THE SOURCE'S INTEREST ON AIR, as ep08 did with the landlord-commissioned report. The load-bearing documentation is Open Web Advocacy, a campaigning organisation whose stated purpose is ending this ban. The underlying fact (no engine has shipped) is independently corroborated and undisputed, including by Apple; the causal attribution to Apple's contract terms is OWA's contention. Those are two different claims and the script must not merge them.

SourcesOpen Web Advocacy · AppleInsider · Michael Tsai (blog aggregation)

11. "The Bell breakup increased telecom innovation" AND "the Bell breakup reduced it"

✅ CONFIRMED — BOTH, and they are one finding from one paper. Watzinger & Schnitzer, The Breakup of the Bell System and its Impact on US Innovation, 5 September 2022. Full text downloaded and extracted (74pp); abstract quoted verbatim in the brief.

"After the breakup, the scale and diversity of telecommunications innovation increased. Total patenting by US inventors related to telecommunications increased by 19%, driven by companies unrelated to the Bell System. Patenting by Bell's successor companies decreased, but not the number of top inventions."

⛔ NEITHER SIDE MAY QUOTE HALF OF IT. This is the most-cited historical analogy in the debate and it does not resolve cleanly for anyone. A script that gives one camp "+19%" without the successor decline, or the other the decline without the +19%, is doing the exact thing this show exists to catch. ⚠ Status: working-paper version dated Sep 2022 obtained from the author's own site. Not confirmed as peer-reviewed/published — treat the venue as unestablished until checked.

12. "Divestitures work — or don't"

✅ CONFIRMED, and the honest statement is conditional. FTC, Merger Remedies 2006–2012: A Report of the Bureaus of Competition and Economics (issued Feb 2017): 89 merger orders, 79 divestitures, 121 buyers, case-study method built on responses from buyers, respondents, competitors and customers. Finding: divestitures of ongoing businesses generally succeeded in maintaining or restoring competition, while buyers of limited asset packages were more likely to struggle.

⚠ SOURCING CAVEAT, stated plainly: ftc.gov returned HTTP 403 to automated fetching, so the report's own PDF was not read. The figures above come from a law-journal analysis that names the report and its numbers precisely (Skadden, A Moment of Repose: The FTC's Merger Remedy Study, NYLJ vol. 257 no. 48, 14 Mar 2017), extracted from PDF. Secondary, not primary. Before any of these numbers goes on screen, pull the FTC report itself.

⛔ AND THE EXTRAPOLATION IS THE BIGGER PROBLEM. This is evidence about merger remedies — unwinding a proposed combination — not about breaking up a standing monopolist. It is the best systematic evidence that exists on whether divestitures work, and it is not evidence about the thing this episode is arguing about. The brief says so and the script must too.

⚠ A separate academic line reports divested assets "probably or likely viable" for 2–3 years in only ~56% of cases (implying >44% failure). NOT independently verified in this pass — surfaced in search results only, no paper identified. Do not use without finding the source.

13. "The consumer-welfare debate is partly definitional"

CONFIRMED as characterisation, not as a number. Multiple economists writing on the standard note that the two camps use different definitions of "consumer welfare" and frequently argue past each other; neo-Brandeisians target harms to workers, suppliers and democratic structure that can occur with no consumer price effect, while defenders answer that a standard counting everything constrains nothing. ⚠ This is a framing claim supported by commentary (ABA Antitrust magazine, ProMarket, Cato, GMU Law Review), not an empirical finding. Present it as the shape of the disagreement, never as a result.

14. The anti-breakup efficiency case

CONFIRMED as the argument, UNRESOLVED as a magnitude. Vertical integration is claimed to be where consumer benefit originates (Prime, Maps, free search, cross-service integration), so cutting in the wrong place destroys efficiencies and raises prices. ⚠ No peer-reviewed estimate of the consumer cost of any specific proposed tech breakup was located in this pass. That absence is itself substantive — it is Contested #3's counterfactual problem — but it must be reported as "not found", not as "does not exist".

17. ✅ Mehta's reasoning — the paraphrase is replaced by the words

CONFIRMED, and sharper than the paraphrase. Round 1 flagged "would harm partners and consumers" as needing quotation. Verbatim:

"Google will not be required to divest Chrome; nor will the court include a contingent divestiture of the Android operating system in the final judgment. Plaintiffs overreached in seeking forced divesture of these key assets, which Google did not use to effect any illegal restraints." "Cutting off payments from Google almost certainly will impose substantial—in some cases, crippling—downstream harms to distribution partners, related markets, and consumers, which counsels against a broad payment ban." "…here the court is asked to gaze into a crystal ball and look to the future. Not exactly a judge's forte."

★ The last line is the episode's Contested #3 counterfactual problem, stated by the judge, in the opinion refusing the breakup. ⚠ A correction to this brief's own earlier wording: rounds 1–2 said the divestitures "went beyond the scope of a liability ruling focused on search distribution agreements." The opinion's actual ground is narrower and better — the assets were not used to commit the violation.

18. ✅ The affirmative remedy, primary

CONFIRMED. Google must make available to Qualified Competitors "certain search index and user-interaction data, though not ads data, as such sharing will deny Google the fruits of its exclusionary acts"; the court narrowed the datasets to tailor the remedy to the conduct; and Google "shall offer Qualified Competitors search and search text ads syndication services."

19. ★ ⛔ MATERIAL FINDING — FTC v. Meta turned on a STATUTORY limit, not on the economics

CONFIRMED, and it changes what the case can be used to argue. ✅ Primary: No. 20-3590 (JEB), Memorandum Opinion, 89pp. The FTC's authority under Section 13(b), 15 U.S.C. § 53(b), reaches a corporation that "is violating, or is about to violate" the law; courts read it as serving a "forward-facing role: enjoining ongoing and imminent future violations." The FTC argued past harm still lingering should suffice. The court:

"That might be a sensible statutory scheme, but it is not the one that Congress passed."

⛔ Every secondary source in rounds 1–2 said "the FTC failed to prove monopoly power" — true, and it buries the reason. The case says almost nothing about whether unwinding Instagram would have been sound policy. Do not let either debater cite it as evidence that breakups are bad.

20. ✅ "changed markedly" — verbatim, with its real context

CONFIRMED. The phrase is genuine and opens the opinion, but its subject is market definition, not Meta's competitive position generally:

"…the Greek philosopher Heraclitus posited that no man can ever step into the same river twice… The landscape that existed only five years ago when the Federal Trade Commission brought this antitrust suit has changed markedly. While it once might have made sense to partition apps into separate markets of social networking and social media, that wall has since broken down."

⚠ Two versions exist: a redacted opinion signed 18 Nov 2025 and a revised opinion signed 2 Dec 2025. Quotations here are from the docket copy obtained. Note the distinction if a line is put on screen.
