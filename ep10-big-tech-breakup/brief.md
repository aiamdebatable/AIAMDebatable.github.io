## The question

**Should the tech giants be broken up — is it good antitrust or bad economics?**

## Why now

★ **THE PEG IS THE PATTERN, NOT ANY SINGLE CASE** — which is what makes it durable enough to render.

After twenty years of "someone should break up Big Tech," the courts finally answered, several
times, and **so far the answer is no.** Every US structural remedy sought against a tech giant in
this cycle has been refused, narrowed, or is still pending:

| date | case | outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Aug 2024** | *US v. Google* (search), Judge **Mehta** | Google **violated** the Sherman Act — an illegal search monopoly |
| **Apr 2025** | *US v. Google* (ad tech), Judge **Brinkema** | Google **unlawfully monopolised** publisher ad servers and ad exchanges |
| **2 Sep 2025** | *US v. Google* remedies, Mehta | ⛔ **Refused** divestiture of **Chrome — and Android**. Behavioural remedies instead: no exclusive distribution contracts, plus mandated sharing of user-interaction data and the search index |
| **18 Nov 2025** | *FTC v. Meta*, Judge **Boasberg** | ⛔ **Dismissed** after a six-week bench trial. The FTC failed to prove Meta *currently* holds monopoly power in personal social networking, citing TikTok and YouTube — the landscape had *"changed markedly."* **Instagram and WhatsApp stay** |
| **21 Nov 2025** | ad tech remedies | Closing arguments end. DOJ sought divestiture of **AdX**, and of **DFP** if that proved insufficient |
| **Jan 2026** | *FTC v. Meta* | FTC **appeals** to the D.C. Circuit |
| **Feb 2026** | *US v. Google* (search) | Behavioural remedies take effect; **DOJ cross-appeals**, asking the D.C. Circuit to revisit Chrome divestiture |
| **26 Aug 2026** | *In re social media* (29 states) | **Meta settles mid-trial** for up to **$16.68bn** plus mandated under-18 product changes. ⚠ Consumer-protection law, **not antitrust** |
| **31 Aug 2026** | ad tech remedies | ⏳ **Still pending** — over nine months after closings |

### ★ And a THIRD model is running right now — the one the prior research missed entirely

⛔ **This section did not exist until 2026-08-31, and its absence was the research failing in exactly
the documented way.** The brief covered the US (litigate structure) and the EU (one general
rulebook) and stopped. **The UK is running a third answer, and it is the one that bears hardest on
this episode's central question.**

Under the **Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024**, the CMA does not litigate a
breakup and does not write one rulebook for everyone. It **designates a named firm** with
**Strategic Market Status** — turnover over £1bn UK or £25bn global, plus "substantial and entrenched
market power" and strategic significance — and then writes **bespoke conduct requirements** for that
firm, for the purposes of *"fair dealing, open choices, or trust and transparency."*

| date | what |
|---|---|
| **Oct 2025** | **Google designated SMS** — general search and search advertising (~90% of UK general search queries) |
| **2026** | **Apple and Google designated SMS** for mobile platforms — operating systems, app stores, browsers and **browser engines** |
| **17 Jun 2026** | ⭐ **The first conduct requirement is imposed** — a **fair-ranking** rule on Google search |

★ **WHY THIS IS THE PEG AND NOT A FOOTNOTE.** Contested #1 asks whether a conduct remedy is a cure or
a subscription, and the strongest evidence that conduct rules underdeliver is the EU browser-engine
null — 26 months, no engine shipped. **The UK has designated Apple and Google over browser engines
specifically, using a different instrument.** The exact thing one conduct regime failed to move is
now being attempted by another. That is this episode's question running as a live comparison across
three jurisdictions, which no camp can wave away as theoretical.

⚠ **It is early and nobody should score it yet.** One conduct requirement has been imposed and its
effects are unmeasured. The honest claim is that the experiment is running, not that it worked.

⛔ **HOW IT WAS FOUND, because the method is the point.** *Strategic Market Status*, *DMCCA* and
*conduct requirement* appear **nowhere** in the prior research. They could not have been found by
searching this brief's vocabulary — the failure mode this show has recorded before. They surfaced from the peg-hunt
blind sweep, by naming a counterparty the brief never mentions.

⛔ **THE FORWARD DATE IN THIS SECTION WAS WRONG AND IS CORRECTED.** The scout pass had *FTC v.
Amazon* opening **13 October 2026** and built a two-to-three-week publishing deadline on it. The
trial has moved **twice** — 13 Oct 2026 → 9 Feb 2027 → **29 March 2027** (Judge John Chun, W.D.
Wash.) — after the court paused the case for the government shutdown on 1 Oct 2025, lifted 18 Nov,
and the parties then jointly moved it a further seven weeks. Amazon separately **lost** a bid to keep
the October date. **The deadline this topic was carrying does not exist.**

⚠ **And it has moved three times in ten months.** Do not put a trial date in the spoken script. Say
"2027"; the exact date belongs on screen and in the description, the only surface re-peggable after
publish.

## ⛔ THE RULINGS DO NOT SAY WHAT THE ARGUMENT SAYS THEY SAY

✅ **Both opinions read in full text, 2026-08-31** — Mehta 230pp (Doc. 1436, filed 09/02/25),
Boasberg 89pp (No. 20-3590 (JEB)). This section exists because reading them changed the episode.

The public argument treats this run of cases as *"the courts considered breaking up Big Tech and
decided it was a bad idea."* **Neither opinion says that.** Both turned on narrower grounds, and the
narrow grounds are the story.

**Mehta did not rule that divestiture is bad policy. He ruled the assets were not the instrument.**
Verbatim:

> *"Google will not be required to divest Chrome; nor will the court include a contingent divestiture
> of the Android operating system in the final judgment. **Plaintiffs overreached in seeking forced
> divesture of these key assets, which Google did not use to effect any illegal restraints.**"*

And on the payments the government wanted banned — the guard this brief previously carried as a
paraphrase, now in the court's own words:

> *"Cutting off payments from Google almost certainly will impose substantial—in some cases,
> **crippling**—downstream harms to distribution partners, related markets, and consumers, which
> counsels against a broad payment ban."*

★ **And Mehta names the counterfactual problem himself, while refusing the breakup:**

> *"…unlike the typical case where the court's job is to resolve a dispute based on historic facts,
> here the court is asked to **gaze into a crystal ball and look to the future. Not exactly a judge's
> forte.**"*

⛔ **Boasberg did not rule that Meta is not a monopoly. He ruled the FTC used a forward-facing
statute.** Section 13(b) of the FTC Act reaches a corporation that *"is violating, or is about to
violate"* the law — 15 U.S.C. § 53(b) — and courts read it as *"enjoining ongoing and imminent
future violations."* The FTC argued past harm that still lingers should suffice. The court's answer:

> *"That might be a sensible statutory scheme, but **it is not the one that Congress passed.**"*

★ **That is a statutory limit, not an economic verdict.** The case says close to nothing about
whether unwinding the Instagram acquisition would have been good policy — it says the agency has to
prove a *present* violation to get an injunction, and could not.

⚠ And the opinion's opening frames the market question, which is what "changed markedly" is actually
about:

> *"Believing that the only constant in the world was change, the Greek philosopher Heraclitus
> posited that no man can ever step into the same river twice… The landscape that existed only five
> years ago when the Federal Trade Commission brought this antitrust suit has changed markedly.
> While it once might have made sense to partition apps into separate markets of social networking
> and social media, **that wall has since broken down.**"*

⚠ **Procedural note:** Boasberg signed a **redacted** opinion on **18 Nov 2025** and a **revised**
opinion on **2 Dec 2025**. The text quoted here is the version obtained from the docket.

## Settled (not the debate)

- ⛔ **"BREAK THEM UP" IS AT LEAST THREE DIFFERENT REMEDIES, and almost no public argument
  distinguishes them.** *Divestiture* (sell AdX, spin out Instagram) · *conduct rules* (no
  self-preferencing, mandated interoperability, data portability) · *structural separation* (you may
  run the marketplace **or** sell on it, not both). Different costs, different evidence bases,
  different failure modes. ★ Same spine as ep08's two policies wearing one name.

- ★ **THE BELL BREAKUP RAISED TELECOM INNOVATION — AND LOWERED THE BROKEN-UP FIRM'S.** Both halves
  are one finding, from one paper. ✅ **Primary-verified 2026-08-31**, full text extracted; abstract
  verbatim: *"After the breakup, the scale and diversity of telecommunications innovation increased.
  Total patenting by US inventors related to telecommunications increased by **19%**, driven by
  companies unrelated to the Bell System. Patenting by Bell's successor companies **decreased**, but
  not the number of top inventions."*
  (Watzinger & Schnitzer, *The Breakup of the Bell System and its Impact on US Innovation*, 5 Sep 2022.)
  ⚠ **Neither side may quote half of this.** It is the single most-cited historical analogy in the
  debate and it does not resolve cleanly for anyone.

- ★ **DIVESTITURE WORKS WHEN IT TRANSFERS A WHOLE ONGOING BUSINESS, AND STRUGGLES WHEN IT TRANSFERS
  A PACKAGE OF ASSETS.** FTC, *Merger Remedies 2006–2012: A Report of the Bureaus of Competition and
  Economics* — **89 merger orders, 79 divestitures, 121 buyers**, case-study method. Divestitures of
  ongoing businesses generally succeeded in maintaining or restoring competition; buyers of limited
  asset packages were more likely to struggle.
  ⛔ **THE EXTRAPOLATION MUST BE DISCLOSED ON AIR.** This is evidence about **merger remedies**, not
  about breaking up a standing monopolist. It is the best systematic evidence anyone has on whether
  divestitures work, and it is not evidence about *this*. Say so, or do not use it.

- **US courts found the monopolies and still declined the breakups.** Liability and remedy came
  apart. Mehta and Brinkema both found unlawful monopoly; neither ordered a structural break. **The
  live disagreement is about remedy design, not about whether these firms are dominant.**

- **Europe ran the other experiment, and one result is a clean null.** The DMA regulates conduct *ex
  ante* rather than litigating structure after the fact. More than **26 months** after Apple's
  Article 5(7) obligation began (7 March 2024), **no browser vendor has shipped its own engine on
  iOS in the EU.**
  ⛔ **Disclose the source's interest, as ep08 did with the landlord-commissioned report.** The fact
  (no engine has shipped) is undisputed, including by Apple. The *attribution* to Apple's contract
  terms is Open Web Advocacy's contention, and OWA campaigns to end the ban. **Two different claims.**

- **The largest behavioural remedy ever imposed on a platform came with no structural relief.** Meta
  settled 29 states' teen-harm claims on 26 Aug 2026 for up to **$16.68bn** (reported totals to
  ~$17.1bn contingent on TikTok and YouTube settling) with mandated under-18 product changes —
  nine months after successfully defending against a breakup. ⚠ **Consumer-protection law, not
  antitrust.** The script must say so.

## Contested (the real fight)

### Contested #1 — is a conduct remedy a cure or a subscription?

**For structure:** a monopolist ordered to behave is supervised forever, litigating compliance case
by case, and the remedy expires. Structural separation removes the *incentive* to self-preference
rather than policing each instance. The ad tech case is the clean example: when one firm runs the
exchange, the publisher ad server and the buy side, no conduct rule fully undoes the conflict. The
EU record cuts this way too — permission alone did not produce a browser engine, because a firm that
controls the terms controls the outcome.

**For conduct:** courts declined for reasons, not timidity. Boasberg's point is empirical — the
market *did* move, TikTok *did* arrive, and a 2020 theory of harm looked different by 2025. Mehta
refused to ban Google's payments to Apple because it would have harmed the partners and consumers it
was meant to protect. Divestiture is irreversible, takes a decade, and the integration being severed
may be where the product quality lives.

### Contested #2 — whether the consumer-welfare standard can price a free product

US antitrust asks whether consumers are harmed: price, quality, output, innovation. Zero-price
products make that test awkward, and Boasberg's TikTok reasoning is that awkwardness in action.

⚠ **AND THE DEBATE IS PARTLY DEFINITIONAL, WHICH BOTH CAMPS OBSCURE.** Economists writing on this
note that the two sides use *different definitions of "consumer welfare"* and are frequently arguing
about different things. Neo-Brandeisians argue the standard ignores harms to workers and suppliers,
and democratic harms from concentration that arrive without any consumer price effect. Defenders
answer that a standard which counts everything constrains nothing.

### Contested #3 — the counterfactual nobody can price

Anti-breakup economists argue vertical integration is where consumer benefit comes from: Prime,
Maps, seamless cross-service integration, free search. Cutting in the wrong place destroys
efficiencies and raises prices. Pro-breakup economists answer that this is unfalsifiable — the
products that were never built by the competitors that never launched cannot be entered on the
ledger.

---

**Side A — "Structure is the cure":** Conduct rules are a subscription — supervised forever,
litigated case by case, and they expire. Structure removes the incentive rather than policing the
instance. The Bell breakup raised total telecom patenting 19%, driven by firms *outside* the old
monopoly, which is exactly what "unlock the ecosystem" predicts. And the EU proves permission is not
enough: 26 months after being required to allow rival browser engines, not one has shipped.

**Side B — "A costly bet on a counterfactual":** Courts declined for reasons. The market moved on
its own — TikTok arrived and a 2020 theory of harm was stale by 2025. Divestiture is irreversible,
takes a decade, and the FTC's own study says divesting *parts* of a business is where remedies fail.
Meanwhile the largest behavioural remedy ever imposed on a platform — $16.68bn and a redesigned
product for every US minor — happened last week, with no structural relief at all.

## The reframe

**The argument is conducted as "break them up: yes or no." The evidence does not answer that
question, because "break them up" is not one act — and the best systematic evidence anyone has says
the answer depends entirely on WHERE THE CUT FALLS.**

The FTC's own remedy study found divestitures of **ongoing businesses** generally succeeded and
divestitures of **limited asset packages** struggled. Apply that to the actual proposals and they
separate immediately: spinning out **Instagram** is transferring an ongoing business with its own
users, staff and revenue. Divesting **AdX** is carving a component out of an integrated stack. Those
are not the same intervention, they do not have the same evidence behind them, and almost nobody
arguing about "breaking up Big Tech" specifies which one they mean. The peer-reviewed literature has
a name for the problem — *scissor lines*, i.e. where a cut can actually be made.

★ **AND THE SECOND REFRAME, which only reading the opinions produces:** the argument treats this run
of cases as courts weighing breakups and rejecting them. **Neither court reached that question.**
Mehta refused divestiture because *"Google did not use [those assets] to effect any illegal
restraints"* — a scope holding. Boasberg dismissed because Section 13(b) reaches only ongoing or
imminent violations and *"that might be a sensible statutory scheme, but it is not the one that
Congress passed"* — a statutory holding. **Both sides quote these rulings as evidence about whether
breakups work. They are evidence about what these plaintiffs proved, under which statute.**

**And the buried point:** the most-cited historical case, the Bell breakup, is quoted by both camps
and supports neither cleanly. Total telecom patenting rose 19% — and the broken-up firm's own
patenting fell. Whether that is a success depends on whether you are counting the ecosystem or the
company, which is the same disagreement one level down.

## Both sides quietly agree

**Neither camp's remedy has the evidence its rhetoric implies.** The pro-breakup side's strongest
systematic evidence is about merger remedies, not monopoly breakups, and it says the *harder* kind of
divestiture is the kind that fails. The anti-breakup side's strongest argument is a counterfactual it
cannot price, and it is currently being run against a live example — a $16.68bn behavioural remedy
that reshaped a product for every US minor without touching corporate structure.

**And both sides accept the finding of liability.** Nobody serious now argues Google's search or ad
tech conduct was lawful; two federal judges found otherwise. The fight is entirely about what
follows.

## Verdict framing

No winner. What is handed to the viewer is the question that actually decides the policy: **not
whether to break up Big Tech, but where a cut could be made that the evidence supports** — and
whether you would accept a slower, supervised, permanent remedy instead of a fast structural one that
might not work.

The viewer's tool, shared with ep07, ep08 and ep09: **ask what is actually being proposed, and
whether the evidence offered is evidence about that.**
