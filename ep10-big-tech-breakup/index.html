Big Tech Antitrust: Should the Tech Giants Be Broken Up?

The question: Is breaking up Big Tech good antitrust or bad economics?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

Three remedies, one phrase — and the rulings everyone cites never reached the question

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in September 2026, three big US cases against Google and Meta had produced no breakups, and the remedy in Google's ad tech case had been pending for more than nine months. Europe and Britain were each running a different approach at the same time.

The fight

Green arguesA Bet Nobody Can Price

You can't un-break a company, and the judge who wrote 230 pages on Google said predicting the future is "not exactly a judge's forte." After the phone company breakup, the industry gained and the company itself lost. Supervision you can adjust beats one permanent cut made in the dark.

Gold arguesStructure Is The Cure

The careful option already has results: Europe required rival browser engines on the iPhone, and in 26 months none has shipped. Behaviour rules need watching forever. When one firm runs the exchange, the ad server and the buying side, no fairness rule removes the conflict, because the conflict is the org chart.

What both sides quietly agree on

After the phone company breakup in 1984, industry patenting rose about 19% while the broken-up company's own fell, and both teams accepted both numbers. Green conceded that where one firm runs every side of a market, no fairness rule fixes it. Gold conceded nobody can know the future, and a cut can't be undone.

What the shouting gets wrong

"Break them up" hides at least three different policies: force a sale, set rules on behaviour, or bar a company from both running a marketplace and selling on it. The best study found that selling off a whole working business generally succeeds and carving out a piece generally struggles, though that study was about unwinding mergers. So the real question is which cut you'd make, and whether you'd accept being wrong about it permanently.

The verdict

Nia This one doesn't resolve, and I want to be precise about where it stops rather than call it a draw.

Nia Both teams accepted the same historical evidence, including the half that hurt them. Industry patenting rose about nineteen percent after the phone company breakup, and the broken-up firm's own research output fell. Neither debater disputed either number.

Nia So the disagreement isn't about what happened. It's about what you are counting — the industry, or the company. That is a values question wearing an evidence question's clothes.

Nia Both also conceded their weak flank. Green conceded that where one firm runs every side of a market, no fairness rule dissolves the conflict. Gold conceded the counterfactual is unknowable and a cut cannot be undone.

Nia The case for waiting rests on irreversibility, and on the judge with the most evidence saying the future was not his to see.

Nia The case for cutting rests on twenty-six months of a conduct rule that produced nothing, and on a market where the conflict is the org chart.

Nia And the thing both cases are downstream of is a phrase that hides three different policies. The best systematic evidence says a whole business survives being sold and a component usually doesn't — so the answer depends on where the cut falls, and almost nobody says where.

Nia So the question we're handing you isn't whether to break up Big Tech. It's which cut you'd actually make, and whether you'd accept being wrong about it permanently. That one's yours.
