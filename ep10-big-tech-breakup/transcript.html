The research · Three countries, three answers, right now

KateFor about twenty years the line has been that somebody should break up Big Tech. In the last two years, courts actually started answering.

RyanAnswering how? Did anyone win?

KateThe government won on liability, twice. A federal judge found Google's search business an illegal monopoly, and a different judge found its ad tech business one too.

RyanSo they lost. That's the headline.

KateThat's the liability. The remedy is a separate fight, and that's where it turns. In September last year the search judge refused to break anything off.

RyanRefused what exactly?

KateThe government asked him to force Google to sell Chrome, with Android held in reserve. He declined both, and ordered behaviour changes instead — no exclusive deals, and Google has to share parts of its search index and user data with competitors.

RyanAnd the other case?

KateTwo months later a third judge threw the Meta case out entirely. Instagram and WhatsApp stay where they are. The agency has appealed.

RyanSo three big cases, no breakups.

KateAnd the ad tech remedy still hasn't come. Closing arguments finished in November of last year. It's been pending more than nine months.

RyanThat's the American story. Is anyone doing it differently?

KateTwo other places are running two other answers, right now. Europe wrote one rulebook for everyone. Britain does something else entirely — it names a specific company and writes rules for that company alone.

RyanHold on. Three different countries, three different methods, all running at the same time? That's not a debate any more, that's an experiment.

The research · "Break them up" is three different policies

KateIt is. And the ground that genuinely isn't in dispute starts with the phrase itself.

KateBreak them up isn't one thing. It's at least three, and they have almost nothing in common.

RyanThree?

KateOne: divestiture. Force the company to sell something — the ad exchange, or Instagram. Two: conduct rules. Let it keep everything, but tell it how to behave. Three: structural separation. You may run the marketplace or sell on it, not both.

RyanDifferent costs, different evidence, different ways of failing.

KateCompletely different. And almost no public argument about this says which one it means.

RyanAlright. What's the evidence on whether any of them work?

KateThe most-cited case in the whole argument is the breakup of the phone company in nineteen eighty-four. And it is quoted by both sides, because it says two things at once.

RyanGo on.

KateAfter the breakup, total American telecoms patenting went up about nineteen percent. But that increase came from companies that had nothing to do with the old phone company.

RyanAnd the pieces of the old company itself?

KateTheir own patenting fell. Same paper, same table. If you're counting the industry it worked, and if you're counting the company it didn't.

The research · It depends entirely on where the cut falls

RyanSo we've got one famous case that answers both ways. Is there anything systematic?

KateThere is, and it's the finding I'd put in front of anyone arguing about this. The competition regulator studied its own divestiture orders — eighty-nine of them, across six years.

RyanAnd?

KateDivestitures that handed over a whole working business generally succeeded. Divestitures that handed over a package of assets generally struggled.

RyanSay that again, because that sounds like it decides something.

KateSelling a going concern works. Carving a piece out of a machine and hoping it runs on its own mostly doesn't.

RyanSo apply that. Spinning off Instagram is —

KateA whole working business. Its own users, its own staff, its own revenue. Whereas the ad exchange the government wants sold is a component wired into a stack.

RyanThose are completely different operations, and everyone calls both of them a breakup.

KateWhich is the whole problem with the phrase. And I have to flag the limit of that study honestly: it's about unwinding mergers, not about breaking up a standing monopoly.

RyanSo it's not evidence about the thing we're actually arguing.

KateIt's the best systematic evidence anyone has, and it is not evidence about this. Both of those are true, and neither side gets to spend it as if only one were.

RyanThat's the most useful thing anyone's said about this argument so far.

The research · The rulings don't say what the argument says

KateLast of the settled ground, and it's the part that changed my mind about the whole file.

RyanWhich is?

KateThat the rulings don't say what everybody says they say. We read both opinions end to end — the search one runs two hundred and thirty pages.

RyanAnd what's in them?

KateThe search judge didn't rule that breakups are bad policy. He ruled the government asked for the wrong thing. His words: the plaintiffs overreached in seeking these assets, which Google did not use to effect any illegal restraints.

RyanMeaning Chrome wasn't the weapon.

KateRight. The violation was in the distribution contracts. He wouldn't take assets that weren't the instrument. That's a scope holding, not a verdict on breaking up companies.

RyanAnd the Meta case?

KateThat one's even narrower. The agency has to prove a company is violating the law now, or about to. It argued past harm that's still doing damage should count. The judge said that might be a sensible scheme, but it is not the one that Congress passed.

RyanSo it lost on the statute. Not on whether buying Instagram was bad for competition.

KateNot on that at all. And both sides quote these rulings as proof that breakups don't work. They're proof of what these plaintiffs proved, under which law.

RyanThat's a completely different claim, and I'd never have known from the coverage.

The reframe

LucasI went in expecting to referee whether breaking up big companies is a good idea. That's not the argument I found.

LucasThe best systematic evidence we have doesn't say breakups work, and it doesn't say they fail. It says it depends entirely on where the cut falls.

LucasHand over a whole working business and it generally survives. Carve a component out of a stack and it generally doesn't. Those are both called breaking up a company, and they are not the same act.

LucasSo when somebody says break them up, the only useful question is: break off what, exactly? Almost nobody says.

LucasAnd the thing that stopped me cold is that the court rulings everyone cites don't settle this either. One judge said the government asked for assets that weren't used to break the law. Another said the agency used a statute that only reaches what's happening now. Neither one ruled on whether it's a good idea.

LucasGreen argues it's a bet nobody can price. Gold argues structure is the cure. Green goes first.

The debate · Team Green · A BET NOBODY CAN PRICE

AndreI want to argue about time, because everything on the other side of this is a claim about a future nobody has seen.

AndreStart with the thing my opponent needs. The phone company breakup raised industry patenting about nineteen percent. I accept that. It's the best historical evidence there is.

AndreNow the half that gets left off. The broken-up company's own research output fell. Same paper, same table. So the honest version is that the industry gained and the firm lost, and which of those you call success is a choice, not a finding.

AndreSecond: irreversibility. You cannot un-break a company. A divestiture takes years, the appeal takes more, and if it was the wrong cut there is no putting it back.

AndreThird, and this is the one I'd sit with. The judge who spent two hundred and thirty pages on this said the remedy question asked him to gaze into a crystal ball and look to the future, and that this was not exactly a judge's forte.

AndreThat's not a technicality. That's the person with the most evidence anyone has assembled saying the counterfactual is not knowable.

AndreFourth: there is a cheaper answer already running. Last week one of these companies agreed to roughly seventeen billion dollars and rebuilt its product for every minor in the country. No structure was touched.

AndreAnd I'll say the weakness in that myself. That was a child-safety case, not an antitrust one. Different law, and I'll own that.

AndreBut it does show that a company's behaviour can be changed enormously without cutting it into pieces.

AndreMy opponent will say conduct rules are a subscription you pay forever. She's right that they need policing.

AndreI'd rather pay for supervision I can adjust than make one irreversible cut in the dark.

The debate · Team Gold responds

MaraI'll take his framing, because the time argument is the strongest thing his side has and I want to answer it directly.

MaraBut notice what he did. He asked us to wait for evidence about the future, while there is evidence about the past sitting right here.

MaraEurope didn't cut anything. It wrote a rule saying rival browser engines must be allowed on the iPhone. That rule has been in force for over twenty-six months.

MaraIn twenty-six months, not one has shipped. Not one. That is the conduct approach, run at scale, and we can already measure what it produced.

The debate · Team Gold · STRUCTURE IS THE CURE

MaraLet me grant his best point first, because I think he's right about it. You cannot un-break a company, and the counterfactual is genuinely unknowable.

MaraMy argument is that we are not choosing between a known thing and an unknown one. We are choosing between two bets, and only one of them has already been run.

MaraStart with Europe, because it's the cleanest result in the file. Twenty-six months of a rule requiring that rival browser engines be permitted on the iPhone, and zero have shipped.

MaraI'll say the honest part myself. The people documenting that campaign for the ban to end, so read their explanation with that in mind. But the fact isn't disputed by anyone, including the company.

MaraSecond: a conduct rule needs somebody watching it forever. The search remedy comes with a technical committee overseeing compliance. That's not a fix, that's a subscription — and it expires.

MaraThird, and this is where the structure argument actually bites. When one firm runs the exchange, the ad server and the buying side, no rule about fair behaviour removes the conflict. The conflict is the org chart.

MaraAnd I'll take the phone company case, including the part that hurts me. Yes, the broken-up firm's own patenting fell.

MaraBut total industry patenting rose about nineteen percent, and it rose because of companies that had nothing to do with the old monopoly. That is exactly what unlocking a market is supposed to look like.

MaraIf you only count the incumbent, you will never count the thing a breakup is for.

MaraAnd Britain is now testing the middle. It names the company and writes rules for it alone — including on browser engines, the precise thing Europe's general rule failed to move.

MaraI'm not asking for a blind cut. I'm asking that we stop pretending the careful option has no results yet, when one of them has twenty-six months and nothing to show.

The debate · Team Green responds

AndreThe browser engine result is her best card. Twenty-six months and nothing shipped is a real number.

AndreWhat it shows is that one badly-designed conduct rule failed. It doesn't show that structure would have worked, because nobody ran that arm of the experiment.

AndreAnd Britain proves my point better than hers. That's a third conduct regime, started this year, aimed at the same target. If conduct were hopeless, nobody serious would be building a new one.

AndreShe's right that a supervised remedy has to be watched. I'd rather watch something than break something.

AndreBut I have to concede her strongest line. If one firm runs all three sides of a market, no rule about fairness dissolves that conflict. On that specific structure, I think she's got the better argument.

The verdict

NiaThis one doesn't resolve, and I want to be precise about where it stops rather than call it a draw.

NiaBoth teams accepted the same historical evidence, including the half that hurt them. Industry patenting rose about nineteen percent after the phone company breakup, and the broken-up firm's own research output fell. Neither debater disputed either number.

NiaSo the disagreement isn't about what happened. It's about what you are counting — the industry, or the company. That is a values question wearing an evidence question's clothes.

NiaBoth also conceded their weak flank. Green conceded that where one firm runs every side of a market, no fairness rule dissolves the conflict. Gold conceded the counterfactual is unknowable and a cut cannot be undone.

NiaThe case for waiting rests on irreversibility, and on the judge with the most evidence saying the future was not his to see.

NiaThe case for cutting rests on twenty-six months of a conduct rule that produced nothing, and on a market where the conflict is the org chart.

NiaAnd the thing both cases are downstream of is a phrase that hides three different policies. The best systematic evidence says a whole business survives being sold and a component usually doesn't — so the answer depends on where the cut falls, and almost nobody says where.

NiaSo the question we're handing you isn't whether to break up Big Tech. It's which cut you'd actually make, and whether you'd accept being wrong about it permanently. That one's yours.
