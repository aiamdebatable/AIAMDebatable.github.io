# Research brief — ep54: AI and Artists: Which Claims Hold Up?

_The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video._

**Episode type: EXPLAINER, a claims ledger.** No teams and no clash. It is a follow-on to ep49 ("AI Art vs
Artists", published; it posts 2026-11-18). The episode takes the claims people repeat about AI and creative
jobs, in their own words, and gives each one a verdict word: **TRUE**, **PARTLY TRUE**, **NOT MEASURED** or
**FALSE**. Why an explainer: most of these claims can be checked against a document (a payroll count, a court
order, a company filing, a contract), and the honest answer to several is "nobody has measured that", which is
better shown than argued.

**Title (ruled 2026-11-02; was "AI and Creative Jobs: Which Claims Hold Up?"):** "AI and Artists: Which Claims Hold Up?" (metadata). **The question:** "Which of the
claims people repeat about AI and creative jobs hold up?"

## The question, in one line

**"You've heard a lot of claims about AI and the people who make things. Which ones are true?"**

## Why now

_Peg hunt run 2026-11-02; every leg below read at the primary, the company's own SEC filings, and the customer
and cause wording corrected by hand pass B (B1, B3–B11). Re-run the hunt before scripting._

On **2026-10-21 Getty Images**, the biggest stock-photo company, filed for **Chapter 11** in the US Bankruptcy
Court for the District of Delaware, to carry out a prepackaged plan agreed with its lenders on 2026-10-12 (P1,
P2). The plan brings up to **$533 million** of new equity from Getty family entities and affiliates of Koch Equity
Development, eliminates **more than $500 million** of roughly **$1.46 billion** of funded debt, and takes the
company private, owned entirely by those investors (P2, P3; B9). Getty has said it will ask the court for a plan
hearing on **December 14, 2026**; that date is requested, not set (P6).

**What the filing says caused it, in Getty's own account.** The disclosure statement is the debtors' own account
to their creditors and the court: an interested party explaining its own failure. Quote it as Getty's account,
never as a finding. It says the filing "was primarily driven by three non-operational events": large judgments in
lawsuits over old warrants, the long and finally collapsed merger with Shutterstock (the UK competition authority
required the sale of Shutterstock's whole editorial business; Getty announced the termination on June 30 and
delivered formal notice on July 7, 2026), and a debt load it calls unsustainable (B1, B9, B12). It says the
pressures on its budget iStock brand and its agency customers "would not alone have necessitated" the filing
(B1).

**Where AI comes in, and where it doesn't (pass B's correction).** Generative AI appears as a cause **only in
the bankruptcy filing**, not in Getty's annual reports. Each 10-K from FY2023 to FY2025 explained the falling
customer count without mentioning AI: the shift to subscriptions, shrinking agency customers, and the end of an
iStock free-trial programme in June 2025 (B5, B6, B7). The disclosure statement then names generative AI as one
of several "structural shifts", alongside changes in media consumption and marketing, under the heading "Broader
Industry Headwinds", and says these shifts "have been felt principally in the Company's iStock brand" and among
its agency customers (B10). It also says AI tools "put pressure on the budget-conscious microstock image market",
which it calls "only a small portion" of its business (B8).

**The customer count is company-wide (pass B's second correction).** Getty's count of paying customers fell from
about 835,000 (the year to December 2022) to about 636,000 (the year to June 2026), "approximately 24 percent"
(B3). That is the same company-wide figure the 10-Ks report (835,000 → 799,000 → 717,000 for 2022–2024), defined
as all customers who made a purchase (B4). Getty's filing says the metric is "driven predominantly" by iStock and
agency customers and reflects conditions specific to them (B3). So: never "iStock lost a quarter of its
customers", and never "Getty lost a quarter of its customers to AI". The peg hunt's "iStock and agency metrics
only" was Getty's framing, not the metric's definition (B3).

**What else the record shows.** Revenue rose each year from 2023 to 2025, to $981.3 million in 2025 (B2); the
filing gives about $978 million for the twelve months to mid-2026 (B1; a different window). Getty is on both
sides of the claim: it licenses images, video and metadata to AI companies for training, "a growing new revenue
stream", and sells its own generative AI (B8; peg hunt). The one independent voice is the UK competition
authority, which cleared the stock side of the merger because large AI firms are "likely" to "increasingly
compete" with Getty and Shutterstock: a forecast, not a measured loss (B13). No going-concern doubt appears in the
FY2025 10-K (filed 2026-03-16); the filing says that doubt arose in 2026, after the warrant judgment and an April
draw on its credit line (B11).

**The ledger row this makes:** "AI bankrupted Getty Images" is **FALSE as the cause** in the company's own
account; AI is one named pressure on one part of the business. That gap between the claim and the record is the
shape of the whole episode.

**The structural version, for the script:** "In October 2026 the world's biggest stock-photo company, Getty
Images, went through bankruptcy court in Delaware. People said AI did it. Its own filing tells a narrower story."
Name Getty Images and the court; no personalities, no share price.

**Also live in the window, not the peg** (the peg hunt's rejects, in the log): Meta's proposed commercial terms
(effective date now 2026-11-20; C38, secondary), the coalition amicus brief in the newspaper case against OpenAI
(2026-10-30, secondary), the publishers' amicus there (C22), and the Copyright Office's §1201 rulemaking (no AI
class).

## The journey (MAKE IT MATTER)

**The viewer:** someone who makes things for a living or on the side (writes, draws, designs, shoots, sings,
voices), or someone who loves the people who do and shares what they read about them. Their real question is not
"is AI good or bad"; it's **"what I've been told about my work, or theirs: is it true?"** And under it: "what is
actually happening to the people who make things, and what can they do about it?"

**The viewer's moment:** the moment before you repost. A headline in the feed ("AI just bankrupted Getty Images"),
a screenshot of a survey, a tool someone swears will protect your art. The episode is what you'd want to know
before you share it.

**What they believe now that the record says is wrong, in one breath:** that the government's numbers show the
jobs collapsing (they don't, A43), that 100,000 film jobs are already gone (that's a forecast, A18), that a free
tool makes your art safe (only in the lab, D2-20–D2-23), and that nothing protects a performer's voice (at least
four states and two union contracts do, with holes, D1-24). **What they may not believe that the record says is
true:** that freelance writing work really did fall after ChatGPT (A24–A28); that you can't own a style (C7, C8);
that the flood is real where people look (B35, B36); and that for some disabled artists AI is the access they
couldn't get any other way, and they fear the same thing everyone does (D2-29).

**The cold open: premise before the object, feeling over figures, ≤ ~100 words.** _The shape and the facts, not
the script; the house lines vary._
- **Premise:** some of what you've heard about AI and creative jobs is true; some of it is a forecast, a
  self-report or a company's own story wearing a fact's clothes. Today we check them.
- **Object:** the biggest stock-photo company in the world went through bankruptcy court in October. The post
  said AI did it. Its own filing names a collapsed merger, old lawsuits and debt (B1, P1).
- **The turn and the promise:** "So let's take the claims you keep hearing, one at a time, and stamp each one:
  true, partly true, false, or nobody's measured it." (One sentence; the four stamps rise at first USE on the
  Getty row, not as a rule block: town change 2.)
- First evidence on screen by ~0:40: the Getty row itself (town change 4).

**The beats, as a journey** (each beat follows one concrete thing, sets the fear against the record, and ends on
"Hold that"; the ringmaster asks the viewer's question; the order is a suggestion for the pitch):

   forecast of jobs "likely" affected, from 300 executives, commissioned by four interested groups; A18–A20). The

## Where the record leans (said plainly; no false balance)

**The supply flood is measured; the jobs collapse isn't; the law is unsettled; the money is small or secret.**

- **The flood is real where it's been counted, and small where it's listened to or bought.** More than half of
  one music service's uploads on peak days (B18) but 1–3% of its listening (B19); about a fifth of one
  genre-fiction sample's books but 12% of its sales (B41); AI images in the top results of most of the popular
  Pinterest searches spot-checked in 2025 (B35, B36). Nobody has a platform-wide count of images, and the viral "79%" has no method
  (B37, B39).
- **The measured job hit is real and narrow.** The best causal evidence is on freelance platforms: writing work
  fell against comparable work after ChatGPT, in four studies that agree in direction (A24, A26–A28); image work
  fell in two of three (A25–A27). In the government's payroll counts, graphic designers fell beyond sampling error
  in one year, 2024–25 (A2, A7), and editors too (A7); writers' fall is inside the noise and leaves them above 2019
  (A1, A7, A8); musicians and photographers rose (A4, A6); the whole arts, design and media group is flat while all
  jobs grew about 5% (A7). BLS assigns none of it to AI (A9, A43). **The doom slogans fare worst on jobs.**
- **The scariest loss figures are forecasts or self-reports.** 118,500 film jobs is executives' expectations,
  commissioned by interested groups (A18–A20); the WEF's net number flipped sign between editions (A21, A22); "a
  quarter of illustrators" is a members' survey with an unpublished illustrator count, and survey wording moves the
  answer from 6% to 21% to 26% (A29–A31).
- **The law is unsettled, and protection comes in patches.** Two district judges in one court found training on
  books fair use and split on pirated copies (C11–C15); one appeals court ruled against a non-generative tool
  (C18, C19); no US court has ruled on image generators (C39); Germany's lyrics ruling is first-instance (C28). At
  least four US states and two union contracts protect a voice or likeness, but no law read bans training on a
  performance (D1-24, D1-18), and NO FAKES is not law (D1-25).
- **The comfort slogans fare worst on pay and protection.** Licensing money is real but small per person or
  undisclosed (D2-1–D2-16, D2-41); Glaze and Nightshade work best in their authors' own tests (D2-20–D2-28); Bartz
  is a settlement for pirated copies that has paid nobody yet (C13, D2-5, D2-6); "AI will create more jobs than it
  destroys" is not measured for creative work at all (A21).

Said as one line: **the record backs "it's flooding the zone" and "the cheap commissioned work is going"; it does
not back "the jobs are gone", "the courts have decided", or "you can protect yourself".**

## The ledger (every claim people repeat, in their words)

### Markets and the peg

**L1. "AI bankrupted Getty Images." — FALSE** (as the cause; AI is a named headwind on one brand)
- Why: Getty's own filing blames three non-operating events and says the iStock and agency pressures "would not
  alone" have forced it (B1).
- For: paying customers fell about a quarter, 2022 → mid-2026 (B3); the filing names generative AI among the
  structural shifts hitting iStock and agencies (B10); the UK regulator expects AI firms to compete more (B13); a
  $206.2M net loss in 2025 (B2).
- Against: revenue rose every year to $981.3M in 2025 (B2); the 10-Ks blamed subscriptions, agencies and a
  free-trial ending, never AI, for the customer fall (B5–B7); the causes named are $200M+ in judgments, $100M+ in
  merger fees and $1.46B of debt (B9); and it's Getty's own account (B1).
- Comparator: its yearly debt service, about $239 million, was about a quarter of a year's revenue (our arithmetic,
  B1, B9); its debt about one and a half years of revenue (our arithmetic, B2, B9).
- Rows: B1–B13, P1–P6.

**L2. "Photographers are being wiped out by AI." — NOT MEASURED** (re-stamped 2026-11-02 by his verdict rule: FALSE only when the record contradicts the claim; when no source ties the change to AI, NOT MEASURED; was FALSE. Payroll photographers rose, which contradicts "wiped out" for payroll jobs, but payroll can't see the self-employed, who are most photographers, and no source measures AI's effect on them)
- Why: payroll photographers rose 9.2%, 2022–25, a rise beyond sampling error (A4, A43).
- For: BLS projects photographers −1% to 2035 and says stock photos will be "partially replaced" by AI images
  (a projection, A13); Adobe Stock's library was about 48% AI by one photographer's 2025 count (APPROXIMATE, B38).
- Against: payroll up (A4); BLS expects self-employed photographers to grow (A13); Getty's contributor royalties
  held at about $220M a year, 2023–25 (B14–B16; totals, not per person).
- Comparator: about 4,400 more payroll photographers in 2025 than 2022 (our arithmetic, A4).
- Rows: A4, A13, B14–B16, B38.

**L3. "Half of new music on streaming is AI." — PARTLY TRUE** (ep49 aired it; callback only)
- Why: true of uploads at one service (Deezer) on peak days in June 2026; AI tracks are 1–3% of what people play
  there (B18, B19).
- For: nearly 90,000 fully AI tracks a day, more than half of deliveries on peak days; the share rose from about
  10% (Jan 2025) to 18% (Apr) to 28% (Sept) (B18, B22–B24).
- Against: 1–3% of streams (B19); Deezer's detector is self-reported, 99.8%, untested by anyone outside (B20);
  Spotify's 75 million are "spammy tracks", not AI (B27).
- Comparator: of every hundred streams on Deezer, one to three are fully AI tracks (B19).
- Rows: B18–B24, B26, B27.

**L4. "AI music is stealing human artists' royalties." — NOT MEASURED** (industry-wide)
- Why: no source measures a realised loss; Deezer says fraudulent AI streams are removed from the pool, so the hit
  to human royalties there is "minimal" (B21).
- For: one proven fraud scheme took about $8M from the shared pool (B30, B32, B34); "up to 85%" of streams on AI
  tracks were fraudulent at Deezer in 2025 (B19); a society forecasts 25% of creators' revenue "at risk by 2028" (a
  forecast, B21).
- Against: Deezer's own statement (B21); fraud is 8% of all Deezer streams (B19).
- Comparator: "at risk by 2028" is a forecast; the one measured case is about $8 million (B21, B32).
- Rows: B19, B21, B25, B30–B34, B46.

**L5. "He faced 60 years." — PARTLY TRUE** (ep49 aired it)
- Why: true only of the 2024 indictment, as three stacked 20-year maximums; it was never what he realistically
  faced: he pleaded to one count with a 5-year maximum and got 18 months (B28–B30).
- Against: guidelines 46–57 months; the government asked for 46, probation 24 (B31, secondary courtroom account).
- Comparator: 18 months is less than half the bottom of the guideline range (our arithmetic, B31).
- Rows: B28–B32.

**L6. "He's punished; the platforms aren't." — PARTLY TRUE**
- Why: no platform was charged, because in the government's account the platforms were among the defrauded,
  with the musicians (B33, B34).
- For: a platform flagged him in 2018, by a prosecutor's account (B33); no restitution was sought, "it cannot be
  calculated" (B33).
- Against: DOJ: a scheme "to defraud music streaming platforms and musicians" (B34); the judge: "It was not the use
  of AI which was targeted here" (B33).
- Rows: B33, B34.

**L7. "The labels settled with Suno and Udio." — PARTLY TRUE**
- Why: two of the three majors licensed Udio, one licensed Suno, Sony settled with neither, and UMG and Sony are
  still suing Suno (D2-11).
- For: UMG–Udio (Oct 2025), Warner–Suno (Nov 2025) (D2-9, D2-10).
- Against: Sony sued Udio again in July 2026 over 30,117 recordings (D2-11); the Warner–Suno terms are withheld even
  from the other labels (D2-13).
- Rows: D2-9–D2-13.

**L8. "Spotify is launching an AI remix tool." — PARTLY TRUE**
- Why: licensed by some rights-holders, not launched; "early 2027" is a press report, not a Spotify date (D2-14,
  D2-15).

### Law, copyright and opt-outs

**L23. "The courts have ruled AI training is fair use, so it's legal." — PARTLY TRUE**
