Transcript — "Does building more housing actually lower rents — and for whom?"

Verbatim spoken track, in broadcast order. Speakers: Lucas (host), Ryan (research lead), Lena (the doubts), Nia (Team Green · Just Build), Walt (Team Gold · Build For Whom), Mara (the judge), and the Narrator who reads the cold open.

Cold open

NarratorAsk the internet whether building more housing lowers rents, and you'll get luxury-condo villains on one side and supply-and-demand lectures on the other.

NarratorAsk the research, and rents do come down. The fight is over who's still living there when they do.

NarratorRents in Austin fell. That part isn't in dispute. What nobody can tell you is whether the people who were already there stayed long enough to see it.

The research · The chain is real

RyanStart with what isn't in dispute. More of this is settled than the shouting suggests.

RyanNew market-rate supply lowers rents at the scale of a metro. That result reproduces across methods.

LenaEven the expensive stuff? The glass towers with the rooftop pool?

RyanSomebody moves into the new building. They vacate an older one. Somebody moves into that. The chain keeps going, downward.

RyanPer one hundred new market-rate units, between forty-five and seventy people vacate a below-median-income neighbourhood.

RyanBetween seventeen and thirty-nine come out of the very poorest tracts. Almost all of it inside five years.

LenaThose are ranges, not single numbers.

The debate · Team Green · Just Build

NiaOne hundred twenty thousand homes. That is thirty percent more housing stock in nine years. Asking rents came down four percent. This is not a model of a city. It is a city.

NiaAnd the cheapest apartments moved most. Class C fell eleven point four percent. Class A fell two point six. The part everyone expects to be true is the part that isn't.

NiaCheck the same claim at another scale. Ten percent more supply in a zip code bought about one point four percentage points of slower rent growth.

NiaCheck it again at the block. Large new buildings in low-income areas were followed by nearby rents five to seven percent lower.

NiaThree scales. Three methods. Three separate datasets that were never meant to agree with each other, and they point the same way.

NiaIt holds outside this country too. Auckland upzoned three quarters of its residential land. Rents landed about twenty-eight percent below the synthetic control.

NiaAnd the chain reaches down. Forty-five to seventy people per hundred new units vacate a below-median neighbourhood inside five years.

NiaSo the obstacle is not economics. It never was. Construction costs are up forty-two percent, and sixty-three percent of the people who turn up to hearings turn up to object.

NiaThose two things are the shortage. Not zoning theory. Not developer greed. A cost problem, and a veto.

NiaMy opponent will say the effect is small per unit. He is right. That is an argument for building more of them, not for building fewer.

The debate · Team Gold responds

WaltShe's right. I want to be precise about what she's right about, because none of it is small and none of it is in doubt.

WaltEvery number she gave you is real, every one of them survived the fact-check, and on direction the evidence is hers.

The debate · Team Gold · Build For Whom

WaltLet me grant her whole case first, because I think she's right about direction and I'd rather argue about something that's actually in doubt.

WaltWhat I want to ask is what those numbers are measuring, because I don't believe it's the thing most people hear when they're read out.

WaltIn Minneapolis, researchers looked at new construction and found no average effect on nearby rents whatsoever — which on its own sounds like a null result and a boring finding.

WaltBut when they sorted the existing buildings by their own baseline rent, the cheapest third went up four point four percent over five years, while the rest of the street didn't move.

WaltSame block. New building goes up. The apartments that were already cheap get more expensive, and the ones that were already expensive don't.

WaltThere's a working paper that finds both halves at once, and I'd flag that it hasn't been through peer review, so I'm using it to frame the question rather than to settle it.

WaltIt reports displacement falling near new construction, and the parcels closest to it becoming more likely to gentrify. Those aren't contradictory. They're the same street doing two things to two different people.

WaltAnd I'd rather have an answer to this next part than a rebuttal, because I don't think it has one yet.

WaltEvery study in this argument prices units. Not people. It follows the apartment and reports what the apartment costs the next tenant.

WaltWhen somebody gets priced out and moves away, they leave the dataset with the neighbourhood. The apartment's rent is observed. What happened to them is not recorded anywhere.

WaltSo a place can post falling rents and rising displacement simultaneously, and the chart looks like good news in both cases. I'm not saying don't build. I'm asking who's being counted.

The debate · Team Green responds

NiaThat is the strongest thing he has said all episode, so let me take it straight on rather than around.

NiaThe vacancy-chain study does follow people. Fifty-two thousand residents, traced by address history rather than by apartment.

NiaForty-five to seventy of them per hundred new units, out of below-median neighbourhoods, inside five years.

NiaIs it a clean experiment? No. It is address tracing plus a simulation model, and that is all I will claim for it.

NiaBut it counts people. That is the thing he says nobody does. And it points his way rather less than he would like.

The verdict

MaraTwo studies. One kind of street. Opposite signs. That's genuinely where this sits, and it deserves better than being called a tie.

MaraMost of the gap turns out to be method rather than disagreement. One priced listings in low-income tracts across eleven strong markets. The other priced existing buildings by their own baseline rent, across all of Minneapolis.

MaraThey're answering different questions. Neither one is wrong.

MaraBut a residual survives, and it's the thing worth arguing about. Both agree new supply lowers rents at the top of the local market. They disagree about whether an amenity effect dominates at the bottom.

MaraThe case for building rests on evidence that reproduces at three separate scales, and on a mechanism you can follow from the new tower down to the oldest block on the street.

MaraThe case against rests on what those measurements leave out, and on how long the chain takes to arrive for the people waiting at the bottom of it.

MaraAnd both sides conceded something without meaning to. Neither team argued about the asset. They fought over rent — while American homeowners hold roughly thirty-four trillion dollars of equity that depends on housing staying scarce.

MaraThree hundred ninety-six thousand of median net worth on one side of that line. Ten thousand four hundred on the other. On direction, the evidence leans, and it leans toward building. Whether the people already living there end up better off — measured on them, not on the apartments they used to rent — nobody has answered. That one is yours.
