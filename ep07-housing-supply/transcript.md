## The verdict
**Mara:** Two studies. One kind of street. Opposite signs. That's genuinely where this sits, and it deserves better than being called a tie.
**Mara:** Most of the gap turns out to be method rather than disagreement. One priced listings in low-income tracts across eleven strong markets. The other priced existing buildings by their own baseline rent, across all of Minneapolis.
**Mara:** They're answering different questions. Neither one is wrong.
**Mara:** But a residual survives, and it's the thing worth arguing about. Both agree new supply lowers rents at the top of the local market. They disagree about whether an amenity effect dominates at the bottom.
**Mara:** The case for building rests on evidence that reproduces at three separate scales, and on a mechanism you can follow from the new tower down to the oldest block on the street.
**Mara:** The case against rests on what those measurements leave out, and on how long the chain takes to arrive for the people waiting at the bottom of it.
**Mara:** And both sides conceded something without meaning to. Neither team argued about the asset. They fought over rent — while American homeowners hold roughly thirty-four trillion dollars of equity that depends on housing staying scarce.
**Mara:** Three hundred ninety-six thousand of median net worth on one side of that line. Ten thousand four hundred on the other. On direction, the evidence leans, and it leans toward building. Whether the people already living there end up better off — measured on them, not on the apartments they used to rent — nobody has answered. That one is yours.
