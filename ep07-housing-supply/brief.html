Episode 7 · The research

Research brief — Does Building More Housing Actually Lower Rents?

The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video.

Topic-shape verdict (guardrail, logged before either side was written): ⚠️ The direction question leans one way. The economics literature is closer to agreement than the public fight suggests — new supply lowers rents. Manufacturing a 50/50 on direction would be dishonest. The episode is therefore built on the question that is genuinely contested and where peer-reviewed work disagrees with itself: for whom, how fast, and still living where.

The question

Does building more market-rate housing actually lower rents — and for whom?

It is live because both answers are being enacted right now. Austin bet on supply and added ~30% to its housing stock; St. Paul (ep08's subject) bet on regulation. Meanwhile the loudest version of this argument on social media — "building luxury apartments raises my rent" — is the one claim the peer-reviewed evidence most clearly contradicts, while the version experts actually argue about almost never gets said out loud.

Settled (not the debate)

  • New market-rate supply lowers rents at metro scale. Austin added ~120,000 units, ~30% stock growth, 2015–2024; asking rents fell 4% from 2021 to 2025 and 16% from the December 2021 peak. — Pew, 18 Mar 2026
  • The cheapest units fell hardest, not least. Austin Class C rents −11.4% vs Class A −2.6%; buildings of 50+ units −7% (2023→2024). — Pew / RealPage
  • Filtering is real and it reaches poor households. Per 100 new market-rate units, 45–70 people vacate below-median-income tracts and 17–39 vacate bottom-quintile tracts, almost all within five years. — Mast, Journal of Urban Economics 2023
  • Filtering is also slow. Real annual rental filtering runs ~2.5%/year, putting genuine low-income affordability on a 20–40 year clock. — Rosenthal, AER 2014
  • New buildings in low-income areas do not raise nearby rents on average. Nearby rents fell 5–7% within ~250m of large new buildings in low-income areas. — Asquith, Mast & Reed, REStat 2023
  • Construction costs are up ~42% (inputs, Feb 2020 → Jan 2026), which is why approved projects sit unbuilt. — BLS PPI via AGC
  • Local opposition is real and lopsided. 63% of comments at zoning and planning meetings were in opposition. — Einstein, Glick & Palmer, Perspectives on Politics 2019

Contested (the real fight)

Side A — "Build": Supply works and the evidence is one-directional. Metro-level rents fall, neighbourhood-level rents fall, and the vacancy chains reach low-income households within five years rather than a generation. The units poor renters actually live in — Austin's Class C stock — fell fastest of all. The obstacle is not economics but the veto: 63% of hearing commenters oppose, and construction costs up 42% mean approved projects only get built when rents outrun the cost of capital. Blocking supply is choosing the shortage.

Side B — "Build, but not for us": The effect is real, metro-scale, and far smaller per unit than the slogan implies. Austin needed ~30% stock growth — a volume almost no American city has ever politically sustained — to move asking rents 4%. The honest claim is that rents rise 20% instead of 30%, not that they fall. And the costs land locally and immediately on exactly the people the policy claims to help: in Minneapolis, new construction had no average effect on nearby rents but raised rents on the cheapest tercile of existing buildings by +4.4% over five years. The strongest version: supply studies measure the rents of units, not the housing costs of people. When an incumbent renter is priced out, they leave the rent panel — the unit's price is observed, their outcome is not.

The reframe

What's actually 50/50 is not whether rents fall — it's whether the people already living there are still there to enjoy it. Two peer-reviewed studies looked at new buildings in poor neighbourhoods and got opposite signs at the bottom of the local stock: Asquith/Mast/Reed found −5–7%, Damiano & Frenier found +4.4% for the cheapest tercile. Verification showed most of that gap is method, not disagreement — they price different things, on different samples. But a real residual survives: both agree new supply lowers high-end rents; they disagree on whether an amenity effect dominates at the bottom. That is the genuine contested core, and it is far narrower and far more defensible than "does building work."

And a second reframe the verification pass produced on its own: almost every headline number in this fight is a measurement window wearing a conclusion's clothes. Austin is −16% or −4% depending on whether you start at the peak or the annual average. Both from the same source. Both true.

Both sides quietly agree

Nobody in this debate is funded to argue about the asset. Both camps fight over rent. US homeowner equity stands at $34.1 trillion (Fed Z.1, Q4 2025) and median homeowner net worth is $396,200 against a renter's $10,400 — the largest leveraged position in the world on housing staying scarce, and no organized movement campaigns against it.

⚠️ Scope discipline required here. The tempting line — "the people blocking housing are the people who own it" — is not established by the study behind the 63% figure; its authors say homeownership "cannot be assessed using the voter file." State the equity stock and the participation imbalance as two separate verified facts. Do not weld them into a causal claim.

Verdict framing

No winner, and the episode says plainly that the direction question is not a tie — the evidence leans toward supply, and pretending otherwise would be the dishonest move. What is handed to the viewer is the question the literature genuinely cannot settle: whether the specific low-income households already living near new construction end up better off, measured on people rather than on the units they used to occupy.

The viewer's tool, offered explicitly: ask what window a number is measured over. Austin taught it in this episode; St. Paul teaches the same lesson from the opposite political direction in ep08.

Reader's caution — what this brief will not say, and why

The following were checked and failed. They are common in popular coverage and will read as missing to anyone who has seen them:

  • "40,000+ units delivered in Austin 2023–2025" — fabricated, appears nowhere in the source.
  • "Tokyo's 1968 law created 12 zones" / "Tokyo rents stayed flat while London and New York doubled" — the first is false (eight zones in 1968; twelve after 1992), the second has no primary source.
  • "The meta-analysis of supply effects" — no formal meta-analysis exists.
  • Pew's causal framing — the article asserts causation with no methodology, and never addresses tech layoffs or migration reversal. The episode names those as live competing explanations.
