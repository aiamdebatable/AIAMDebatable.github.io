# Fact-check log — Does Building More Housing Actually Lower Rents?

_Independent verification pass, run 2026-08-19 (web-sourced, not trusting the draft). Each claim gets a
verdict: **CONFIRMED**, **CORRECTED**, **UNRESOLVED** or **APPROXIMATE**. This is a REQUIRED gate in
this show's production process. The show's differentiator is that this file is real._

- **CONFIRMED** — the source says what the episode says.
- **CORRECTED** — the source says otherwise; the script was changed, and the change is listed under
  "Corrections applied to source" below. These stay in the file: they are the point of it.
- **UNRESOLVED** — could not be established from a source worth trusting, so it is not used on air.
- **APPROXIMATE** — checked, roughly right, and **deliberately not cited on air**.

**Verification status:** adversarial-pass

_Method: five independent research lenses produced the claim set; seven verification agents then
checked every numeric and named claim against its primary source, with no access to the lenses'
reasoning. Two claim clusters were given an explicit presumption against on sourcing grounds. Shared
with [ep08](../../ep08-rent-control/research/factcheck-log.md), which draws on the same pass._

---

## 1. "Austin added roughly 120,000 units — about 30% stock growth — between 2015 and 2024."
**CONFIRMED.** Verbatim in the Pew analysis; supply figures derive from ACS.
- https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2026/03/18/austins-surge-of-new-housing-construction-drove-down-rents

## 2. "Austin asking rents fell 4% from 2021 to 2025, and 16% from the December 2021 peak."
**CONFIRMED, with both baselines required.** Pew: "Asking rents decreased 4% in both the city and the
surrounding suburbs from 2021 to 2025" (Apartment List data). Separately, median rent $1,546
(Dec 2021) → $1,296 (Jan 2026) = −16.2%. Both figures are real and describe the same market: −16% is
peak-to-point, −4% is annual-average to annual-average. **Quoting −16% without naming the peak
baseline is cherry-picking and the episode does not do it.**

## 3. "40,000+ units were delivered in Austin between 2023 and 2025."
**CORRECTED.** This figure **appears nowhere in the cited source in any form.** It was fabricated
upstream. Replaced with the sourced figure: ~120,000 units, 2015–2024.

## 4. "Austin Class C rents fell 11.4% while Class A fell 2.6%; buildings of 50+ units fell 7%."
**CONFIRMED.** Both verbatim, sourced to RealPage Explore via Pew's July 2025 study. Caveat carried
on air: this is a one-year, metro-frame comparison.

## 5. "The evidence shows new construction *caused* Austin's rent decline."
**UNRESOLVED.** The Pew article asserts causation in its title and text ("the efforts worked") but
contains **no methodology, no regression and no appendix**, and the study it leans on is hedged to
"associated with." Demand softening is not addressed anywhere in it — zero mentions of layoffs,
remote work or migration, in a metro where tech layoffs are a live competing explanation. **The
episode states the correlation and names the confound; it does not claim causation.**

## 6. "A 10% ZIP-code supply increase bought ~1.4pp slower rent growth."
**CORRECTED.** Real, but from a **different publication** — Pew's 31 July 2025 ZIP-code study (1,654
Zillow ZIP codes, 2017–2024), not the Austin article it was attributed to. Citation corrected.
- https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2025/07/31/new-housing-slows-rent-growth-most-for-older-more-affordable-units

## 7. "Large new apartment buildings in low-income areas cut nearby rents 5–7% within ~250m."
**CONFIRMED.** Asquith, Mast & Reed, *Review of Economics and Statistics* 2023, 105(2):359–75. Abstract
says "about 6 percent"; the introduction gives the 5–7% range. Three difference-in-differences designs,
1,483 buildings over 50 units, 11 strong-market cities. **Scope caveat carried on air:** restricted to
"pioneer" buildings in below-median-income tracts; the all-buildings extension was "noisy…
indistinguishable from zero"; 3-year follow-up; disamenities not ruled out.
- https://direct.mit.edu/rest/article-abstract/105/2/359/100977

## 8. "Per 100 new market-rate units, 45–70 people vacate below-median-income neighbourhoods within five years."
**CONFIRMED, with a method correction.** Verbatim from Mast, *Journal of Urban Economics* 2023,
133:103383 — "45–70 and 17–39 people… below-median and bottom-quintile income tracts… almost all
within five years." **CORRECTED:** the design is address-history tracing across 52,000 residents plus
a simulation model, **not** a quasi-experiment. Describing it as causal evidence overstates it, and
the episode does not.
- https://research.upjohn.org/up_workingpapers/307/

## 9. "New construction raised rents on the cheapest existing buildings nearby by 6.7%."
**CORRECTED to 4.4%.** The +6.7% / −1.7% pair is from a **pre-2020 draft**. The peer-reviewed version
— Damiano & Frenier, *Journal of Urban Affairs* 2026 — reports **+4.4% over five years** for the
bottom tercile, with no average effect overall. The stale figure makes the conflict with claim 7 look
sharper than it is.
- https://www.tonydamiano.com/project/new-con/bbb-wp.pdf

## 10. "Studies 7 and 9 contradict each other."
**CORRECTED — mostly an artifact, with a real residual.** Distance is not the explanation (250m vs
300m, both DiD). They measure different things: Asquith prices *listings* in low-income tracts and
keeps only pioneer buildings across 11 hot markets; Damiano prices *existing buildings sorted by their
own baseline rent* across all of Minneapolis. Asquith never estimates a bottom-tercile effect, so no
figure of his contradicts +4.4%. **The genuine residual, which the episode presents as the contested
core:** both agree new supply lowers high-end rents; they disagree on whether an amenity effect
dominates at the bottom of the local stock.

## 11. "Real annual rental filtering runs about 2.5% per year."
**CONFIRMED.** Rosenthal, *AER* 2014, 104(2):687–706, verbatim: "real annual filtering rates are faster
for rental housing (2.5 percent) than owner-occupied (0.5 percent)."
- https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.104.2.687

## 12. "Displacement risk fell near new construction, yet nearby parcels became more likely to gentrify."
**APPROXIMATE.** Pennington finds displacement (moves to poorer ZIP codes) −17.14% within 500m, while
parcels within 100m were 2.5pp (29.5% relative) more likely to gentrify, decaying to zero by 700m.
Both directions are real and the pairing is the episode's central tension — but the paper is **still
an unrefereed working paper**, retitled "Rents, Movers, and Stayers" in Nov 2025. Used to frame the
open question, **not cited as a settled number on air.**

## 13. "Auckland's 2016 upzoning cut rents 22–28% below synthetic control."
**CORRECTED to ~28%, with the dispute characterised honestly.** Greenaway-McGrevy & So, *Economic
Inquiry* 2026: ~28% below synthetic control at six years, over 2017–2024, donor pool of 51 NZ
commuting zones. Estimates range **14–35% by dwelling type**, so the headline is segment-dependent.
The prominent critique (Murray & Helm) is an **unreviewed Substack post attacking a different paper**,
and a peer-reviewed rebuttal in *Land Use Policy* (2025) found the critiques had little merit.
**The episode does not stage this as a 50/50 dispute, and does not hide that a critique exists.**

## 14. "Auckland consents hit a record 12 per 1,000 residents in 2022."
**CORRECTED.** The figure (12.7 per 1,000, July 2022) is real Stats NZ data, but it was **not a
national record** — Canterbury was higher at 13.2. "Record" removed.

## 15. "Upzoning raises land values without producing buildings."
**CONFIRMED, with the author's own caveat mandatory.** Freemark, "Upzoning Chicago," *Urban Affairs
Review* 56(3):758–789 (2020): 2013/2015 transit upzonings raised allowed density 17–50% and parcel and
condo values 15–23%, with **no** effect on permitted dwellings over five years. **The abstract itself
limits the findings to "short-term, local-level," and Freemark has publicly objected to the paper
being read as anti-upzoning.** Stating it as "upzoning doesn't build housing" misstates the author,
and the episode does not.
- https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1078087418824672

## 16. "Tokyo's 1968 City Planning Law created 12 national land-use zones."
**CORRECTED.** The 1968 Act set **eight** use zones; the **1992** amendment raised the count to twelve.
The underlying mechanism — zoning is national, and localities have very limited power to block
conforming development — is confirmed. The number and date were wrong and are removed.

## 17. "Tokyo rents stayed flat while London's and New York's doubled."
**UNRESOLVED.** Traces only to blog and think-tank commentary; no primary or peer-reviewed source was
found, and contrarian analyses note Tokyo's housing-stock growth actually slowed after the reforms.
**Not used on air.**

## 18. "A meta-analysis of the supply literature finds…"
**CORRECTED.** **No formal meta-analysis of new-supply effects on rents exists.** The best synthesis —
Been, Ellen & O'Regan, "Supply Skepticism Revisited," *Housing Policy Debate* 35(1):96–113 (2024) — is
a **narrative literature review** with no pooled effect sizes. On air it is described as "a review of
the literature."
- https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10511482.2024.2418044

## 19. "Construction costs are up about 40% since 2019."
**CONFIRMED.** BLS PPI via AGC, Feb 2020 → Jan 2026: inputs to new construction **+42.2%**; new
nonresidential building construction +42.5%.
- https://www.agc.org/sites/default/files/users/user21902/PPI%20Tables%202026_1.pdf

## 20. "63% of commenters at local zoning meetings oppose new housing, and they are disproportionately homeowners."
**CORRECTED — the first half only.** Einstein, Glick & Palmer, *Perspectives on Politics* 17(1), 2019,
verbatim: "Sixty-three percent of all comments were in opposition" (22.8% neutral), from zoning and
planning board minutes across **97 eastern Massachusetts municipalities, 2015–2017**, matched to the
state voter file. **The homeowner half is not supported:** the authors state that homeownership "we
cannot assess using the voter file," and the overrepresentation claim rests on a single-town case
study. The two facts are stated separately on air and never welded into a causal claim.
- https://maxwellpalmer.com/docs/articles/Einstein_Glick_Palmer_Participation.pdf

## 21. "US homeowner equity stands at $34.9 trillion (Fed Z.1, Q1 2026)."
**CORRECTED to $34.1 trillion, Q4 2025.** The cited release is the March 2026 Z.1, which covers **Q4
2025, not Q1 2026** (Q1 2026 data was not released until June 2026). Table B.101 line 51, "owners'
equity in household real estate": **$34,148.5B**. $34.9T matches no quarter in that release.
- https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/20260319/html/b101.htm

## 22. "Median homeowner net worth is $396,200 against a renter's $10,400."
**CONFIRMED.** Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances 2022.

## 23. "It costs about $1 million to build one affordable unit in San Francisco; hard costs run $360K–$450K per unit."
**APPROXIMATE.** The hard-cost figures are verbatim from SF Planning's feasibility report ($360K
low-rise, $450K high-rise, "between 50% to 75% of total development costs") — but that is a **2020
white paper based on 2017–2019 studies**, six years stale, and it prices **market-rate rental**, not
affordable. **The ~$1M-per-affordable-unit figure is not in that report at all**; it traces to press
reporting of Mayor's Office of Housing data. Direction is sound; **no precise figure is stated on air.**

## 24. "Multifamily starts ran 74% below the 2021 peak in Q2 2025."
**CORRECTED.** Census/NAHB 5+ unit starts: 2021 = 462K, 2022 peak = 531K, 2024 trough = 336K, Q2 2025
≈ 380–430K SAAR — roughly **10–20% below 2021**, not 74%. The accompanying yield-on-cost spread
figures are **UNRESOLVED**: sole source is an industry blog. Neither is used on air.

## Corrections applied to source

- "40,000+ units delivered 2023–2025" → removed entirely; replaced with ~120,000 units, 2015–2024. No
  earlier render shipped this claim.
- Austin rent decline → both baselines now stated (−4% average-to-average, −16% from the Dec 2021 peak).
- Causal language on Austin → downgraded to correlation, with tech layoffs and migration reversal named
  on air as competing explanations.
- 1.4pp ZIP-code figure → re-attributed to Pew's July 2025 study.
- Damiano & Frenier bottom-tercile effect → +6.7% corrected to the published +4.4%.
- Mast → described as address-tracing plus simulation, not a quasi-experiment.
- Auckland → ~28% with the 14–35% by-dwelling-type range; "record" consents claim dropped; the critique
  described by its actual publication status.
- Freemark → the "short-term, local-level" qualifier is now spoken, not implied.
- Tokyo → zone count and date corrected; the rent comparison cut for lack of any primary source.
- "Meta-analysis" → replaced everywhere with "a review of the literature."
- Homeowner equity → $34.1T (Q4 2025); the "disproportionately homeowners" causal link removed.
- Multifamily starts and the yield-spread cluster → cut.

## Fairness audit

- **Neither side written beyond its evidence?** The supply-side case is the one the evidence favours on
  direction, and the brief says so explicitly rather than manufacturing a 50/50. The contested core was
  deliberately relocated to the distributional question, where peer-reviewed work genuinely disagrees.
- **Straw-manning / cherry-picking?** The strongest anti-supply claim available (Damiano's bottom-tercile
  effect) is presented at its corrected, *weaker* published value rather than the more dramatic draft
  figure — a choice against the episode's own dramatic interest. The Austin peak-baseline disclosure
  cuts the same way against the supply side.
- **Sourcing symmetry:** two claim clusters were flagged for industry sourcing and given a presumption
  against. That presumption turned out to be **wrong in both directions** on the New York cluster, where
  the strongest claim came from the state regulator and a "landlord" claim actually originated with a
  tenant-side nonprofit. Recorded here because judging claims by which side benefits would have gotten
  three of four wrong.
- **Identity↔stance rotation:** not yet applicable — cast rotation is step 4, not run in this pass.

## Open questions this episode could not settle

- Do the specific low-income households already living near new construction end up better off, measured
  on **people** followed over time rather than on the units they used to occupy? This is the frontier
  question and no lens or source answered it.
- How much of Austin's rent decline is supply and how much is demand (tech layoffs, migration reversal)?
  No source addressed this, and it is the single biggest threat to the episode's flagship evidence.
- Does social and public housing at scale (Vienna, Singapore, the Dutch housing-association sector)
  outperform both options on the board? Every lens accepted the build-versus-control frame without
  examining it.
