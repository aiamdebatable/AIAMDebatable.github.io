Housing Prices: Does Building More Housing Actually Lower Rents?

Does new market-rate housing supply lower rents — and for whom?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in August 2026, analysts kept pointing at Austin. Between 2015 and 2024 it added about 120,000 homes, about 30% more housing, and its asking rents fell.

Settled

What both sides accept

Gold granted the direction: new market-rate housing lowers rents across a city, and both sides agree it lowers rents at the top of the local market. The chain of moves down to cheaper homes is real but slow, putting genuine low-income affordability on a 20 to 40 year clock.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesJust build

Austin added about 30% more housing in nine years and asking rents fell, with the cheapest apartments falling most. The same result shows up at three scales: city, zip code and block, where big new buildings in low-income areas were followed by nearby rents 5 to 7% lower. What blocks building is cost and objectors at hearings.

Gold arguesBuild for whom

On direction, the evidence favors building. But in Minneapolis, the cheapest third of existing buildings near new construction got 4.4% more expensive over five years while the rest didn't move. And the studies follow apartments, not people: whoever gets priced out leaves the data, so a place can post falling rents and rising displacement at once.

What the shouting gets wrong

The fight over whether building lowers rents is mostly over, and the builders won it. What's still open is the bottom of the local market, where two good studies found opposite effects, and whether the people already living there end up better off. We measure apartments, not people, and nobody has closed that gap.

The verdict

The two cases, as the judge put them
The case for building rests on evidence that reproduces at three separate scales, and on a mechanism you can follow from the new tower down to the oldest block on the street.
The case against rests on what those measurements leave out, and on how long the chain takes to arrive for the people waiting at the bottom of it.
