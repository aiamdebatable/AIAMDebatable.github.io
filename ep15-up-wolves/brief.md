# Research brief — Who Should Manage the Wolves — and by What Rule?

_The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video._

**Episode type: DESIGN.** Green and gold each steelman a *design* for managing the wolves of
Michigan's Upper Peninsula, not a position on whether wolves are good. The verdict is what survives
both designs plus the one dial the viewer sets. Chain: none (a deliberate category change after ep12
and ep14).

## Why now

**On 4 September 2026 the President ordered the federal government to decide, within 90 days, whether
the gray wolf still needs federal protection — and, either way, to make it easier to kill one.**
Executive Order 14425, *Supporting America's Ranchers* (published 10 Sep 2026, 91 FR 57765), §3
directs the Secretary of the Interior, within 90 days (**3 December 2026**), to (a) determine whether
the gray wolf and the Mexican wolf "have met the recovery criteria for delisting or downlisting" under
the Endangered Species Act and "begin the process of delisting or downlisting them" if so; (b) submit
"a legislative recommendation to fully delist or downlist" them; (c) on a recovery finding, "engage
with States to encourage them to delist gray wolves … from any State-specific lists" and "revise their
standards for lethal takings"; (d) "consider" amending producer-compensation regulations so
depredation claims are "consistently, accurately, and expeditiously assessed"; and (e) "consider"
revising "the Department's criteria for authorizing lethal gray wolf and Mexican wolf removal".
[primary, read in full: Federal Register text and the White House PDF]

That order is the episode's question stated as federal policy: *who* manages the wolf (Washington
under the ESA, or Lansing under a state plan) and *by what rule* (a population objective and a hunt,
or removal only for a confirmed conflict). Every other live item is one of the two designs trying to
happen:

- **The count.** On 6 Aug 2026 the Michigan DNR and Michigan State University released the first
  camera-based estimate of UP wolves: 1,311 cameras, a "space-to-event" model, monthly estimates from
  **525 (March 2025) to 1,198 (October 2024)** — a seasonal cycle (pups born in spring, winter
  mortality), not a margin of error. The 2026 winter track survey — the number the press reports as
  "the population" — is a **minimum of 792**. The DNR's Tyler Petroelje: the track survey "was doing
  exactly what it was designed to do, and that is to get a minimum count of wolves in wintertime." The
  two methods have been compared once, for Feb/Mar 2024: camera 773/794, track 768. [primary, read]
- **The delisting bill is stuck.** H.R. 845 (Pet and Livestock Protection Act) passed the House
  211–204 on 18 Dec 2025. Its text is two sentences: the Secretary "shall reissue" the 3 Nov 2020
  delisting rule within 60 days, and that reissuance "shall not be subject to judicial review." The
  Senate companion S.1306 (Johnson, Barrasso, Lee) is the same text. On 4 Aug 2026 Sen. Johnson sought
  unanimous consent; Sen. Booker objected. The bill dies with the Congress on 3 Jan 2027. [bill texts
  primary, read; the floor objection: E&E News, 5 Aug 2026]
- **The courts are the other track.** The 2020 nationwide delisting was vacated on 10 Feb 2022 (N.D.
  Cal., *Defenders of Wildlife v. USFWS*), restoring pre-2020 status: wolves are federally
  **endangered** in Michigan and Wisconsin, **threatened** in Minnesota. The Ninth Circuit appeal is
  reported to be argued on 13 Oct 2026 (Sportsmen's Alliance; not confirmed on the docket). The Great
  Lakes population was delisted in 2012 and relisted by court order in Dec 2014, affirmed in *Humane
  Society v. Zinke* (D.C. Cir. 2017). In Jan 2025 the Fish and Wildlife Service found a bear-hunter
  petition to delist a Western Great Lakes population "not substantial". [primary: WI Legislative
  Council brief; FR 90-day finding; FWS 2025 Species Status Assessment]
- **Michigan has a hunt bill waiting for the trigger.** HB 6008 (Rep. Prestin, introduced 21 May
  2026) changes one word in MCL 324.40110b: within 90 days of the state's wolf-take statutes becoming
  operable (i.e. federal delisting), the Natural Resources Commission "~~may~~ **shall**" issue orders
  establishing annual wolf hunting and trapping seasons. It is tie-barred to a companion bill not yet
  numbered. [primary, read]

**Description peg:** EO 14425 and its 3 Dec deadline; the camera survey. **Script (structural):** the
Endangered Species Act vs the Michigan Wolf Management Plan; 792 and 525–1,198; the Wisconsin
February 2021 hunt as the record of what a mandated season did; the 2016 Ontonagon kills as the
record of what conflict-removal did. Never the personalities.

## The question

**Who should manage Michigan's Upper Peninsula wolves, and by what rule?** Two designs:

- **GREEN — "Manage them like a game species."** Delist. The state sets a population objective, the
  Natural Resources Commission opens a licensed hunting and trapping season, farmers and dog owners
  may shoot a wolf in the act. The Wisconsin and Minnesota model whenever delisting has been in force;
  HB 6008; S.1306.
- **GOLD — "Manage the conflict, not the number."** Keep protection. No recreational season. A wolf is
  killed only after the state verifies it has killed livestock or a dog; losses are paid at market
  value; non-lethal deterrence first. Michigan's own regime in the windows when it was allowed to run
  it (2003–2022).

It is live because both designs are on a clock at once: a 90-day federal determination, a Senate bill
dying in January, a state bill that would make a season mandatory, and a state population estimate
that just got a second number.

## Settled (not the debate)

1. **How many.** At least 792 wolves in the UP in winter 2026 (track-survey minimum); roughly 500 to
   1,200 across the year (camera survey, by month). The two DNR methods measure different things by
   design and agreed where they overlapped (2024). No camera estimate for winter 2025–26 has been
   published. — DNR/MSU release, 6 Aug 2026; DNR wolf-population-research page.
2. **The state has no target.** Michigan's 2022 Wolf Management Plan: "This plan does not identify a
   target population size, nor does it establish an upper limit for the number of wolves in the
   State." Its 200 is a 1990s federal-recovery viability floor and "not a target population size."
   Estimates of the UP's biological carrying capacity "are imprecise and range from 600 to 1,350." —
   Wolf Management Plan 2022, §5.1.1.
3. **The neighbours do it differently.** Minnesota's 2022–32 plan manages to 2,200–3,000 wolves;
   Wisconsin's 2023 plan replaced its numeric goal with abundance bands. — the two plans (finder-read).
4. **What wolves actually do to livestock in Michigan.** 1998–2021: 320 verified wolf–livestock
   depredation events on 105 farms (10% of the UP's ~1,000 livestock farms), about 13 a year. 1996–2021:
   94 verified attacks on domestic dogs, 64% of them hunting hounds. The most recent year Bridge
   Michigan reported: 6 confirmed livestock kills, 5 dogs. — Wolf Management Plan 2022, §6.10; Bridge.
5. **What Michigan has paid.** $213,712 in compensation, cumulative 1998–2024; 100% of fair market
   value for a verified kill under the 2012 Wildlife Depredations Indemnification Act; nothing for
   dogs. Wisconsin paid $322,970 in 2024 alone and about $3.9M since 1985 — but its program pays for
   missing calves and Michigan's does not, and Minnesota's own director attributes its 2025 record to
   livestock *prices* with claims flat. Dollars are not a conflict count. — FWS SSA 2025; WI DNR;
   Agweek.
6. **Michigan's wolves are ENDANGERED, not threatened.** Since the Feb 2022 vacatur, the Fish and
   Wildlife Service's 2025 assessment says: outside the windows when lethal control was permitted,
   "management of depredating wolves in Michigan was limited to nonlethal methods, such as scare
   tactics and aversion conditioning." Minnesota's wolves are threatened and managed under a 4(d) rule
   that lets USDA Wildlife Services remove depredators (147 in 2022). In its permitted windows,
   2003–2022, Michigan removed 92 wolves for depredation — 53 by the DNR and Wildlife Services, 39 by
   private citizens under permit — between 1 and 18 a year. Today: none. — FWS SSA, Nov 2025.
7. **Nobody has been attacked.** No documented wolf attack on a human in Michigan. Across Europe and
   North America, 2002–2020: 12 attacks, 14 victims, two fatal (Saskatchewan 2005, Alaska 2010), with
   about 60,000 wolves in North America. — Linnell, Kovtun & Rouart, NINA Report 1944 (2021), read.
8. **Wolves are not what is thinning the deer herd.** The DNR's 2009–2020 Predator–Prey Project:
   coyotes kill the most fawns, then bears, bobcats, and wolves last; wolves are the top predator of
   adult does at 8.6% of doe mortality, but 68% of those kills come in late winter and spring and
   nearly half the does were already malnourished. "Winter weather has a much greater impact on the
   deer numbers than wolves." Winter 2025–26 had 111 days of 12-inch-plus snow, the fourth most severe
   since 2003; the 2025 UP firearm harvest fell about 15%, which the DNR attributed to weather, not
   predators. — DNR "Factors Limiting Deer Abundance" (2023), read; Iron Mountain Daily News; TV6.
9. **Every Great Lakes delisting has been vacated.** 2012 → vacated 2014 (affirmed 2017); 2020 →
   vacated 2022. The only lower-48 regime that has run unbroken is Idaho/Montana since a 2011
   congressional rider barred judicial review — the mechanism H.R. 845 and S.1306 copy. — WI
   Legislative Council; bill texts.
10. **Both designs use the same gate.** Under either design, the DNR or USDA Wildlife Services
    physically verifies a depredation before compensation or any control action. The designs differ
    only in what is allowed *after* confirmation, and whether anyone may kill a wolf that has killed
    nothing. — Wolf Plan §6.10; FWS SSA.

## Contested (the real fight)

**GREEN — Manage them like a game species.** Recovery was the promise and it was kept thirty years
ago: 792 wolves against a 200-wolf floor, four times over, in a state whose own plan says carrying
capacity may be 600 to 1,350 and the fall population is near 1,200. A recovered population under a
permanent emergency statute is a design failure, and it produces exactly what Michigan has: a
plan that cannot name a number, a council that has not met since 2022, and a regime in which the state
may not touch a wolf that is eating a farmer's calves. Every other game species is managed to an
objective by a commission that hears from the people who live with it; Minnesota manages to
2,200–3,000 and its three hunts took 923 wolves with no measurable population effect. A season is not
extermination; it is a lever the Upper Peninsula has never been allowed to hold. The February 2021
Wisconsin hunt is the case against *mandamus*, not against management — the agency was ordered to
run a breeding-season hunt it had not designed; give the commission the season on its own calendar and
it will look like Minnesota's, not Jefferson County's. And the conflict, small in a statewide total, lands on the same hundred farms and the same
hound-hunters every year; in the plan's own 2021 survey, 63% of livestock-producer respondents wanted
fewer wolves within five years, against 24% of the general public.

**GOLD — Manage the conflict, not the number.** The record of the design GREEN wants is the February
2021 hunt: 218 wolves in 63 hours against a state quota of 119, one every 17 minutes, 86% run down
with hounds, the tribes' 81-wolf share consumed because registration lagged the kill; then a lawsuit,
then an injunction. That was not a court hijacking the design — a mandated season is the design, and
HB 6008 turns the same "may" into "shall." Meanwhile the thing the season is supposed to fix does not
respond to it: Michigan's dog kills bear no relationship to wolf abundance; no study anywhere shows a
recreational season lowering depredation; the Michigan-specific study of lethal removal (Santiago-Avila
2018) found its local benefit statistically insignificant and offset by recurrence nearby; Minnesota's
hunts moved neither the population nor the claims curve. The conflict is real and it is small — about
13 verified livestock events and 4 dog attacks a year, $214,000 in 26 years, zero humans — and it
concentrates on ten percent of farms and on hounds released near dens in bear season. Pay 100% for a
confirmed kill, remove the confirmed animal, put fladry and guard dogs on the hundred farms, and keep
the count honest. And note who never gets a seat in GREEN's design: the Ojibwe bands whose 1836 and
1842 treaties cover the UP and who, in Wisconsin, hold that their treaty right includes the right to
*protect* wolves.

**Where they actually disagree** (four questions, not one):

1. **Does killing wolves reduce conflict?** Wielgus & Peebles (2014) found lethal control associated
   with *more* depredation the next year; Poudyal et al. (2016) re-ran the same data and got the
   opposite sign; nobody has refereed it. The literature is about targeted removal versus non-lethal
   methods. Almost nothing tests a recreational *season*.
2. **Who decides?** A federal court, a Secretary with a 90-day clock, a legislature with a 90-day
   mandate, a game commission, or treaty tribes with a share.
3. **What is the number for?** Michigan refuses to set one; Minnesota sets one; Wisconsin abolished
   its own. The camera survey shows "the population" is a wave from 500 to 1,200.
4. **Is "zero attacks" the right safety metric?** Europe manages *bold* behaviour before an attack.
   The one time Michigan invoked human safety — three Ontonagon wolves in 2016 — Bridge Michigan's
   FOIA'd emails showed a technician hazing a wolf at 60–80 yards with a horn and a warning shot, and a
   single cattle operation that had received about $50,000 in state payments.

## The reframe

The shouting treats "delist" as a switch that turns a hunt on, and "protect" as a switch that turns
conflict management off. Neither is true. Michigan's wolves are *endangered*, not threatened, so
today's regime is stricter than the one GOLD describes and stricter than Minnesota's next door:
**no lethal removal at all**, even of a wolf standing in a calf pen. And GREEN's population objective
has nowhere to land: Michigan's plan refuses to name a number, Wisconsin abolished its number, and the
state's own new survey shows the population is a 500-to-1,200 wave, not a figure a season can be set
against. The real dial is **who may kill a wolf that has killed nothing — and who decides**. Both
designs also lean on an unproven premise: GREEN that fewer wolves means fewer losses (Michigan's dog
kills track nothing about abundance), GOLD that fladry and guard dogs cut repeat strikes (no farm-level
Michigan evidence exists). The number everybody quotes, 792, is a floor. The number that would settle
the fight — whether either regime changes the thirteen-farms-and-four-dogs-a-year reality — has not
been collected for Michigan since 2022.


## The deer, micro and macro (added 2026-09-12 after validation)

Lucas's ask: put context around "what you feel" versus what is reported. The state's own documents
supply both scales, and both are true:

| scale | figure | source |
|---|---|---|
| one wolf | 15–19 deer a year (Mech 1971, Keith 1983, Fuller 1989); as many as 37–50 (Pimlott 1967, Huntzinger 2004) | Wolf Management Plan 2022, §4 |
| a pack's ground | 3.6–8.3 wolves per 100 sq mi across the western UP's three snowfall zones | DNR "Factors Limiting Deer Abundance" (2023), Predator–Prey Project |
| the deer on that ground | 984–1,891 deer per 100 sq mi, same zones | same |
| the arithmetic | ~100 sq mi × ~6 wolves × 15–50 deer ≈ 100–300 deer a year, 10–20% of the deer standing on a pack's territory | derived — spoken as "ten to twenty percent" |
| the peninsula | wolves lowest fawn predator; top adult-doe predator at 8.6% of doe mortality, 68% of it late winter/spring, ~half already malnourished; buck harvest tracks winter severity, not the wolf count | same report |

So a 200-acre camp inside an active territory in a hard winter *will* find adult kills, and the
report's sentence that "winter weather has a much greater impact on the deer numbers than wolves" is
also right. The script says which scale each claim lives at (s04_cost.d and .l).

**The perception record.** Hunting-forum threads are paywalled to automated readers, so the on-air
"what people report" is sourced to two documents instead: Bridge Michigan (2021) — sportsmen with
trail cameras "say the sheer number of wolf images they collect must mean there are more wolves … than
the DNR's estimated 631" — and the plan's own survey gap: 31% of interested UP producers believed
wolves had taken their stock (Beyer et al. 2006) against 10% of farms with a verified event. The
camera survey the DNR launched in 2022 is, in its own release, partly an answer to that perception.

## Both sides quietly agree

Both accept the same gate (a verified kill before any payment or any control), both keep the
compensation program (a state statute that has run through every listing status since 1998), both
agree a confirmed chronic depredator should be removable by someone, and both agree the population is
far above viability and is not what limits the deer herd. Neither, in this record, argues for a
population near 200 or for zero lethal control ever. The buried consensus — targeted removal of a
confirmed problem animal, paid losses, and a count that reports honestly — is roughly what Michigan
ran from 2003 to 2022 in its 4(d) windows and cannot run today.

## Verdict framing

No winner. What survives both designs: the verification gate, market-value compensation, removal of a
confirmed chronic depredator, a population far above the floor. What each design still has to prove:
GREEN, that a season does anything to conflict that targeted removal does not; GOLD, that non-lethal
tools cut repeat strikes on the hundred farms that carry the losses. **The dial the viewer sets: what
counts as a conflict worth killing a wolf over** — a calf, a hound, a deer, or the feeling of being
watched — because in Michigan's own 2021 survey the only scenario where a majority would kill a wolf
was an attack on a human, which has never happened. The judge may name the second dial: who should
hold that decision — a commission, a legislature, a court, a Secretary, or the treaty tribes.

## Tack-on topics noticed during research (for the bench, not this episode)

- **Isle Royale** — 37 wolves, moose down 75% since 2019, 24% predation rate, first census with no
  calves: a closed-system predation experiment the NPS started by translocating 19 wolves. A
  design/ethics episode of its own ("should we manage an island?").
- **Colorado's reintroduced wolves** — a GOLD-style regime under strain: ~$1.07M in 2025 claims
  against a $350k fund, six lethal removals, chronic-depredation directive OW-15.
- **"Delist by Congress"** — the 2011 Idaho/Montana rider and H.R. 845's judicial-review bar: should
  a legislature be able to switch off the courts for one species?
- **Hounds** — 64% of Michigan's dog attacks and 86% of Wisconsin's 2021 kills involve hunting
  hounds; the same practice is the conflict and the tool.
- **Who owns the count?** — track survey vs camera model vs public perception; a media-literacy
  episode on minimums, ranges and the word "population".
