Wolf Hunting in Michigan: Who Should Manage the Wolves, and by What Rule?

Who should manage Michigan's Upper Peninsula wolves, and by what rule: delist and hunt to a state target, or keep protection and kill only for confirmed conflict?

Two designs for a recovered predator — and Washington just put a 90-day clock on one of them.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

On September 4, 2026, the President signed an order giving the Interior Secretary 90 days, to December 3, to decide whether the gray wolf should come off the endangered list. A delisting bill had passed the House and was stuck in the Senate, and a Michigan bill would require a hunting season if the wolf were delisted.

Settled

What both sides accept

Both teams accepted that there are at least 792 wolves, and 500 to 1,200 across a year as pups are born and winter takes them. There are about 13 verified livestock attacks a year, on one farm in ten, no wolf has ever attacked a person in Michigan, and winter runs the deer herd more than wolves do. Both also want a farmer able to act on the wolf in front of him.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesManage them like a game species

The wolf recovered: Michigan passed the federal floor of 200 wolves in 2000, and the 2026 winter count was at least 792. Delist it, let the state set a number and the commission set a season, and let a farmer shoot the wolf in his calf pen. A plan with no number has decided by default.

▶ Hear Green at 3:39 on YouTube
Gold arguesManage the conflict, not the number

Attacks follow a few packs, not the size of the population. Remove the wolf that kills, pay full value for losses including dogs, and put guard dogs and other tools on the hundred farms that carry the losses. Minnesota's three hunting seasons moved neither the wolf count nor the losses.

▶ Hear Gold at 9:23 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

Since February 2022, nobody in Michigan may kill a wolf except to protect a human life, so both teams were arguing about a tool the state doesn't have. The real dial is who may kill a wolf that has killed nothing: a licensed hunter, nobody, or someone in between. And who decides that: a court, a secretary on a clock, a legislature, a commission, or the tribes whose treaties cover the ground.

The verdict

What survives both teams
The wolf that kills is removable, by the farmer in the act and by the state on confirmation. Losses paid at full value, dogs included. The count published as a floor and a range, never as one number. And a plan that says what the number is for, even if the answer is a band and not a line.
What didn't hold up
Green's own engine: a recreational season as a tool against attacks. No study anywhere has measured it, and she said so. And gold's tools on the farm: fladry and guard dogs, unmeasured in Michigan, and he said so.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: What counts as a conflict worth killing a wolf over?

The judge handed you this question and declared no winner. You could count more than one. Open one to see what the record shows about that harm, and what it says about acting on it. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1A calf
    What the record shows
    About 13 verified attacks on livestock a year: 320 from 1998 to 2021, on 105 of more than 1,000 livestock farms in the Upper Peninsula, one farm in ten. Nearly a third of producers believed they'd lost stock to wolves. The conflict follows packs, not the count.
    What the record says about acting on it
    Both teams want the farmer able to act on the wolf in front of him, and the design that survives both makes the wolf that kills removable, by the farmer in the act and by the state on confirmation. But the one Michigan study of lethal removal found no statistically significant effect, at the farm or nearby, and 57% of Michiganders surveyed did not support killing a wolf for livestock.
    Not measured
    No study anywhere has measured a hunting season as a tool against attacks, and fladry and guard dogs are unmeasured in Michigan.

    On the record: row 9 of the fact-check log, row 31 of the fact-check log, row 11 of the fact-check log, row 17 of the fact-check log, row 20 of the fact-check log

  2. 2A hound
    What the record shows
    94 verified wolf attacks on dogs from 1996 to 2021, about four a year, and 64% of them were on hunting hounds. Michigan pays nothing for a dog.
    What the record says about acting on it
    Most Michiganders surveyed did not support killing a wolf for a hunting dog. The judge kept the Gold team's answer instead: pay for the dogs. The design that survives both teams pays losses at full value, dogs included, and makes the wolf that kills removable.

    On the record: row 9 of the fact-check log, row 10 of the fact-check log, row 20 of the fact-check log

  3. 3A deer
    What the record shows
    Wolves eat deer. One wolf kills between 15 and 50 deer a year, and among adult does in the Upper Peninsula, wolves are the top predator, at 8.6% of deaths. If your camp sits inside a pack territory, in a hard winter, you will find wolf kills.
    What the record says about acting on it
    Winter runs the herd: the state's report says winter weather has a much greater impact on the deer numbers than wolves. Most wolf kills of does came in late winter and spring, and nearly half the does wolves killed were already starving. And the one record of a designed season, Minnesota, moved neither the population nor the losses.

    On the record: row 30 of the fact-check log, row 13 of the fact-check log, row 14 of the fact-check log, row 16 of the fact-check log

  4. 4The feeling of being watched
    What the record shows
    No wolf has ever attacked a person in Michigan. Across Europe and North America over 18 years, researchers found 12 attacks and two deaths. An attack on a person is the only case in which a majority of Michiganders surveyed accepted killing a wolf.
    What the record says about acting on it
    The one time the state used the human-safety exception, three wolves near Ontonagon in 2016, Bridge Michigan later reported from the state's own emails that the incident was a technician honking a horn and firing a warning shot from 60 to 80 yards away, on behalf of a farm that had received about $50,000 in state payments.

    On the record: row 12 of the fact-check log, row 20 of the fact-check log, row 19 of the fact-check log

Whatever you count: Today in Michigan, nobody may kill a wolf for anything but a human life. Not the state, not the farmer. And two dials stay open under every answer: who may kill a wolf that has killed nothing, and who decides that.

What our fact-check caught

Our drafts got these wrong before they aired. Every correction is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidSince 2022, Michigan has allowed a wolf to be killed only after a confirmed attack on livestock.

    The record saysThat was the rule from 2003 to 2022. Since February 2022, no wolf may be killed in Michigan to stop attacks on farms; the only exception is to protect a human life.

  2. Draft saidUnder current rules, a wolf may be killed if it's caught in the act of killing livestock or dogs.

    The record saysThat's Michigan's own state law, and it doesn't operate while the wolf is on the federal endangered list.
