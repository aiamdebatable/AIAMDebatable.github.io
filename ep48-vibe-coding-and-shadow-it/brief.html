Episode 48 · The research

Research brief — Vibe Coding: When Anyone Can Build the App, Who Keeps It Safe?

The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video.

Episode type: DEBATE. Two teams argue at full strength; the verdict declares no winner and hands the viewer one dial to set for their own tool. Why a debate: the record settles who carries the duty when a business-built tool leaks (the company that holds the data, under each of the three regimes read: New York, California and the SEC's disclosure rule), but it cannot settle how much review a tool needs before it goes live, or how fast to let building spread, because we found no measurement of whether business-built tools are less safe than what they replace, or whether IT's guardrails prevent anything. That is where the two sides genuinely split (Contested, below).

T1 — apps built on the wrong data

  • No business team's vibe-coded app is on record for this; the pattern is. England's lost Covid cases (2020): 15,841 cases from 25 Sep to 2 Oct dropped in a hand-built loading step ("a file-size limit", PHE's words), reached contact tracing late (T1-1); peer-reviewed work tied each late referral to measurable extra infections (T1-4). The Excel row cap airs as "the BBC reported" (T1-2). It was PHE's own pipeline, not shadow IT (T1-3).
  • "Right tool, wrong use": West Coast Main Line, 2012. A model built for one job was reused for another and its outputs misread (real vs nominal), understating by nearly half; the award was cancelled, £8.9m in direct costs by Dec 2012 (T1-6, T1-7).
  • The AI case is one founder's account: Replit's agent, by its user's account, also made fake data, fake reports and a database of 4,000 people who didn't exist (T1-12 to T1-14). "He said"; a trial build (beyond the deletion, B44).
  • A tool contradicting the official source: Air Canada's chatbot; a tribunal held the company responsible either way (T1-10; the decision text is still to be opened before air).
  • The famous cost figures are weak: Gartner's "$12.9m" is vendor reference customers' estimate (T1-16); the "$3.1 trillion" doesn't air (T1-17). How often business-built tools use the wrong source: we found no measurement (T1-19).
  • Regulators: banks only. Basel/ECB want one authoritative source and an inventory of user-built apps (T1-25, T1-27), and few banks comply (2 of 31; 0 of 25) (T1-26, T1-27). ⚠ Correction to this brief: US SR 11-7 was replaced on 2026-04-17 by SR 26-2, which leaves spreadsheet arithmetic and generative/agentic AI outside its scope (T1-20 to T1-22). "Regulators require banks to validate vibe-coded tools" is wrong for the US.
  • Airable as a question: "If the person who built it is the only one who knows where its numbers come from, who checks the numbers?"

T2 — when the builder leaves, and can IT find the app?

  • The platforms say it themselves. Google: "If someone leaves your organization and their account is removed, access to scripts without other owners is lost" (T2-22); untransferred files are permanently deleted after a 20-day window (T2-25). Microsoft: a simple flow's owner "is part of the flow identity" (T2-26); a leaver's OneDrive goes after 30 days by default (T2-28). Replit: deleting a personal account removes every app on it (T2-33).
  • Where it was built decides it. A company workspace hands work on (Lovable transfers a leaver's projects except Restricted ones, T2-29). A personal account leaves the company no reach: only the builder can move the app in, irreversibly and without its secrets (T2-34, T2-35). This IS the dial's "does anyone else know it exists?"
  • Finding it is partial: discovery matches traffic against a catalogue of known services (T2-38); one employee can connect an outside app to their own mailbox by default (T2-40). "Can't see what's inside" is our reading.
  • Bank regulators have asked the leaver question since 2011 (inventory, input sources, owners, documentation "for continuity of operations", T2-1 to T2-3); Metro Bank's £5.4m fine named "key person dependencies" (T2-11, T2-12; a dependency story, nobody left).
  • Airable only as a question: whether an orphaned app keeps running. The documents support "nobody can fix it", not "it stops" (T2-37). We found no costed case of a business-built tool orphaned by its builder, and no count (T2-21, T2-43).

T3 — why people go around IT, and the governed middle

  • The case FOR building, sourced: the UK NCSC says shadow IT is "rarely the result of malicious intent", names a slow request process as a cause, and recommends a fast, controlled route, not blame (T3-1, T3-2). IT leaders' own (vendor) surveys say half to 59% didn't finish the year's projects (T3-3, T3-4). But builders' first reasons are speed and convenience, and one IT-leader survey ranks "IT can't meet the timeline" last at 9% (T3-7, T3-10): say "it's faster and easier", never "IT is the bottleneck".
  • What building delivers: we found no independent measurement. Every time-saved figure is a self-estimate; the one audit (GSA's 240,000 hours) found the claim "inaccurate and unreliable" (T3-12; centrally built bots).
  • The owner models documented: Microsoft on its own staff (Microsoft's claim) is the only full build-run-retire model: a risk rating sets the review, enterprise tools need two named owners re-attesting every six months, idle personal environments are deleted at 90 days, a personal agent is deleted when its owner leaves (T3-23 to T3-27). Gartner's zones (a prescription): staff build in green/yellow; customer-facing or customer data = no staff building (T3-28, T3-29). Stripe (its claim): a quarter of staff prototyped on mock data; engineers rebuild for production (T3-15, T3-30, T3-31). Ramp is a high-trust example, not a guardrail one (T3-36); its "1,500 apps" doesn't air (T3-17). Who is on call for a non-engineer's app at Ramp or Stripe: not published (T3-38).

The pitch, in one line

Anyone at work can now build the app. When it leaks, the law already puts the duty on the company that holds the data, and doesn't ask who built the tool. What nobody has settled is the part before the leak: does anyone know the tool exists, and what data does it touch? New York's and California's data-security laws put the duty on whoever owns, licenses or maintains the data, and the SEC's rule covers systems the company "owned or used", with no carve-out for who built them (A5, A6, A17, A27). The one public filing found that names an unapproved AI tool came from a bank: an internal incident in which customers' names, Social Security numbers and dates of birth were handled with "an unauthorized artificial intelligence-based software application" (A8, A10, A14). And the company selling one of the tools told investors, in its IPO filing, that the law on its own liability for its agents' actions is "unsettled" (P6, P9): its own exposure, in required risk language. The real fight is not IT against the business. It is how much a tool touches before someone else has to know it exists.

The viewer's moment

It starts with a question, not a policy: "If I build something at work with AI and it leaks, who's on the hook?" The episode answers it first, plainly: legally, the company that holds the data, under every law read (A17, A27); the one filing on record came from an internal incident in which customer data was handled with an AI application nobody had authorized (A8); and the person who built it is not off the hook in every sense, because the documented consequences for staff are a trade-secret lawsuit and a threat of firing, not hacking charges (A38, A39, A41). Then the question goes to three people, each with one moment and one number. (Invented and unnamed. Each is built only from figures in the log.)

  • The salesperson who built a tool that works. The moment: it runs, the team loves it, and it took an afternoon. The number: in Anthropic's own study of its coding tool's users, sales is among the fastest-growing non-software groups, and non-engineers' code-producing sessions succeed 29% of the time against 34% for software workers (D12, D13). ⚖ What working is not: in a peer-reviewed benchmark, an AI agent's solutions worked 57% of the time but only 11.8% were secure (B31); and in a study of people coding with an assistant, those with AI were more likely to believe their code was secure (B28).
  • The IT person who finds it after it leaks. The moment: the first they hear of the app is the incident. The number: among organisations that had a data breach, the share whose incidents involved "shadow AI" (staff using unapproved AI) more than doubled, from 20% in IBM's 2025 study to 43% in 2026 (B6). IBM separately prices not knowing how many apps exist and where ("shadow IT") at about $201,000 added to an average breach (B17). ⚖ Both are shares and costs among breached firms, and neither separates a chatbot paste from an app staff built (B1, B6). In Microsoft's own tools, every employee can reach the default environment, and the sharing audit runs only after sharing (C27).
  • The manager who has to sign the breach notice. The moment: a letter to customers with their name on it. The number: in New York, notice to affected residents within 30 days of discovering the breach (A22); for a public company that judges the incident material, an SEC filing within four business days of that decision (A1). The real case: the bank became aware on 5 May 2026, judged it material on 7 May and filed on 11 May (A8, A9, A12). ⚖ Not every leak to an AI tool means letters: New York waives individual notice for an inadvertent disclosure by staff allowed to see the data, if the company documents that misuse is unlikely (A26).

All three leave with the same check they can run themselves: does anyone else know this tool exists, and what data does it touch?

The question

If I build something at work with AI and it leaks, who's on the hook? It sits under the title's question, when anyone can build the app, who keeps it safe?, and the record forces two more: how much does a tool have to touch before someone else must know it exists; and is any of this changing IT work?

Why now

Peg hunt run 2026-10-27 (peg-hunt skill), then every peg passage re-checked verbatim against the saved filing by hand pass A (P1–P13). Re-run the hunt before scripting: an S-1 is amended before an IPO prices.

On 26 October 2026 Anthropic filed to go public (P1). In the registration statement it gave the Securities and Exchange Commission, the company says it is "already seeing" non-technical teams "increasingly adopting" its coding agent, Claude Code, and its sister product Claude Cowork "to manage their own workflows" (P2). The risk factors then name the other half of this episode's question. The company may face claims for harm caused by its agents' autonomous actions "even where the customer configured, deployed, or supervised the agent", because the legal frameworks for liability for actions taken autonomously by AI systems "are unsettled" (P6, P9). It adds that enterprise deployments "will require broad permissions and deep integrations with customers' internal tools, data, and workflows", which "increases the potential consequences of any security incident" (P10), and that incidents caused by "customer misconfigurations or misuse" may still bring it liability exposure (P11). The company selling the tool is telling investors two things in writing: people outside IT are building with it, and nobody has yet decided who answers when what they build goes wrong. (Anthropic, PBC, Form S-1 registration statement, filed 2026-10-26, SEC EDGAR — https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm ; filing index — https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/0001628280-26-067168-index.htm)

⚠ Corrected by hand pass A: the peg hunt drafted the S-1's "AI-generated code" sentence as a warning that concerns about AI-generated code could make enterprises limit use. In context it sits in the open-source licence risk factor: AI-generated code could reproduce open-source code so that copyleft obligations "apply to us without our knowledge", about Anthropic's own use of Claude Code; one follow-on sentence says "similar concerns" could lead enterprise customers to limit their use (P12). It is not a security warning and is not used as one. ⚠ The same clause still stands in the metadata's news_peg.what (not edited by this pass; see Open items).

What the filing does not say. The adoption line is the company's own claim to investors, with no count: no passage reached gives a user number for non-technical teams or for Cowork, only that Cowork's early adoption "outpaced" Claude Code's at the same point (P4). The "non-technical prosumers" line is about individual Pro and Max subscribers, not employees (P5). The liability passages are risk factors, a required disclosure of what could happen, not admissions that it has (hand pass A). Context, not the peg: in Q3 2026, Claude Enterprise users who did not use Claude Code spent under $75 a month on average, while the top 5% who did spent about 20 times that (P13).

Why this fits the thesis. What the viewer saw (the IPO) is not what the episode argues. The filing matters because the seller of the tool states both halves of the question in a legal document: building is spreading beyond engineers, and who answers for it is open. The script carries the structural version ("the companies selling these tools now tell the SEC, in writing, that non-technical teams are adopting them, and that the law on who answers when their agents cause harm is not settled"; it is the seller's own exposure, said in required risk language); the description carries the hot one. Name the filing, never the personalities; never in the title or thumbnail.

Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

The duty sits with the company that holds the data, whoever built the tool

  • SEC: a public company's Form 8-K for a material cybersecurity incident is due four business days after it determines the incident is material, a decision that must come without unreasonable delay after discovery (A1, A2). Materiality includes harm to reputation and customer relationships (A3). The rule covers information systems "owned or used by" the company, and materiality "is not contingent on where the relevant electronic systems reside or who owns them" (A5, A6). The SEC refused to exempt third-party systems; it says nothing about AI tools or employee-built apps, whose coverage follows from "owned or used by" (A7, APPROXIMATE: a sound reading, not an SEC statement).
  • New York: any person or business that owns or licenses New Yorkers' private data "shall develop, implement and maintain reasonable safeguards" (A17). The examples of compliance include assessing risks in software design and training employees (A18). Only the Attorney General enforces it (A19). Affected residents must be notified within 30 days of discovery (A22).
  • California: a business that owns, licenses or maintains Californians' personal information must keep reasonable security (A27). Consumers can sue only when unencrypted data is taken or disclosed because of that failure, for $107 to $799 per consumer per incident or actual damages (A28, A29).
  • Said plainly: none of these texts asks who built the tool or whether IT approved it. That is a reading of the statutory text; no court, attorney general or SEC statement found applies it to an employee-built AI tool (hand pass A).

The one filing on record was about using, not building

  • CB Financial Services (Community Bank, Pennsylvania): on 5 May 2026 the bank became aware of "an internal incident involving the handling of certain non public customer information using an unauthorized artificial intelligence-based software application" (A8). It judged the incident material on 7 May "due to the volume and sensitive nature" of the data, and filed on 11 May (A9, A12). Names, Social Security numbers and dates of birth were disclosed (A10). No disruption, no expected material financial impact, root cause still under investigation, notices under way (A11, A13).
  • It is the only such filing found. A 17-query EDGAR full-text search of 8-Ks from 2025 to 27 October 2026 found no second Item 1.05 filing mentioning AI and no Item 8.01 incident report naming an AI tool, shadow IT or AI-generated code (A14). Limit: the search sees only literal wording.

The security record measures four different things, and none of them is "business-built apps"

  • Breached organisations (IBM and Ponemon): security incidents involving shadow AI, "where workers use unapproved AI", rose to 43% of the 602 breached organisations studied in 2026, from 20% in 2025 (B6, B12, B1). They cost an average of $5.39M, against $4.99M for all breaches (B7, B8). 68% of breached organisations had no AI governance policy or were still writing one (B9); 19% said governance and security teams coordinated (B10). Separately, attacks on AI models or apps rose to 21% from 13%, mostly through surrounding APIs and cloud misconfiguration, not AI-written code (B13, B16).
  • Raw models in a lab (Veracode, a scanner vendor): syntax correct over 95% of the time, security pass rate stuck near 55%, flat since 2023 except reasoning models; four flaw types, no agent, no review (B20, B22, B23, B24, B18).
  • Agents on real code (peer-reviewed): 57% of one agent's solutions worked; 11.8% were secure (B31).
  • Deployed AI-built apps: an unreviewed 2026 preprint audited 200 deployed apps built with Claude Code and Lovable and found at least one vulnerability in 91% (B32); a vendor (RedAccess) counted about 2,000 publicly reachable corporate apps exposing sensitive data, which the platforms dispute and WIRED could not confirm (B35–B37); a second vendor's passive scan of 5,600+ apps found 2,000+ vulnerabilities and 400+ exposed secrets (B42, B43). None shows the builders were business staff working outside IT.

The platforms put it on the user, and have failed on their own side too

  • Terms: Replit: "You remain responsible and accept liability for your content, apps, and projects" and AI code "may be erroneous or incomplete" (B53, B54). Lovable: the user is responsible for the apps they build, liability is capped at 12 months' fees, and AI output "should not be relied upon without independent review and testing" (B57–B59). Lovable's 2025 CVE record is formally disputed on exactly that ground (B62).
  • Incidents: in July 2025 Replit's agent deleted a production database; its CEO called it "unacceptable" and Replit then separated development and production databases (B44, B45, B48). From 3 February to 20 April 2026, public Lovable projects' chat history and source code could be reached by any Lovable user with a link; researcher reports from 22 February were closed without escalation; Lovable says it shipped a fix within two hours, and is making public projects private (B49–B51).

History: the one-person, unreviewed tool is a recurring failure mode, at its real size

  • JPMorgan, 2012: the value-at-risk model ran on Excel sheets filled by copy and paste (C3); a formula divided by the sum of two rates instead of their average, which "likely" muted volatility by a factor of two, by an amount the task force could not pin down (C1). Reviewers called the process error prone and set an automation deadline; nobody checked whether it happened (C2). The firm relied on the model's builder to operate it (C2). ⚖ The task force put principal blame on the trading strategy, not the spreadsheet; losses were about $5.8bn by 30 June 2012 (C5).
  • Reinhart and Rogoff: the spreadsheet error alone moved the over-90%-debt growth figure from 2.2% to 1.9%; excluded years and weighting did most of the rest (C7, C8, C9).
  • Spreadsheet audits: careful field audits found errors in most of the spreadsheets they audited (at least 86% in 2000's better audits; 94% of 85 in 2015's), with small, old samples (C10, C11).
  • Auditors already have a category for this: "End-user computing controls are controls over spreadsheets and other user-developed applications" (PCAOB staff, 2009; C12). Inspectors caught audit firms not testing the spreadsheets and queries controls relied on (C13), and companies have disclosed material weaknesses over them (C14, C15).

We found no independent count of the builders

  • No independent source counts people outside engineering who build software with AI (D14). Census is firm-level: 17–20% of US businesses used AI recently (D1), and in 36% of firms where workers use AI there is no formal firm-level adoption, the closest official number to "shadow AI", covering any AI use, not building (D9). The richest evidence is Anthropic's own data on its own users (D11–D15).

Guidance is tiered, not a ban

  • The UK's cyber agency says full vibe coding "can often be perfectly fine" for prototypes, demos and internal tools with limited exposure, and should slide toward manual coding for login logic, sensitive personal data and secrets (C31). Its CTO for Architecture says non-technical builders get code that is "often unreliable, hard to maintain, or has critical issues" (C32). The joint guidance from Australia's cyber centre, CISA, the NSA and partners says organisations "should only use agentic AI for low-risk and non-sensitive tasks", with no high-impact actions without prior human approval (C33).

Insurance is splitting, not settled

  • Cyber insurers are mostly clarifying how existing wording applies to AI, with targeted exclusions discussed "in some pockets" (A43); QBE says an AI-triggered conventional incident stays covered (A44). ISO's generative-AI exclusions are for general liability, not cyber (A46). Coalition and Beazley added "affirmative AI" cyber cover (A49, A50), but Beazley's addresses attackers using AI and is silent on a client's own AI use (A51). The staff-built tool sits in a gap nobody has written wording for (hand pass A).

Contested (the real fight)

The record settles who carries the duty. The fight is about how much review a tool needs before it goes live, who does it, and how fast to let building spread, on evidence that measures none of these directly.

Side A — "Let the business build." (Steelmanned from the checked rows.)

  • It is already happening, and banning hides it. In 36% of US firms where workers use AI there is no formal firm-level adoption (D9). Among AI-using firms, Sales and Marketing is the most common function (52%; D6). Anthropic told investors non-technical teams are "increasingly adopting" its coding tools (P2; the company's claim). In its own study, business and financial operations are the second-largest group of Claude Code users after computer jobs, and management, sales and legal are growing fastest (D12).
  • Non-engineers can do it. Their code-producing sessions succeed 29% of the time against 34% for software workers (D13; Anthropic's data). A small academic case study found most non-programmers finished a basic web app with AI help and supported the approach (D24).
  • The law already makes it the company's problem, so the answer is to see it, not stop it. The duty sits on the company whoever builds (A5, A17, A27); California's coming audit asks for software inventories and approval processes, not bans (A31). Microsoft offers an agent registry anyone with a role can view (C30).
  • The official advice is tiered, not a ban. The UK's cyber agency: full vibe coding "can often be perfectly fine" for limited-exposure internal tools (C31).
  • The scary numbers are not about business-built tools. Veracode tests raw models on four flaw types, with no agent, no review and no security prompt (B18, B22, B24). IBM's 43% counts breached firms whose incidents involved staff using unapproved AI, not apps they built (B6). One user study found AI users' critical bugs no more than 10% above a control group (B30). No source compares AI-built tools with the spreadsheets, macros and Access databases business staff built before (hand pass B).
  • The business has always built. Auditors have treated spreadsheets and "other user-developed applications" as a control category since at least 2009 (C12); at Deutsche Bahn, Microsoft reports 4,000 citizen developers with 500+ apps in production (C17, a vendor case study).
  • The platforms are fixing the defaults. Replit separated development and production databases (B48); Lovable is making public projects private (B51).

Side B — "Govern it first." (Steelmanned from the checked rows.)

  • The one filing on record is a close cousin, not the thing itself: staff using an unapproved AI tool, not building one. Customer names, Social Security numbers and dates of birth went into "an unauthorized artificial intelligence-based software application", and the bank carried the disclosure and the notices (A8–A13).
  • Working is not safe. 57% working, 11.8% secure, for an agent on real code (B31). 91% of 200 deployed AI-built apps had at least one vulnerability, in a preprint (B32). In the lab, security sits near 55% while syntax passes over 95% of the time (B20). And people coding with AI were more confident their code was secure, and 36% of those with AI wrote solutions open to SQL injection, against 7% of those without (B28, B29).
  • The officials say non-experts are the risk. The UK cyber agency's CTO for Architecture: output for non-technical builders is "often unreliable, hard to maintain, or has critical issues", with risks "likely far outside most organisations' risk tolerances" (C32). The Five Eyes agencies: agents only for "low-risk and non-sensitive tasks" (C33).
  • The platforms push the risk onto you, and fail too. Their terms make the user liable (B53, B57, B58); public apps being reachable is "expected behavior", with privacy "a single click" away, in Replit's CEO's words (B38); a vendor counted about 2,000 exposed corporate apps (B35, disputed). Replit's agent deleted a production database (B44); a Lovable flaw left public projects' chats and code reachable by any logged-in user with a link for about 11 weeks (our arithmetic; no evidence anyone used it), with researcher reports closed unescalated (B49, B50).
  • History shows how it fails: one builder's tool, a review that flagged the risk, and no follow-up. JPMorgan relied on the model's builder to operate it, and nobody checked the automation deadline (C2); the error was treated as a one-off (C4). Wegmans paid New York $400,000 after failing to "inventory its cloud assets containing personal information" (A42). Orphaned apps "can pose a security risk", and Microsoft's free governance kit is no longer maintained (C28, C29).
  • Governance is thin where it matters. 68% of breached organisations had no AI governance policy or were still writing one; 19% said governance and security coordinated (B9, B10). Microsoft's data policies are off until an admin creates them and cannot tell test from production (C26).
  • The seller says its own liability is unsettled. Anthropic says the law on liability for its agents' actions is "unsettled" (its own exposure, in required risk language, not a ruling about staff-built apps) and may land on it even where the customer set the agent up (P6, P9); deployments need "broad permissions and deep integrations" (P10). Insurance wording for a staff-built tool is unwritten (A51, A46).
  • The cost is forecast to land on IT. Gartner forecasts that by 2028, 30% of software engineering teams will be under material strain maintaining vibe-coded apps built by business users, and 60% of vibe-coded apps will be retired for failing to deliver predictable value (C21: forecasts, not measurements; the note is distributed by a governance vendor, C23).

Where the record lands, said plainly (no false balance)

  • On "whose problem is it when a business-built tool leaks?" the record leans one way: the company's. Every regime read puts the duty on whoever owns, licenses or maintains the data, or on systems the company "owned or used" (A5, A17, A27). No court or regulator has yet applied that to an employee-built AI tool, so it is the law's text, not a ruling (hand pass A).
  • On "is it already causing breaches?" the record shows unapproved AI USE in breaches, not business-built apps. IBM's 43% (from 20%) counts breached firms whose incidents involved staff using unapproved AI; it is not a share of all companies and does not separate pasting into a chatbot from a tool someone built (B6, B1). The one filing is about using a tool (A8).
  • On "is AI-built code insecure?" the record separates lab from life. Raw-model lab tests say about half (B20, B24); one peer-reviewed agent benchmark says 11.8% secure (B31); deployed-app evidence is a preprint and vendor scans (B32, B35–B43). Nothing measures apps business staff actually shipped.
  • On "how many non-engineers are building?" nobody knows. No independent count exists (D14).
  • On history, the one-person, unreviewed tool is a recurring failure mode, and the famous cases are smaller than their legends. The London Whale spreadsheet understated risk; the strategy lost the money (C1, C5). The Reinhart-Rogoff typo was about 0.3 of a point (C8).
  • On "do guardrails work?" they exist, and we found no measurement of whether they work. No incidents-avoided figure exists for data policies, inventories or registries (C26–C30); the "89% drop" when sanctioned tools are provided is one vendor's figure with no stated method (C24).
  • On IT jobs, nothing ties the changes to business teams building. IT-services payrolls are about 5% below their March 2023 high (D63); BLS projects programmers down 7% and software developers up 10% (D64, D65); no source links any of it to business-unit building (D75).

Netthe duty is settled, the damage is measured only loosely, and the remedy is unmeasured. That is

why the honest fight is over the dial, not the turf.

The industry beat: what it does to software and IT work

One beat, kept small, because the record is thin and the sister episode (ep46) carries jobs.

  • What is measured: IT-services payrolls (computer systems design) fell from 2,483,500 in March 2023 to 2,355,400 in September 2026 (preliminary), about 5.2% (D63, our arithmetic; IT-services firms, not in-house IT departments). BLS projects computer programmers down 7% and software developers, QA analysts and testers up 10% from 2025 to 2035 (D64, D65); computer user support specialists down 3%, with chatbots named (D66). Young workers aged 22–25 in AI-exposed jobs are 19% below where they would be had they kept pace with less-exposed peers, a descriptive signal whose authors say it is not causal (D43, D48). Coder employment is still growing, just much more slowly (D59).
  • What is said or forecast: Gartner forecasts worldwide IT spending up 13.5% in 2026 (D71). In a survey by an investor in IT services, 67% of 200+ US IT executives plan bigger IT teams, and 55% expect to replace some commercial software with AI-built tools (D72, D73). Gartner's 2028 forecasts above (C21).
  • Demand for the tools themselves is soft and mixed: of 1,720 US panellists with a Lovable receipt in late 2025, 168 had one again in May–August 2026, which YipitData itself warns is a receipt count, not a churn rate (D26, D28).
  • Said plainly: the risk is measured; a change in what IT jobs are is not (consistent with ep46's "Is the way in moving?", T3 there). Nothing ties IT job changes to business teams building (D75).

The reframe

The shouting frames it as a turf war: the business against IT, speed against control. The record points somewhere quieter: visibility and data. The engine's synthesis said the honest core is "does anyone know this tool exists, and what data touches it?" (engine summary). Tested against the hand passes, it holds, with two qualifications.

What bears it out:

  • The law doesn't ask who built it. The duty follows the data and the systems the company uses (A5, A6, A17, A27).
  • The trigger in the one real case was the data. The bank judged it material "due to the volume and sensitive nature" of the information (A9), not because of who wrote any code.
  • The failures the record documents are failures of knowing. Wegmans failed to inventory its cloud assets (A42); IBM prices "lack of visibility into the number and location of applications" (B17); Microsoft's sharing audit runs only after sharing, and orphaned resources can pose a risk (C27, C28); JPMorgan's review deadline was never followed up and the builder operated his own model (C2).
  • The rules coming are about knowing. California's audit will ask for software inventories, allowlisting and approval processes (A31); Microsoft's answer for agents is a registry (C30).
  • The guidance tiers by what a tool touches. Prototypes and limited-exposure tools at one end; login logic, personal data and secrets at the other (C31); agents only for low-risk, non-sensitive tasks (C33).

The two qualifications:

  1. Code quality is a second, real layer. The lab and benchmark numbers (B20, B31) and the one deployed-app audit (B32) say AI-written code is often insecure. "It doesn't matter who wrote the code" overstates it; what is true is that the record cannot yet show business-built code is worse than what it replaces.
  2. Knowing is the common thread of the evidence, not a measured cure. Most of the "visibility" evidence is analogy (spreadsheets, cloud storage, low-code), and no source measures that inventories or registries prevent incidents (C26–C30).

So the useful question for a viewer who builds a tool at work is not "am I allowed to build?" It is "does anyone else know this exists, what data does it touch, and who looks after it when I leave?"

Both sides quietly agree

Even while arguing, both camps accept the same three things:

  1. The company carries the duty. "Let the business build" relies on it (the company must see what exists); "Govern it first" relies on it (the company pays when it doesn't) (A5, A17, A27, A8).
  2. Risk scales with what the tool touches. The UK's cyber agency tiers by exposure (C31); the Five Eyes guidance tiers agents by sensitivity (C33); the platforms' own fixes are private-by-default and separate production data (B48, B51); and the seller's filing ties the stakes to "broad permissions and deep integrations" (P10).
  3. Some kind of inventory is the minimum. California's audit (A31), the Wegmans settlement (A42), IBM's shadow-IT cost (B17) and Microsoft's agent registry (C30) all come back to knowing what exists.

And both are short of the same evidence: neither side has a measured outcome. The citizen-developer promise rests on vendor case studies, a commissioned study that left "reduced shadow IT" unquantified, and forecasts (C17, C18, C19, C21, C25); the guardrails have no incidents-avoided figure (C26–C30). The real disagreement is over who builds and how fast, not over whether an unlisted tool holding sensitive data is a problem.

Verdict framing

No winner. The judge says what the record settles (the company carries the duty; the one filing came from customer data in an unapproved tool; we found no measurement of either the damage from business-built apps or the benefit of guardrails) and hands the viewer one dial: "How much does your tool touch?" The viewer sets where their own tool sits, and where on the dial "someone else must know it exists" begins. The judge may name the settings; the judge never sets the dial for the viewer.

Three settings, each with a real institution behind it and a documented failure:

  1. "Just me, nothing sensitive": a prototype, a demo, a helper with limited exposure. The UK's cyber agency: full vibe coding "can often be perfectly fine" here (C31). Documented failure: "just me" on a public platform can still be public. Replit's CEO calls public apps reachable "expected behavior" (B38), and a vendor found about 2,000 corporate apps exposing data by searching the platforms' default domains (B35, B40; disputed).
  2. "My team, company data": someone else knows it exists, and it has an owner who isn't leaving. Behind it: California's coming audit asks for software inventories and approval processes (A31); Microsoft's agent registry (C30). Documented failure: Wegmans failed to inventory its cloud assets and paid New York $400,000 (A42); orphaned apps can pose a security risk (C28); JPMorgan's review deadline that nobody checked (C2). Weakness: no source measures that inventories prevent anything (C26–C30).
  3. "Customers' personal data, money, logins, or the open internet": a second pair of eyes before it goes live. Behind it: the UK cyber agency's slide toward manual for login logic, personal data and secrets (C31); the Five Eyes "low-risk and non-sensitive" line and human approval for high-impact actions by agents (C33). Documented failure: the bank's customer data in an unapproved AI tool (A8, A10); 11.8% secure in the agent benchmark (B31); the builders who were more confident than they should have been (B28). Weakness: review slows the work, and its benefit is unmeasured too.
