Vibe Coding: When Anyone Can Build the App, Who Keeps It Safe?

When business teams build their own apps with AI, who should govern, support and secure them?

Last changed 2026-11-01 · See every change

It works and the team loves it. Who checks it, and who looks after it when the builder leaves?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

On October 26, in its filing to go public, Anthropic told investors that non-technical teams are increasingly adopting its coding tools, without giving a number. So anyone at work can now build an app just by describing it to an AI, and when the team loves it, someone has to ask who checks it and who looks after it when its builder leaves.

Settled

What both sides accept

Both teams accepted that if a business-built tool leaks, the duty sits with the company that holds the data, whoever built it. The one filing found involved a bank's customer data in an AI tool nobody had approved. And neither side could say how many of these apps exist, whether they're less safe than the spreadsheets they replace, or what a review prevents: nobody has measured any of it.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesGovern it first

Working isn't safe: in a peer-reviewed test on real software projects, about six in ten of one AI agent's solutions worked and about one in eight were secure, and people coding with an AI assistant were more likely to believe their code was secure. Wrong data can hide inside a tool that works, as England's lost Covid cases showed, and when a builder leaves, the platforms' own pages show the company can be left unable to fix it. So once an app touches customers' data, money or logins, someone besides its builder checks it before it goes live, and it gets two owners, one who isn't leaving.

What the shouting gets wrong

It sounds like a turf war: the business against IT, speed against control. That's not where the record puts the fight. Who pays when an app leaks is settled: the company. The question that's left is quieter: does anyone besides the person who built it know the app exists, and what does it touch?

The verdict

Where the record leans
Where the record leans: the risk rises with what a tool touches. The UK's cyber agency, the five countries' guidance on agents, Gartner and Microsoft's own rules all draw the line there, and the companies that govern this give every tool that matters an owner who stays.
What didn't hold up
That people build mainly because IT is too slow; one survey, mostly of IT managers, ranked that last. That the alarming numbers are about business-built apps. That an app always dies with its builder; in a company workspace it can pass on. And that US bank rules require checking AI-built tools.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: How much does your tool touch?

The judge handed you a dial for your own tool: how much does it touch? These are the three settings the judge named, from just you to customers' data, and nobody was declared the winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Just you, nothing sensitive
    What it buys
    Speed, with nobody to ask. People build because it's faster and easier, and rarely out of malice. The UK's cyber agency says fully vibe-coding a prototype, or an internal tool with limited risk, can often be perfectly fine. Gartner draws a similar line: staff build small, personal tools. At Stripe, by its own account, 25% of staff have built prototypes on realistic mock data, and engineers rebuild the ones worth keeping.
    What it costs
    Public is still public: for about 11 weeks this year, the code and chat history of public projects on Lovable could be opened by any Lovable user with a link. And working isn't safe: in a test of one AI agent on real software projects, about one in eight of its solutions were secure. Under Microsoft's rules for its own staff, a personal tool gets one owner and is deleted after 90 days unused.
    Not measured
    Whether these apps are any riskier than the spreadsheets and macros business teams built before: nothing on the record compares them.

    On the record: row C31 of the fact-check log, row T3-1 of the fact-check log, row T3-15 of the fact-check log, row B49 of the fact-check log, row T3-23 of the fact-check log

  2. 2Your team and company data
    What it buys
    Someone else should know it exists, without a ban. Ban it, and it doesn't stop; it just stops being visible: in more than a third of US firms where workers use AI, Census Bureau researchers found no sign the firm had formally adopted it. The UK's cyber agency's advice is a quick, controlled route to what people need, and no blame.
    What it costs
    Wrong data can hide inside a tool that works: in England in 2020, nearly 16,000 positive Covid cases were left out of the daily figures at a hand-built step, the health agency's own process. And 'in the open' only works if someone's looking: in Microsoft's product as shipped, every employee can reach the shared default space, and the sharing check runs after the sharing. Britain's bank regulator, fining Metro Bank, named its dependence on the small number of people familiar with its spreadsheets.
    Not measured
    How often business-built tools pull their numbers from the wrong source: we found no measurement.

    On the record: row D9 of the fact-check log, row T3-2 of the fact-check log, row T1-1 of the fact-check log, row C27 of the fact-check log, row T2-12 of the fact-check log

  3. 3Customers' data, money or logins
    What it buys
    A second pair of eyes first, which is where every official line on the record is drawn. The cyber agencies of five countries, America's among them, say AI agents should only be used for low-risk, non-sensitive tasks. Gartner recommends that anything customer-facing or holding customer data stays with engineers. That's where the bank's filing came from: names, Social Security numbers and dates of birth handled with an unauthorized AI tool, though the bank was using a tool, not building one.
    What it costs
    Review slows people down, and what review prevents isn't measured, on either side; the team arguing for checks conceded it on air. And the alarming numbers aren't about apps like hers: they measure lab tests of raw models, one agent on developers' projects, and staff using unapproved AI.
    Not measured
    What a check before go-live actually prevents: nobody has measured it.

    On the record: row C33 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row T3-28 of the fact-check log, row A8 of the fact-check log, row A10 of the fact-check log, row B31 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pick: If the tool leaks, the duty sits with the company that holds the data, whoever built it. And the judge left a second dial: who looks after it when you leave? On the platforms' own pages, the company can be left unable to fix a tool its builder owned alone, and the companies that govern this give every tool that matters an owner who stays. Before you build, three checks: does anyone else know it exists, what does it touch, and who looks after it when you leave?

What our fact-check caught

28 of the 367 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidPeople build their own apps mainly because IT is too slow.

    The record saysIn the survey we checked, answered mostly by IT managers, IT missing a department's timeline came last, at 9%. The top reason was that the tools were easier to get. The UK's cyber agency says going around IT is usually people struggling with the tools they've been given.

  2. Draft saidStudies show the apps business teams build with AI are insecure.

    The record saysThe alarming numbers measure lab tests of raw AI models, one agent on developers' projects, and staff using unapproved AI. None of them measures apps that business teams actually shipped.

  3. Draft saidAn app always dies with the person who built it.

    The record saysIt depends where it was built. In a company workspace on Lovable, a leaver's projects pass to the most senior member left, unless a project is marked restricted. Deleting a personal Replit account removes every app on it.

  4. Draft saidUS bank rules require banks to check the tools their staff build with AI.

    The record saysThis spring US bank regulators replaced their guidance on banks' models. The new version leaves spreadsheet arithmetic and generative AI outside its scope, for each bank to govern itself.

  5. Draft saidIT can see every app staff use.

    The record saysMicrosoft's discovery tool matches network traffic against a catalogue of known cloud services, and by default can't find apps that aren't in it. Seeing traffic to a platform doesn't show which app someone built there, or what's in it.
