Free Trade vs Protectionism: Is It Worth the Cost to Local Towns?

Is free trade worth its concentrated cost to local communities?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

Aggregate gains, concentrated losses — and a transfer nobody made

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out at the end of August 2026, a 50% US duty on about $20 billion of Canadian goods had just taken effect, under a 1930 law never before used to impose a tariff. Canada answered with its own tariffs and put C$7.5 billion behind the workers and firms hit.

Settled

What both sides accept

Both teams accepted the same totals: about two million jobs lost, about 2.6 million gained, netting near zero nationally. Green conceded that the compensation its case depends on was never funded. Gold conceded that the count of harmed places swings five-fold on one assumption.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesWorth It

About two million US jobs were lost to Chinese imports, but counting exports about 2.6 million were gained, so the national total nets near zero. Manufacturing jobs peaked in 1979, long before NAFTA or China joined the WTO. Tariffs cost about $800,000 per washing-machine job. The answer to an unpaid transfer is to pay it.

Gold arguesNot At This Price

A national total hides where the losses landed. On every published assumption some places end up worse off, from about a third of the country down to one in sixteen. Laid-off workers neither recovered their earnings nor moved, and disability payments rose more than 30 times as much as trade aid.

What the shouting gets wrong

The towns came back, and the people who lost the jobs did not, so each side can quote a true recovery number. Free trade's case says the winners gain enough to compensate the losers, and in the US that payment was never made: the trade aid programme peaked at about $700 million a year and was switched off in 2022. The question handed to you is whether you'd pay what would make it worth it to the people who paid for it.

Lena This one doesn't resolve, and I want to be exact about where it stops rather than call it a draw and move on.

Lena Both teams accepted the same aggregate. About two million jobs lost, about two point six million gained, netting near zero nationally. Neither debater disputed it in either direction.

Lena So the disagreement isn't about what happened to the country. It's about whether a national total is an answer to a question asked about a place — and that is a values question wearing an evidence question's clothes.

Lena Both sides also conceded their weak flank. Green conceded the compensation that makes her case work was never funded. Gold conceded the aggregate nets to roughly zero and that the count of harmed places swings five-fold on one assumption.

Lena The case that it was worth it rests on a ledger that nets to zero, on a decline that began in nineteen seventy-nine, and on a remedy measured at about eight hundred thousand dollars per job.

Lena The case against rests on a loss that never reaches zero on any published assumption, on adjustment that outlasts a career, and on sixty percent replacement after twenty-three years being the good outcome.

Lena And the thing both cases are downstream of is a payment nobody made. The compensation principle is a theorem about a transfer, not a promise that it happens.

Lena Eight days ago one country made that transfer and another one didn't. So the question we're handing you isn't whether trade was worth it. It's whether you'd pay what would make it worth it to the people who paid for it. That one's yours.

What our fact-check caught

17 of the 32 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidChinese imports explain about a quarter of the decline in US manufacturing jobs.

    The record saysA quarter is the loose wording in the paper's summary. Its own main result says 44% over the same years, and careful estimates run from 16% to 55%.

  2. Draft saidThe US trade aid programme for laid-off workers peaked at about $600 million a year.

    The record saysIt peaked at about $710 million a year, in 2015. It was shut down in July 2022.

  3. Draft saidThe 2018 tariffs cost Americans $1.4 billion a month.

    The record saysThat was only the economic waste. The total cost to consumers and importers reached about $4.7 billion a month by December 2018.

  4. Draft saidBritain's coalfields never recovered.

    The record saysAbout 60% of the male coal jobs lost in England and Wales were replaced after 23 years, ranging from none in some areas to all of them in others.
