The research · Same shock, opposite answers

WaltEight days ago a fifty percent duty took effect on about twenty billion dollars of Canadian goods. Cars, dairy, alcohol.

ColeFifty percent is steep. What's the legal basis?

WaltSection three thirty-eight of the Tariff Act of nineteen thirty. It had sat on the books for ninety-six years without ever once being used to impose a tariff.

ColeNever used. So why now?

WaltBecause it needs almost nothing. Other tariff powers require an investigation or a national-security finding. This one lets a president act by proclamation alone.

ColeAnd Canada answered.

WaltThree days later, dollar for dollar — about twenty-eight billion Canadian on American goods, from the eighth of September.

ColeThat's the part I'd expect. Tariffs, then counter-tariffs.

WaltIt's the other half of their announcement that matters here. Alongside the retaliation, Canada put seven and a half billion dollars behind the workers and firms the shock lands on.

ColeBehind the workers. Not the companies?

WaltBoth, but the largest single piece — three and a half billion — is income support, retention and retraining. And the release says it builds on nearly twenty-five billion already spent since the tariffs started.

The research · The ledger nets to roughly zero

WaltThat's where we're going. But start with what genuinely isn't in dispute, because it's more than the shouting suggests.

WaltChinese import competition destroyed a large number of American jobs, and the number is not small.

ColeHow large?

WaltAbout two million, economy-wide, between nineteen ninety-nine and twenty eleven. That's Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Price. Just under a million of those were in manufacturing itself.

ColeAnd nobody disputes that?

WaltNobody serious. What's disputed is what you put next to it. Count the export side too, and Feenstra, Ma and Xu find gains of about two point six million.

ColeSo it nets to roughly zero.

WaltNationally, roughly zero. A small plus one way of measuring it, a small minus the other. Both are real, and both are on screen.

ColeBut the losses and the gains didn't land in the same towns.

WaltThey did not. And the second settled thing is that American manufacturing employment peaked long before any of this. The absolute peak is June nineteen seventy-nine.

ColeBefore NAFTA. Before China joined the World Trade Organization.

WaltFourteen years before one and twenty-two years before the other. Anyone dating the decline to a trade deal is starting the clock late.

The research · The places came back, the people didn't

ColeSo if the national ledger is roughly zero, where's the argument?

WaltIn the places. And this is the finding that reorganised the whole file for me. The towns recovered. Employment levels in trade-exposed places more than fully rebound after twenty ten.

ColeThat sounds like the story ending well.

WaltIt's the employment-to-population ratio that doesn't come back. The jobs returned. The share of working-age people holding one didn't.

ColeHow does that happen?

WaltThe recovery is compositional. New, younger cohorts move in and fill lower-paid healthcare, education, retail and hospitality work. The people who lost the manufacturing jobs neither recover their earnings nor move away. They age in place.

ColeSo the place healed and the population changed underneath it.

WaltWhich is why the follow-up paper by Autor, Dorn and Hanson is titled Places versus People. And it's the reason both sides can quote a true recovery statistic at each other forever.

ColeThen what happened to the compensation? There's supposed to be compensation.

WaltThat's the third settled thing, and it's the one I'd put in front of anyone who argues about this. In trade-exposed regions, the per-person rise in disability payments was more than thirty times the rise in trade adjustment assistance.

ColeThirty times. So the safety net that caught people wasn't the trade one.

WaltIt was disability. And the trade programme was terminated in twenty twenty-two. Meanwhile about a hundred billion dollars in tariff refunds has been certified — to importers — and it's clearing in months.

ColeRefunds to importers in months, and the workers' programme is four years dead. I want to be careful here, but that is a choice somebody made.

The research · Every headline number is a band

WaltLast of the settled ground, and it's the part that decides how big you think this fight is.

ColeGo on.

WaltAsk how much of the manufacturing collapse trade actually caused, and the honest answer is a range, not a number. Sixteen percent at the low end. Fifty-five at the high end.

ColeThat's not a range, that's two different arguments.

WaltIt is. And I want to flag something, because we got this wrong ourselves in an earlier draft. The most-quoted figure in this entire debate is one quarter, and it comes from the abstract of the Autor, Dorn and Hanson paper.

ColeAnd the abstract's wrong?

WaltThe abstract says one quarter. The paper's own main specification says forty-four percent over the same window. Anyone citing one quarter as the headline number is repeating the paper's loose wording, and we did it too until we read the table.

ColeWhat's at the other end?

WaltJust over two hundred commuting zones on the standard assumption — about a third of the country. Eighty-two if you use the highest published estimate of those gains — about one in sixteen. Same calculation, five times the answer.

The reframe

LucasI went looking for a fight about whether trade was worth it, and I came back with a fight about a payment nobody ever made.

LucasHere's what I mean. The case for free trade was never that nobody gets hurt. It's that the winners gain enough to compensate the losers and still come out ahead.

LucasThat's a real result. But it's a theorem about a transfer. It never claimed the transfer actually happens. And in the United States, it didn't. About seven hundred million a year at its peak, against a shock that moved disability payments thirty times more.

LucasSo when somebody tells you trade was worth it, or that it wasn't, ask them the same question: worth it to whom, and measured where?

LucasAnd the thing neither side can wriggle out of is that this stopped being hypothetical eight days ago. One country attached a worker package to its tariffs. The other refunded the importers and left the workers' programme switched off.

LucasGreen argues it was worth it. Gold argues not at this price. Green goes first.

The debate · Team Green · WORTH IT

ErinI want to argue this in ledgers, because the whole case rests on totals and I'd rather show my arithmetic than assert a conclusion.

ErinStart with the number my opponent needs as much as I do. About two million jobs lost to Chinese import competition. I accept it. It's the best-identified estimate we have.

ErinNow finish the ledger. The same literature, counting exports, finds about two point six million jobs gained. Nationally that nets to roughly zero — a small plus one way, a small minus the other, depending on the unit. Both numbers are on screen.

ErinSecond: the decline started long before the policy that gets blamed. Manufacturing employment peaked in June nineteen seventy-nine — fourteen years before NAFTA, twenty-two before China joined the World Trade Organization.

ErinThird, and this is the one I'd ask you to sit with: even the largest careful estimate of trade's share of the manufacturing decline leaves most of it unexplained. Sixteen percent at the low end. Even at the high end, most of that collapse is not trade.

ErinFourth, the alternative has a price and we have measured it. The washing-machine tariffs cost about eight hundred thousand dollars per job created.

ErinAnd the twenty eighteen tariffs passed through completely into American prices. Not partly. Completely — nearly five billion dollars a month by that December.

ErinSo the remedy on offer costs consumers more per job than the job pays, several times over, and it does not bring the factories back.

ErinNow the concession, and it's a real one. The compensation that makes my case work was never funded. About seven hundred million a year at peak, terminated in twenty twenty-two.

ErinMy opponent will say that a national net of zero is no comfort to the town that lost its plant. He's right, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

ErinBut the answer to a transfer that was never made is to make it. Not to tax the imports and hand the refund to the importer.

The debate · Team Gold · NOT AT THIS PRICE

RajLet me grant the aggregate first, because I think she's right about it and I don't want to spend the segment pretending otherwise.

RajNationally, the employment ledger is close to zero. I accept that. My argument is that a national net is an accounting answer to a geographic question.

RajHere's the measured version. Take the calculation of how many American commuting zones ended up absolutely worse off — net, after the consumer gains everybody on her side quotes.

RajOn the standard estimate of those gains, it's just over two hundred commuting zones. About a third of the population of the United States.

RajAnd I'll say the honest part myself, because she'd say it otherwise. Use the most generous published estimate of the gains and that falls to eighty-two zones — one in sixteen. Five times smaller.

RajBut notice what does not happen at any value. It never goes to zero. On every assumption anyone has published, millions of people end up net worse off, and we know which places they're in.

RajSecond: adjustment didn't happen on any timescale a career survives. The effects are still measurable in twenty nineteen. Incumbent workers neither recovered their earnings nor moved. They aged in place.

RajAnd the comparison that ought to end the optimism is Britain's coalfields. About sixty percent of lost male jobs replaced — after twenty-three years. That's the good outcome.

RajThird, the compensation. She conceded it, so I'll just put the size of it on the record. More than thirty times as much per person in disability payments as in trade adjustment assistance.

RajThat's not a safety net catching people. That's people ending up on the only programme that was still open.

RajI'm not arguing the aggregate is wrong. I'm arguing that a policy whose costs are this concentrated and this permanent needs consent from the people paying them, and it never got it.

The debate · Team Green responds

ErinThe concentration point is his best card. Let me say precisely what it does and doesn't buy.

ErinIt establishes that the costs landed in specific places and stayed there. Agreed. It does not establish that the tariff is the answer, and that's the policy actually on the table.

ErinEight hundred and fifteen thousand dollars per washing-machine job, and complete pass-through into American prices. Those are measurements of the remedy, not forecasts about it.

ErinAnd I'd point at the same week he would. Canada answered tariffs with tariffs — and with seven and a half billion dollars aimed at the workers. Those are two different policies, and only one of them is aimed at his people.

ErinHe's right that I have to say what I'd do instead. So: fund the transfer. My case is strongest on the aggregate and weakest on the distribution, and I'd rather concede that than overclaim it.

The verdict

LenaThis one doesn't resolve, and I want to be exact about where it stops rather than call it a draw and move on.

LenaBoth teams accepted the same aggregate. About two million jobs lost, about two point six million gained, netting near zero nationally. Neither debater disputed it in either direction.

LenaSo the disagreement isn't about what happened to the country. It's about whether a national total is an answer to a question asked about a place — and that is a values question wearing an evidence question's clothes.

LenaBoth sides also conceded their weak flank. Green conceded the compensation that makes her case work was never funded. Gold conceded the aggregate nets to roughly zero and that the count of harmed places swings five-fold on one assumption.

LenaThe case that it was worth it rests on a ledger that nets to zero, on a decline that began in nineteen seventy-nine, and on a remedy measured at about eight hundred thousand dollars per job.

LenaThe case against rests on a loss that never reaches zero on any published assumption, on adjustment that outlasts a career, and on sixty percent replacement after twenty-three years being the good outcome.

LenaAnd the thing both cases are downstream of is a payment nobody made. The compensation principle is a theorem about a transfer, not a promise that it happens.

LenaEight days ago one country made that transfer and another one didn't. So the question we're handing you isn't whether trade was worth it. It's whether you'd pay what would make it worth it to the people who paid for it. That one's yours.
