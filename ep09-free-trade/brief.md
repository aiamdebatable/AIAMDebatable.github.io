# Research brief — Is Free Trade Worth the Cost to Local Communities?

_The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video._

> **Topic-shape verdict (guardrail, logged before either side was written):** ✅ **Genuinely two-sided.**
> Aggregate gains against concentrated local losses is a real empirical fight with credible
> peer-reviewed work on both sides. No manufactured balance is needed. What verification changed is
> *which* fight — the direction of the aggregate is closer to resolved than either camp admits, and
> the live question is distributional.

## The question

**Is free trade worth the cost to local communities?**

Live because the argument is being settled with policy while this was written. In the week of
22 August 2026 a 50% duty landed on US$20bn of Canadian goods under a 1930 statute never used before,
Canada answered dollar-for-dollar and attached a C$7.5bn package for the workers hit, and the USMCA —
which the US declined to renew on 1 July — went to annual review. Behind it sits a bipartisan turn
against the consensus that held from 1945 to roughly 2016: both parties now claim the pro-worker
position and propose opposite remedies. See § Why now.

## Why now

### The week this brief was written, the argument stopped being about 1999

**22 August 2026: a 50% US duty took effect on roughly US$20bn of Canadian goods** — motor vehicles,
dairy and alcoholic beverages, plus electronics, furniture, building materials, plastics, clothing and
agricultural products across 500+ categories. Three proclamations, signed 20 July, published in the
Federal Register 23 July under the heading *"Imposing Additional Duties To Offset Canadian
Discrimination Against the Commerce of the United States."*

⛔ **The authority is the story.** They were issued under **Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930** —
a provision that had sat on the books for **96 years without ever being used to impose a tariff**.
Unlike Section 232 (requires a national-security finding) or Section 301 (requires a USTR
investigation), Section 338 lets a president act **by proclamation alone**: no investigation, no
hearing. ⚠ These duties apply **even to CUSMA-compliant goods** and carry **no expiry date** — unlike
the Section 301 forced-labor duties of 24 July, which exempt CUSMA-compliant Canadian goods. The
agreement the US declined to renew is currently the thing shielding Canadian exporters from one of the
four tariff regimes, and not from the other.

| date | what happened |
|---|---|
| **2026-07-20** | Three Section 338 proclamations signed — **11046, 11047, 11048** (motor vehicles · dairy · alcoholic beverages); published FR 07-23 as `2026-14997`, `2026-14992`, `2026-14991`; effective 08-19 |
| **2026-08-18** | ⓘ **Proclamation 11056 postpones them 3 days**, to 08-22 — stated reason: *"Canada has expressed a commitment to remove the discriminations"* and talks are ongoing. Three days later they were not |

| **2026-08-25** | Canada answers, matching *"dollar for dollar, rate for rate"*: **counter-tariffs of 15, 25 and 50% covering C$27.6bn of US imports**, effective **8 September**, on steel and aluminium, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper and electronics. ⚠ The widely-reported "700+ products" is **not** in the government's release — it states the value, not a count |
| **2026-08-28** | ⓘ **25 states, led by Oregon, Arizona and California, sue at the Court of International Trade** — challenging the July Section 301 forced-labor duties as an Administrative Procedure Act violation, alleging USTR's investigation was rushed. ⚠ Reported, not yet read against the filing |

Canada's stated reason, from the Prime Minister's own remarks of 22 August: the US proposed terms that
were *"uneconomic, unfair, and undermined the net benefits to Canada"* — *"they asked too much and
offered too little."*

★ **AND THE PRIMARY SOURCE CARRIES A FIGURE NO SECONDARY REPORTED**, which makes the comparison below

The transfer is not C$7.5bn. **It is roughly C$32.5bn and counting.**

⛔ **Set that against the US side of the same border.** Trade Adjustment Assistance — the programme
built for exactly this — peaked at **$710.6M in FY2015**, entered phased termination on **1 July 2022**,
and has no successor enacted. Meanwhile roughly **$100bn** in IEEPA tariff refunds has been certified to
*importers* through July 2026 and is clearing in months.

★ **The orders of magnitude are the argument.** Canada, one tenth the population: **~C$32.5bn** to
workers and firms across eighteen months. The United States: a **$710.6M** peak for the programme
meant to do it, dead for four years, against ~$100bn refunded to importers in the same period.

✓ **Currency, resolved 2026-08-30:** the Canadian figures appear both ways because they are one number
in two currencies — **C$27.6bn ≈ US$19.9bn**, **C$7.5bn ≈ US$5.4bn**. Label the currency; never convert
silently.

★ **That is this brief's central claim, happening in public, on both sides of one border at once.**
The compensation principle is a theorem about a transfer that was never made. One country made it this
week. The other refunded the firms and left the workers' programme lapsed for four years. Neither
outcome settles which side is right — it settles that the transfer is a **choice**, not an
impossibility, which is precisely what both camps' remedies assume away.

### The USMCA itself is up, and it is the same fight

**On 1 July 2026 the United States declined to renew the USMCA.** Under Article 34.7 the three parties
had to say, at the agreement's sixth anniversary, whether to extend for another sixteen years. Canada
and Mexico each confirmed. The US did not — *"The United States did not agree to renew the USMCA in
its current form. As a result, the USMCA is not renewed."* (Ambassador Greer, 1 July 2026).

Nothing expired. The agreement stays in force but now faces a **joint review every single year** until
the parties agree or it dies on **1 July 2036**. The headline negotiating issue is **automotive rules
of origin**: how much of a car has to be built in North America, and where.

★ That is this episode's question in procurement language, asked by governments instead of economists.
NAFTA and its successor are the single most-blamed cause of American manufacturing-town decline, and
"rules of origin" is a technical name for an argument about which towns hold the jobs.

## Settled (not the debate)

- **Chinese import competition destroyed roughly 2 million American jobs.** **Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn,
  Hanson & Price** put it at **985,000 in manufacturing and 1.98 million economy-wide (1999–2011)** at
  national-industry level, and **2.37 million** at commuting-zone level. ✅ **Re-verified against the
  paper itself, 2026-08-30:** the abstract reads *"net job losses of 2.0 to 2.4 million stemming from
  the rise in import competition from China over the period 1999 to 2011,"* and the paper's own summary
  table reproduces every sub-figure (Table 6B: −985k manufacturing, −994k non-manufacturing, −1,979k
  total; Table 7 CZ-level: −2,367k). ⚠ Two different methods, **not a range** — and both are net of
  *reallocation* but gross of *export gains*, which is why Feenstra can add exports and reach ≈ zero.
- ⛔ **A substantial number of places lost in ABSOLUTE terms — and the count swings by 5× on one
  assumption.** ADH's persistence paper computes how many commuting zones suffered net absolute welfare
  losses, and it depends entirely on how large you believe the consumer gains from trade were:

  | assumed aggregate gain from China trade | CZs with a NET absolute loss | share of US population |
  |---|---|---|
  | **0.22%** — Caliendo et al. / Galle et al., standard quantitative models | **223** | **32.8%** |
  | **0.44%** — double that | **173** | **15.9%** |
  | **1.25%** — Jaravel–Sager, "over five times" the model-based gains | **82** | **6.3%** |

  ★ **All three are net figures from the same calculation.** This is the episode's reframe in one
  table: the answer to "how many Americans were made absolutely worse off" runs from a third of the
  country to one in sixteen, purely on a parameter choice — and each camp quotes the end that suits it.
  ⚠ Do not present 32.8% or 6.3% alone; either one alone is a half-quotation.
- **Counting export gains, the national net is roughly zero.** Feenstra, Ma & Xu replicate a ~2.0M
  commuting-zone loss, then add ~2.64M export-driven gains: **net +379k at industry level, −68k at
  commuting-zone level**. ⚠ Both figures are real. The losses and the gains landed in *different towns*.
- ⏭ **"Trade's consumer gains are pro-poor" MOVED OUT of settled on 2026-08-30.** The 63%/28% figure
  is a calibrated-model result and direct measurement contradicts it. See § Contested #2.
- **The places recovered. The people did not.** Employment levels in trade-exposed places more than
  fully rebound after 2010, while employment-to-population ratios stay depressed and manufacturing
  keeps atrophying. The recovery is compositional: new cohorts fill low-wage healthcare, education,
  retail and hospitality jobs; incumbents neither recover earnings nor migrate — they age in place.
- **Compensation was never delivered.** In trade-exposed regions the per-capita rise in **SSDI
  payments was more than thirty times** the rise in Trade Adjustment Assistance. TAA peaked at
  $710.6M (FY2015) and was terminated on 1 July 2022.
- **Tariffs were paid by Americans.** Complete pass-through into domestic prices in 2018; ~$4.7bn/month
  total cost to consumers and importers by December 2018; $51bn/yr consumer and importer loss.
- **US manufacturing employment peaked before either NAFTA or China.** Absolute peak **June 1979
  (19,553,000)**; postwar share peak **May 1953 (32.4%)**.

## Contested (the real fight)

### Contested #1 — how much of the manufacturing collapse trade actually caused

**The range is wide enough to change the argument, and it is not the single number this brief used to
give.** ⛔ An earlier version read *"~21% … (the abstract's 'one quarter'; the body is more
conservative)"* — the body is **less** conservative, not more:

| source | share of the manufacturing employment decline attributed to Chinese import competition |
|---|---|
| ADH 2013, main specification (Table 3, col. 6) | **33%** (1990–2000) · **55%** (2000–2007) · **44%** (1990–2007) |
| ADH 2013, further supply-shock-only adjustment | **~21%** |
| ADH 2013 abstract | *"one-quarter … in our main specification"* — ⚠ does **not** match the main specification |
| Caliendo, Dvorkin & Parro, *Econometrica* 2019 | **16%** — see below, and it is now primary-verified |

⛔ **Anyone citing "one-quarter" as the main-specification figure is repeating the paper's own loose
wording**, and this brief did it too until 2026-08-30. The honest statement of the fight is
**16% to 55%**, depending on specification, window and whether you accept a general-equilibrium model.

✅ **CDP primary-verified 2026-08-30**, full text of the published *Econometrica* 87(3) article.
Abstract, verbatim: *"We find that the China trade shock resulted in a reduction of about **0.55 million**
U.S. manufacturing jobs, about **16%** of the observed decline in manufacturing employment from 2000 to
2007."* Footnote 7 gives the observed decline as **3.4 million**.

★ **And the same result is also 36%.** Footnote 8: the 0.55 million is *"about 36% of the change in the
aggregate manufacturing employment share unexplained by a secular trend"* — their linear 1967–1999 trend
predicts an 12.83% manufacturing share for 2007 against an observed 11.85%. **One number, two
denominators, 16% or 36%.** That is this episode's reframe sitting inside a single footnote.

⛔ **A trap on the way to this number.** Secondary summaries report CDP as "0.8 million, about 25%" —
that is an earlier working-paper vintage, and worse, *"about 25 percent"* appears in the published paper
meaning something else entirely: the **computer and electronics industry's** share of the manufacturing
decline. Cite 0.55M / 16% from the published article.

### Contested #2 — whether the consumer gains are actually pro-poor

**This was in § Settled until 2026-08-30 and should not have been.** It is a fight between a
calibrated model and direct measurement:

- **Fajgelbaum & Khandelwal** (*QJE* 2016) model shutting down all trade across 40 countries and find
  real-income losses of **63% at the 10th percentile against 28% at the 90th** — gains ~2.25× larger
  for the poor. ⚠ A 40-country average, not a US-specific figure, and the counterfactual is total
  autarky rather than any real tariff.
- ⛔ **Borusyak & Jaravel**, *The Distributional Effects of Trade* (NBER w28957, June 2021), use linked
  expenditure–customs microdata instead of a calibrated model. ✅ **Primary-verified 2026-08-30**;
  abstract, verbatim: *"Contrary to common wisdom, we find that import shares are flat throughout the
  income distribution: the purchasing-power gains from lower trade costs are distributionally neutral."*
  The measured spread is tiny — a **12.6%** national average import share against **11.7%** for
  households earning under $10k a year.

★ **This decides whether "trade helps the poor through cheap goods" is a real rebuttal to the
local-labour-market case or a modelling artifact** — and it is the load-bearing plank under Side A.

⚠ **But do not overstate them either, and the paper is explicit about it.** Borusyak & Jaravel do *not*
find that trade has no distributional effect. They find *"substantial distributional effects that arise
within, but not across, income and education groups"* — effects that are **"horizontal" (within income
deciles) rather than "vertical" (across deciles)**, with *"winners and losers at all income levels"* via
wages. ★ That is a sharper version of this episode's spine than the pro-poor fight itself: the losses are
concentrated, just not along the income gradient either camp argues about.

---

**Side A — "Worth it":** The gains are real, large, and — *on the modelled estimate, which Contested
#2 shows is disputed* — land hardest on the poor as consumers, 63% of real income at the bottom decile. The national employment ledger nets to roughly zero once exports
are counted. Manufacturing employment was falling for decades before NAFTA or China; the causal
automation evidence is independent and robust. The failure was never trade — it was that the
compensation the theory *assumed* was never funded: a $710M program against a shock that moved SSDI
thirty times more, terminated in 2022. Tariffs don't fix that; they cost $815,000 per washing-machine
job and produced measurably *fewer* manufacturing jobs through input costs and retaliation.

**Side B — "Not at this price":** "Net zero nationally" is an accounting answer to a geographic
question. Two million jobs died in specific towns and the offsetting gains appeared somewhere else,
which is no comfort to the place that lost them. Adjustment did not happen on any timescale a human
career survives: effects still measurable in 2019, incumbents aging in place, earnings losses
concentrated on exactly the low-wage, low-tenure workers least able to absorb them. Britain's
coalfields replaced about **60% of lost male jobs after 23 years**, ranging from 0% in some areas to
100% in others — and that is the *optimistic* comparison. A policy whose costs are this concentrated
and this permanent needs consent from the people paying them, and never got it.

## The reframe

**The question everyone argues — "did the towns recover?" — has been answered, and the answer is
"the towns did; the people didn't."** ADH's 2025 follow-up is literally titled *Places versus People*.
Employment levels came back; the employment-to-population ratio did not. The place refilled with
different, younger, more diverse workers moving into lower-paid service jobs, while the incumbent
manufacturing workers neither recovered nor left. Both camps have been arguing about a recovery
statistic without noticing they meant different nouns.

**And the buried point:** the compensation principle is a *theorem about a transfer that was never
made*. Trade theory proved the winners' gains are large enough to compensate the losers. It never
claimed compensation happens. Everything both sides argue about is downstream of a payment nobody made.

## Both sides quietly agree

**Neither camp's remedy survives its own evidence.** The free-trade side's answer was adjustment
assistance — and the government's own evaluation found participants' fourth-year earnings were
"either $3,300 less than, or about the same as" non-participants. Never positive. The protectionist
side's answer was tariffs — and they cost $815,000 per job on washing machines, with the gap between
consumer cost and Treasury revenue flowing to producer margins at largely foreign-owned plants.

Neither lobby is funded to argue for the transfer, because there is no margin in it. That is why a
$710M program died quietly in 2022 while a multi-billion-dollar steel tariff has a permanent
constituency.

★ **And the "it can't be done" defence broke this week.** On 25 August 2026 Canada answered the US
tariffs with counter-tariffs *and* a **C$7.5bn package aimed at the workers** — C$3.5bn of EI
extensions, retention and retraining, plus C$4bn of regional, diversification and liquidity support
(§ Why now). On the same border in the same week, the US programme built for that purpose has been
lapsed since 1 July 2022 while roughly $100bn of tariff refunds clears to *importers*.

⛔ This does not make Canada right, and the episode must not use it that way. A package announced is
not a package delivered, and C$7.5bn against a shock this size may prove as inadequate as TAA was.
What it removes is the **assumption both camps share**: that the compensating transfer is
hypothetical. It is a choice, it was available, and two governments facing the same shock in the same
week made opposite ones. That is the fight the episode hands to the viewer.

## Verdict framing

No winner. Both core claims survive: trade delivered real, pro-poor aggregate gains, *and* the local
losses were larger, more concentrated and far more permanent than the models promised. What is handed
to the viewer is the question that actually decides the policy — **not whether trade was worth it in
aggregate, but whether a country is willing to pay the transfer that would make it worth it to the
people who paid for it**, since neither tariffs nor free trade has ever delivered that — and since, as of the week this brief
was written, one country on this continent is trying to and the other is not.

The viewer's tool, shared with ep07 and ep08: **ask what is being counted, and where.** "Two million
jobs lost" and "net zero" are the same economy measured two ways.

## Reader's caution — what this brief will not say, and why

Checked and **failed**:

- **"Flaaen & Pierce found the steel tariffs cost 75,000 manufacturing jobs"** — that figure is in
  neither version of their paper. It originates with Cox & Russ at EconoFact, who computed it.
- **"87% of manufacturing job losses were caused by automation, 13% by trade"** — the split is real

First pass **2026-08-19**; re-checked **2026-08-27**. Both items below moved, and one of them moved

### 1. "The Supreme Court struck down the tariffs" is true, and said alone it is misleading

The first pass recorded the 6–3 ruling of 20 February 2026 and collection halting on 24 February. What
it did not record is that a replacement took effect **the same day**, and a second replacement took
effect the minute the first one expired — see § Why now for the sequence. An episode that says the
tariffs were struck down and stops there hands the viewer a picture of the present that is simply
false: the effective tariff rate in June 2026 (**7.1%**) is more than triple January 2025 (**2.3%**),
and Section 232 — steel, aluminium, copper, autos, parts — was never in the case at all.

⛔ The legal position is still moving. The Federal Circuit's stay of the CIT's Section 122 ruling was
administrative; the Section 122 surcharge has since lapsed on its own terms; the Section 301
forced-labor action taking its place is weeks old and untested. **Re-check the legal status, not only
the numbers.**

### 2. The manufacturing recovery is real, but it is NOT a five-month streak

The first pass wrote *"risen for five consecutive months (Feb → Jul 2026, +28,000)"*. On the BLS series
as it stands today the **+28,000 is right and the streak is wrong** — April and May both fell:

| | Dec 25 | Jan 26 | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul (p) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **CES3000000001** (thousands) | 12,580 | 12,582 | 12,583 | 12,598 | **12,597** | **12,595** | 12,606 | 12,611 |

⚠️ July is **preliminary** and June was revised **up** from +3,000 to +11,000 after first print. This
series is revised twice. **Do not put a monthly figure on air** — the cumulative level is the only safe
spoken claim, and the month-by-month belongs on screen if anywhere.

What survives the re-check:

- Manufacturing employment bottomed at **12,580,000 in December 2025** and is **+31,000** since.
- The circulated **"89,000 jobs lost under the tariffs"** (Apr 2025 → Feb 2026) is **−79,000** on
  revised data — confirmed again on 2026-08-27.
- Tariff-era decline (Apr 2025 → Jul 2026) runs at roughly **−3,400/month** against roughly
  **−9,100/month** over the pre-tariff slide (Jan 2023 peak → Mar 2025) — about a third the rate.

⛔ **That last line is arithmetic, not causation**, and the episode must not let either side spend it
as if it were. It compares two windows of a series that has been falling since June 1979, over a
period that also contains a pandemic recovery, a capex cycle and two recessions' worth of revisions.
It belongs in the brief as a caution against the stale "89,000" number, not in either team's mouth.
