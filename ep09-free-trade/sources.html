The China shock — how big, and net of what

  • Import competition explains ~21% of the manufacturing employment decline 1990–2007 (the abstract says "one quarter"; the body gives 16% for 1990–2000, 26% for 2000–07, 21% overall). Net reduction 548,000 (1990–2000) + 982,000 (2000–07) = 1.53M — Autor, Dorn & Hanson, "The China Syndrome," AER 103(6):2121–68: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.103.6.2121
  • 1.98 million economy-wide (985,000 in manufacturing, 994,000 non-manufacturing) at national-industry level, and 2.37 million at commuting-zone level, 1999–2011 — ⚠ two different methods, not a range; net of reallocation, gross with respect to exports. ✅ Primary source re-verified 2026-08-30 by full-text extraction — the estimate originates with Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson & Price, JOLE 34(S1) / NBER w20395, not with the ADH Annual Review restatement this brief previously cited. Abstract, verbatim: "Our central estimates suggest net job losses of 2.0 to 2.4 million stemming from the rise in import competition from China over the period 1999 to 2011." Sub-figures in the paper's own summary table (Table 6B, cols 6/9 and Table 7, cols 6/9); direct effect alone is −560k for 1999–2011: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20395/w20395.pdf
  • CZs suffering NET absolute welfare losses — a three-point sensitivity ladder, all net, from ADH On the Persistence of the China Shock, NBER w29401. Verbatim: "the implied number of CZs experiencing net losses from trade is 223, representing 32.8% of the U.S. continental population in 2000. Even if we double these gains to 0.44%, 173 commuting zones, representing 15.9% of the U.S. population, would be estimated to have suffered net absolute welfare losses." And, applying the much larger Jaravel–Sager price effect: "there are 82 CZs, representing 6.3% of U.S. population, with declines in personal income per capita of 1.25% or more." Out of 722 continental CZs. ⛔ Both circulating versions are half-quotations: 32.8% is not a "gross" figure later corrected to 6.3% — they are the same calculation at two ends of an assumption range: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29401/w29401.pdf
  • ★ The same paper's own normative reading, useful because it is neither camp's: "Few economists would interpret our empirical results as justifying greater trade protection… quantitative models indicate that U.S. aggregate gains from trade with China are positive. Yet, the fact that the losses from trade are regionally concentrated and long lasting suggests that existing policies failed to insulate workers from the disruptive impacts of globalization." Also: standard quantitative models generate regional variation only one-quarter as large as the reduced-form estimates.
  • ADH 2013 attribution shares, verbatim (AER 103(6):2121): abstract — "In our main specification, import competition explains one-quarter of the contemporaneous aggregate decline in US manufacturing employment"; body — "rising exposure to Chinese import competition explains 33 percent of the US manufacturing employment decline between 1990 and 2000, 55 percent of the decline between 2000 and 2007, and 44 percent of the decline for the full 1990 through 2007 period." ⛔ The abstract's "one-quarter" does not match the main specification; ~21% follows only after a further supply-shock-only adjustment: https://www.ddorn.net/papers/Autor-Dorn-Hanson-ChinaSyndrome.pdf
  • Net +379k (industry level) and −68k (commuting-zone level), 1991–2011, after adding ~2,636,000 export-driven gains. ⚠ Feenstra's own commuting-zone import-destruction estimate is ~1,994,000 — it replicates ADH. The 1991–2011 import effect is −644k (the circulating "533k" is the 1999–2011 figure) — Feenstra, Ma & Xu, JIE 120 (2019):46–58: https://mahong.weebly.com/uploads/2/7/0/9/27093249/feenstra-ma-xu-2019jie.pdf
  • ⚠ Live critique: ADH's instrument correlates with non-China imports, so the effect may be globalization-wide — Kaestner, Econ Journal Watch 23(1), March 2026, 27–45. Published, but EJW is editor-reviewed commentary, not a refereed empirical journal: https://econjwatch.org/articles/globalization-and-the-china-shock-a-reassessment-of-autor-dorn-and-hanson

Places versus people — the episode's spine

  • Effects still measurable in 2019: −1.54pp manufacturing employment-to-population ratio (55% of observed change); 86% absorbed as a fall in the overall employment rate; population decline only among foreign-born and native-born aged 25–39. ⚠ Brookings Papers is a conference journal with named discussants (Bertrand, Glaeser), not blind peer review — ADH, "On the Persistence of the China Shock," NBER WP 29401 / BPEA Fall 2021: https://www.nber.org/papers/w29401
  • Employment levels more than fully rebound after 2010 while employment-to-population ratios stay depressed and manufacturing further atrophies; refilled by cohorts below working age at shock onset — disproportionately native-born Hispanics, immigrants, women, college-educated — into low-wage healthcare, education, retail and hospitality; incumbents "age in place" with both in- and out-migration depressed. Census LEHD linked records, 2000–2019. ⚠ Unrefereed NBER working paper — Autor, Dorn, Hanson, Jones & Setzler, "Places versus People," NBER WP 33424: https://www.nber.org/papers/w33424
  • ⚠⚠ WP 29401 and WP 33424 share the same Bartik/ADH instrument. Their identification stands or falls together — neither checks the other.
  • Earnings losses concentrate among low initial wage, low tenure, low labour-force-attachment workers, who churn within manufacturing into repeat shocks; high-wage workers move employers with minimal loss. The only fully refereed item in this group — Autor, Dorn, Hanson & Song, QJE 129(4), 2014:1799–1860: https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/129/4/1799/1854509

What trade gave, and to whom

  • Closing off trade costs 63% of real income at the 10th percentile vs 28% at the 90th. ⚠ A 40-country unweighted average, NOT a US figure (27 European + 13 other large economies, WIOD, 2005–2007); per-country results in Table V — Fajgelbaum & Khandelwal, QJE 131(3):1113–80: https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/131/3/1113/2461162
  • ⚠ NOT USED: the Broda & Romalis 6pp rich/poor inflation gap. The paper attributes only about a third of it to Chinese exports (~0.3pp/yr), so the popular framing overstates by ~3×, and the manuscript is still marked "Preliminary and Incomplete. Please do not circulate." (2008), never peer-reviewed: https://www.etsg.org/ETSG2008/Papers/Romalis.pdf

Automation vs trade

  • One additional robot per 1,000 workers reduces the employment-to-population ratio 0.2pp and wages 0.42%, holding up against controls for Chinese and Mexican import exposure, routine-job decline and offshoring. The one claim in this research that needed no correction — Acemoglu & Restrepo, "Robots and Jobs," JPE 128(6):2188–2244 (2020): https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/705716
  • ⚠ The "87% automation / 13% trade" split — Hicks & Devaraj, The Myth and the Reality of Manufacturing in America, Ball State CBER, June 2015, ~8-page policy brief, not peer-reviewed. Table 4, all manufacturing, 2000–2010 only, 5,647,700 losses: productivity 87.8%, trade 13.4%, domestic demand +1.2% (a gain; shares exceed 100% because demand offsets). It is an accounting identity, not a causal estimate. https://projects.cberdata.org/reports/MfgReality.pdf
  • Airable phrasing, verbatim: "A 2015 Ball State policy brief — not peer-reviewed — decomposed the 5.6 million manufacturing jobs lost from 2000 to 2010 and found about 88% of the drop lines up with rising output per worker and about 13% with trade. That's an accounting split, not a causal one, and the authors say so themselves."
  • Hicks to NPR (24 Oct 2019): "productivity" is not "automation" — it also captures education, process improvements and other technology: https://www.npr.org/2019/10/24/772798717/

Who paid the tariffs

  • Complete pass-through into domestic prices (duty-exclusive import prices did not fall; coefficient −0.012, s.e. 0.023). ⚠ $1.4bn/month is the deadweight loss; added tax costs were $3.2bn/month and total cost to consumers and importers reached $4.7bn/month by Dec 2018. ⚠ JEP articles are solicited, not peer-reviewed — Amiti, Redding & Weinstein, JEP 33(4), 2019:187–210: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.33.4.187
  • $51 billion consumer/importer loss (0.27% of GDP); $7.2 billion net (0.04%), annual at 2016 prices. ⚠ The $7.2bn is not statistically significant; the $51bn is — Fajgelbaum, Goldberg, Kennedy & Khandelwal, QJE 135(1), 2020:1–55: https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/135/1/1/5626442
  • Tariff exposure at the 25th→75th percentile: +0.4% protection, −2.0% input costs, −1.1% retaliation = net −2.7% employment, prices +3.3% (published REStat version). ⚠ The FEDS working paper gives net −1.4% and +4.1% prices — do not mix vintages — Flaaen & Pierce: https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2019086pap.pdf
  • ⛔ NOT Flaaen & Pierce: the "~75,000 jobs / 0.6%" figure is Cox & Russ, EconoFact (2020), who computed it from steel/aluminium input costs. Not peer-reviewed. It appears in neither version of the Fed paper.
  • Washing machines: "over 815,000 USD per job created," ~$1.5bn annual consumer cost, $82.2M revenue, 1,800 jobs — ⚠ those jobs are company-announced figures the authors adopt, and 1,600 of them are at foreign-owned Samsung and LG plants — Flaaen, Hortaçsu & Tintelnot, AER 110(7), 2020:2103–27: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20190611
  • Steel ~$650,000/job ($5.6bn consumer cost vs $2.4bn industry income, 8,700 jobs) — ⚠ **PIIE blog post, no peer review, ex ante model projections; PIIE's own 2025 update revises to $900,000/job**. No single figure stated on air — Hufbauer & Jung, PIIE, 20 Dec 2018.

Adjustment — the transfer that was never made

  • TAA participants' fourth-year earnings were "either $3,300 less than, or about the same as" comparison-group members — explicitly lower- and upper-bound estimates. Propensity-score matching against non-eligible UI claimants, 25 states, no random assignment; net benefits negative from every perspective — D'Amico & Schochet, DOL ETAOP 2013-08: https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/ETA/publications/ETAOP_2013_08.pdf
  • TAA peaked at $710.6M (FY2015), $685M in FY2009–10; phased termination 1 July 2022, no new certifications; FY2022 pre-sequestration appropriation $540M — CRS R47200 and R42012.
  • "For regions affected by Chinese imports, the estimated dollar increase in per capita SSDI payments is more than thirty times as large as the estimated dollar increase in TAA payments" — verbatim in ADH, AER 2013. Compares per-capita transfer increases across commuting zones. ✅ Re-verified by full-text extraction 2026-08-30, and the arithmetic checks out: per $1,000 of growth in a CZ's per-worker import exposure, TAA benefits rise $0.23 per capita and SSDI rises ~$8 — a ratio of ≈35×. For scale the paper adds that average TAA payments were "a mere $2 per capita, which is less than 0.05 percentage points of total transfers from governments to individuals."
  • Janesville: 1,740 Blackhawk Technical College enrollees vs ~28,000 local UI claimants who did not enroll; by mid-2011 about a third of enrollees employed year-round vs about half of non-enrollees, earnings −36% vs −8%. ⚠ Authors could not control for quality differences — selection bias unresolved: https://www.propublica.org/article/inside-the-janesville-data
  • Displaced-worker reemployment, January 2020 release (2017–19 displacements, USDL-20-1620): ~75% ages 25–54, 67% 55–64, 44% 65+; 17.1% of reemployed full-timers took a 20%+ pay cut. ⚠ The circulating "21%+" figure comes from a different year (Jan 2024: 24.9%, with ages 74.5/55.3/34.4). Pick one survey year and say which — https://www.bls.gov/news.release/disp.nr0.htm

Against it, BLS counts manufacturing employment down ~87,000 in 2024: https://reshorenow.org/june-9-2025/

The historical record

  • Corn Laws (1846): British welfare −0.1% to −0.3% Hicksian equivalent variation; landowners −3.1% to −3.9% (the circulating "3–5%" is working-paper only); bottom 90% +0.3–0.6%; top 10% −1.4%. Static CGE on an 1841 input-output table. ⚠ Their 4% land-rent fall contradicts Williamson (12%) and O'Rourke (9%) — Irwin & Chepeliev, Economic Journal 131(640):3322–3337 (2021): https://www.nber.org/papers/w28142
  • Smoot-Hawley (1930): real exports fell ~1.7% of 1929 GDP. ⚠ The rate comparison is contested (38.5%→41.1% per EH.net; Irwin reads 35.65%→41.14%), the "~21% of the GDP decline" is 1.7% × an assumed multiplier of 2 (an upper bound), and realized rates hit 59.1% by 1932 as deflation inflated specific duties — so 41.1% understates protection. Retaliation was real: exports to retaliating countries fell 28–33% (Mitchener, O'Rourke & Wandschneider, EJ 2022): https://eh.net/encyclopedia/smoot-hawley-tariff/ · https://www.nber.org/papers/w28616
  • Japanese auto VERs: ⚠ figures cover 1986–90, not 1981–, and the units differ — $13bn consumer loss and ~$3bn net welfare loss are five-year cumulative; the $2bn producer gain is annual. Real source is Berry, Levinsohn & Pakes, AER 89(3):400–430 (1999), not the think-tank reprint. Crandall's cost-per-job is $160,000/yr (Brookings Review, Summer 1984, not peer-reviewed); a circulating $241,000 figure is unsourced and unused.
  • NAFTA: high-school dropouts in the most vulnerable localities saw 8pp slower wage growth 1990–2000 (not 9 — that is a 2011 draft vintage); the 16pp figure is a separate industry channel; null for college graduates. ⚠ The locality effect "essentially disappears" under the paper's own import-share robustness check — Hakobyan & McLaren, REStat 98(4):728–741 (2016): https://www.nber.org/papers/w16535
  • British coalfields: 222,000 male coal jobs lost, 132,400 replaced — about 60% after 23 years, ranging 0% (Lancashire, Northumberland) to 100% (Durham); 400,000+ incapacity claimants as hidden unemployment (11.3% vs 8.1% GB). Scope: male jobs, England and Wales, to 2004 — never a single national average — Beatty, Fothergill & Powell, Environment and Planning A 39(7):1654–1675 (2007): https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/envira/v39y2007i7p1654-1675.html
  • US manufacturing peaks (verified twice, BLS API and FRED independently): absolute peak June 1979 = 19,553,000; postwar share peak May 1953 = 32.4% (1943 hit 38.8% and Nov 1946 33.0%, which BLS excludes as the war era). Both precede NAFTA (1994) and WTO accession (2001): https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-9/forty-years-of-falling-manufacturing-employment.htm

  • "China gave the poor a 6-point inflation break" — overstates ~3×; never peer-reviewed.
  • "533,000 jobs lost 1991–2011" — misdated; that is the 1999–2011 figure.
  • Deaths of despair attributed to trade — contested and confounded with opioid supply; Ruhm finds economic conditions explain under 10% of the 1999–2015 rise in drug deaths.
  • "89,000 manufacturing jobs lost under the tariffs" — revised to −79,000 (re-confirmed 2026-08-27).
  • "Manufacturing employment has risen five consecutive months" — our own first pass wrote this and it is wrong: April and May 2026 both fell. The +28,000 Feb→Jul is correct; the streak is not.
  • "The Supreme Court struck down the tariffs" — true, and materially incomplete on its own: replaced under Section 122 the same day, then under Section 301 the minute that lapsed, with Section 232 never in the case. The effective tariff rate went up, not away.
