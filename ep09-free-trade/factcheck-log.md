# Fact-check log — Is Free Trade Worth the Cost to Local Communities?

_Independent verification pass, run 2026-08-19 (web-sourced, not trusting the draft). Each claim gets a
verdict: **CONFIRMED**, **CORRECTED**, **UNRESOLVED** or **APPROXIMATE**. This is a REQUIRED gate in
this show's production process. The show's differentiator is that this file is real._

- **CONFIRMED** — the source says what the episode says.
- **CORRECTED** — the source says otherwise; the script was changed, and the change is listed under
  "Corrections applied to source" below. These stay in the file: they are the point of it.
- **UNRESOLVED** — could not be established from a source worth trusting, so it is not used on air.
- **APPROXIMATE** — checked, roughly right, and **deliberately not cited on air**.

**Verification status:** adversarial-pass

_Method: five independent research lenses produced the claim set; six verification agents then checked
every numeric and named claim against its primary source. Two claim clusters carried an explicit
presumption against on sourcing grounds. BLS series were pulled directly from the BLS API rather than
read from secondary coverage._

---

## 1. "Chinese import competition explains about a quarter of the decline in US manufacturing employment."
**CORRECTED to ~21%.** Autor, Dorn & Hanson, "The China Syndrome," *AER* 103(6):2121–68, peer-reviewed.
The abstract says "one-quarter," but the body is more conservative: 16% (1990–2000), 26% (2000–2007),
**21% over the full period**. Identification is a shift-share exposure measure instrumented by eight
other high-income countries' Chinese imports.
- https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.103.6.2121

## 2. "The China shock cost 2.0 to 2.4 million jobs."
**CORRECTED — these are two different estimates, not a range.** *Annual Review of Economics* 8:205–240.
**2.0 million** is whole-economy at national-industry level including input-output effects (985,000 of
them in manufacturing); **2.4 million** is commuting-zone level, adding aggregate-demand and
reallocation effects. Both 1999–2011. Presenting them as a range implies an uncertainty band that is
not what the authors report. Both are **gross with respect to exports** — no export offset is estimated.

## 3. "Counting exports, the net effect was +379,000 jobs."
**CONFIRMED, and it does NOT contradict claim 2.** Feenstra, Ma & Xu, *JIE* 120 (2019):46–58,
peer-reviewed: net **+379k at industry level and −68k at commuting-zone level**, 1991–2011, verbatim.
Critically, Feenstra's own commuting-zone import-destruction estimate is **~1,994,000 jobs — which
replicates ADH's 2.0 million.** The net figure arrives only after adding ~2,636,000 export-driven
gains that ADH never estimated. **Both camps have been quoting real numbers past each other.**
- https://mahong.weebly.com/uploads/2/7/0/9/27093249/feenstra-ma-xu-2019jie.pdf

## 4. "533,000 jobs were lost to Chinese imports, 1991–2011."
**CORRECTED — misdated.** −533k is the **1999–2011** figure. The 1991–2011 import effect is **−644k**,
and it covers China plus rest-of-world imports. Not statable as originally given.

## 5. "A net reduction of about 1.5 million manufacturing jobs, 1990–2007."
**CONFIRMED** — and it is ADH's own number: 548,000 (1990–2000) plus 982,000 (2000–2007) = 1.53M.
⚠ "Net" here means net of the demand-driven component, **not** net of export gains.

## 6. "ADH's identification has been credibly challenged."
**CONFIRMED, with a status correction.** Robert Kaestner, "Globalization and the China Shock: A
Reassessment of Autor, Dorn, and Hanson," *Econ Journal Watch* 23(1), March 2026, 27–45 — arguing the
instrument correlates with non-China imports, violating the exclusion restriction, and that the effect
is globalization-wide rather than China-specific. **CORRECTED:** this was described upstream as an
unrefereed 2025 SSRN preprint; it is now published. ⚠ But *Econ Journal Watch* is editor-reviewed
commentary, not a refereed empirical journal — a serious published critique, **not a settled
refutation**. This matters disproportionately because claims 8 and 9 share the same instrument.
- https://econjwatch.org/articles/globalization-and-the-china-shock-a-reassessment-of-autor-dorn-and-hanson

## 7. "Trade's consumer gains are pro-poor: 63% of real income at the 10th percentile vs 28% at the 90th."
**CORRECTED — not a US figure.** Fajgelbaum & Khandelwal, *QJE* 2016, 131(3):1113–80, peer-reviewed,
verbatim in the published text. But it is the **unweighted average across 40 countries** (27 European
plus 13 other large economies, WIOD, flows averaged 2005–2007), not a US-specific result. Per-country
results are in Table V. The direction is safe either way: loss-from-autarky and gains-from-trade are
the same number here, so "trade costs the poor 63%" would be backwards.

## 8. "Effects of the China shock were still measurable in 2019, with 86% absorbed as a fall in the employment rate."
**CONFIRMED, with a review-status caveat.** ADH, "On the Persistence of the China Shock," NBER WP 29401
/ *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity* Fall 2021: −1.54pp manufacturing employment-to-population
ratio (55% of observed change), 86% absorbed as a fall in the overall employment rate, population
decline only among foreign-born workers and native-born aged 25–39. ⚠ **BPEA is a conference journal
— editor-selected with named discussants (Bertrand, Glaeser) publishing alongside — not blind
refereeing.** This is the most-quoted "adjustment doesn't happen" source in the debate.

## 9. "Trade-exposed places more than fully rebounded after 2010."
**CONFIRMED, and it is compatible with claim 8.** Autor, Dorn, Hanson, Jones & Setzler, **"Places
versus People: The Ins and Outs of Labor Market Adjustment to Globalization,"** NBER WP 33424 (Jan
2025, rev. June 2025), verbatim: employment levels more than fully rebound while employment-to-
population ratios remain depressed and manufacturing further atrophies. Refilled by cohorts below
working age at shock onset — disproportionately native-born Hispanics, immigrants, women and
college-educated workers — into low-wage healthcare, education, retail and hospitality. Both in- and
out-migration are depressed; incumbents "age in place." Census LEHD linked employer-employee records,
2000–2019. ⚠ **Unrefereed NBER working paper**, reportedly forthcoming as a *Handbook* chapter.
**⚠⚠ Claims 8 and 9 share the same Bartik/ADH instrument, so their identification stands or falls
together. Neither is a check on the other.**

## 10. "Displaced workers adjust."
**CORRECTED — true only for workers who were already doing well.** Autor, Dorn, Hanson & Song, "Trade
Adjustment: Worker-Level Evidence," *QJE* 129(4), 2014 — the only fully refereed item in this cluster.
Cumulative earnings losses concentrate among low initial wage, low tenure, low labour-force attachment
workers, who churn *within* manufacturing into repeat shocks; high-wage workers move across employers
with minimal loss.

## 11. "Americans paid the 2018 tariffs; the cost was $1.4 billion a month."
**CORRECTED — that is the deadweight loss, not the cost.** Amiti, Redding & Weinstein, *JEP* 33(4),
2019: complete pass-through confirmed (duty-exclusive import prices did not fall; coefficient −0.012,
s.e. 0.023 — foreign exporters absorbed nothing). But $1.4bn/month is the **efficiency loss**; added
tax costs were **$3.2bn/month**, and total cost to consumers and importers by December 2018 was
**$4.7bn/month**. ⚠ **JEP articles are solicited, not peer-reviewed** — do not describe it as such.

## 12. "The 2018 tariffs cost $51 billion, or $7.2 billion net."
**CONFIRMED, with a significance caveat.** Fajgelbaum, Goldberg, Kennedy & Khandelwal, *QJE* 135(1),
2020, peer-reviewed (five anonymous referees): "$51 billion, or 0.27% of GDP"; "aggregate real income
loss was $7.2 billion, or 0.04% of GDP," annual at 2016 prices. ⚠ **The $7.2bn is not statistically
significant**; the $51bn is. The net figure cannot carry weight on air.

## 13. "Flaaen & Pierce found the 2018 metals tariffs cost about 75,000 manufacturing jobs (~0.6%)."
**CORRECTED — false as attributed.** That figure appears in **neither version** of Flaaen & Pierce
(both were searched). It originates with Cox & Russ, EconoFact (2020): "we compute that this amounts
to about 75,000," covering steel and aluminium input costs only. EconoFact is not peer-reviewed.
**Additionally, the quoted breakdown spliced two vintages:** the FEDS working paper gives net −1.4%
(protection +0.3, inputs −1.1, retaliation −0.7) with 4.1% higher prices; the published *REStat*
version gives the −2.7% breakdown with **3.3%** prices. Neither version contains both halves.

## 14. "Washing-machine tariffs cost $815,000 per job created."
**CONFIRMED.** Flaaen, Hortaçsu & Tintelnot, *AER* 110(7), 2020, peer-reviewed — all four figures are
the authors' own: "over 815,000 USD per job created," ~$1.5bn annual consumer cost, $82.2M in revenue,
1,800 jobs. ⚠ Those 1,800 jobs are **company-announced figures the authors adopt** ("advertised,"
"claims"), not an econometric estimate, and 1,600 of them are at foreign-owned Samsung and LG plants.

## 15. "Steel tariffs cost about $650,000 per job."
**APPROXIMATE.** Verbatim from Hufbauer & Jung, PIIE, 20 Dec 2018 — but a **think-tank blog post with
no peer review**, and the figures are *ex ante* model projections, not measured outcomes. PIIE's own
2025 update revises it to **$900,000/job**. The direction is sound; **no single figure is stated on air**.

## 16. "Manufacturing employment fell 89,000 from April 2025 to February 2026, indistinguishable from the pre-tariff pace."
**CORRECTED, and the window is now OBSOLETE — do not use it.** Flagged with a presumption against
(opinion media). BLS CES3000000001 pulled directly from the BLS API and cross-checked against FRED
(`MANEMP`, identical on every overlapping month):

- **−79,000, not −89,000.** Apr 2025 = 12,662k → Feb 2026 = 12,583k over 10 months = −7,900/month.
  The −89,000 was accurate *at publication*: Feb 2026 first printed at −12,000, was revised to −6,000
  on 3 Apr 2026, and now stands at +1,000. That ~10,000 upward revision exactly accounts for the gap.
- **⛔ The trend reversed.** Feb 2026 → Jul 2026: **12,583 → 12,611 = +28,000**, five consecutive
  months of gains. Across the full tariff era, Apr 2025 → Jul 2026 = **−51,000 over 15 months =
  −3,400/month**, roughly a third of the pre-tariff pace. **Any script using the Apr 2025–Feb 2026
  window states something that stopped being true months ago.**
- **⛔ Context no lens raised: the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs 6–3 on 20 February
  2026, with collection halting 24 February.** Noted as coincident with the reversal; causation NOT
  asserted. An episode arguing tariff effects without this is arguing about a substantially
  dismantled policy.
- **The series cannot resolve the question being asked of it.** The preliminary benchmark revision
  (9 Sep 2025) cut manufacturing by **95,000** for March 2025 — larger than the entire 89,000 effect
  claimed — and a single month moved 10,000 through routine revision. At this resolution −7,900/month
  and −9,583/month are not distinguishable.
- The 9,583/month baseline **reproduces to the digit** (Jan 2023 12,903k → Jan 2025 12,673k =
  −230,000 ÷ 24) but is the **most decline-favorable defensible window**, blending a nearly flat 2023
  (−1,917/mo) with a collapsing 2024 (−14,917/mo). Alternative windows land between −8,300 and
  −9,600, so the conclusion is robust but the precision is false.
- ⚠ **"Statistically indistinguishable" was never in the source** — it was the research lens's gloss.
  The article concedes the baseline and pivots to causation.

⚠ **Sourcing note, third occurrence:** this cluster carried a presumption against because it came
from opinion media, and **the numbers checked out** — both trace to real BLS data and one reproduces
exactly. The weakness was framing, not fabrication. Together with the New York cluster in
[ep08](../../ep08-rent-control/research/factcheck-log.md), that is three times a
which-side-benefits screen pointed the wrong way. **Verify the claim, not the claimant.**

## 17. "Trade Adjustment Assistance participants earned $3,300 less than comparable non-participants."
**CORRECTED — that is the lower bound of a range.** D'Amico & Schochet, *The Evaluation of the TAA
Program: A Synthesis of Major Findings*, DOL ETAOP 2013-08, verbatim: participants' fourth-year
earnings were "either **$3,300 less than, or about the same as**, those of comparison-group members" —
explicitly labelled lower- and upper-bound estimates. Method is propensity-score matching against
non-eligible UI claimants across 25 states, not random assignment, and the authors caution about
unobservables and about a 4-year window in which trainees re-entered during the recession. **The
honest claim: TAA's earnings effect ranges from clearly negative to null, never positive.** Net
benefits were negative from every perspective in the cost-benefit analysis.

## 18. "TAA peaked around $600M/yr and was terminated on 1 July 2022."
**CORRECTED on the peak.** Termination confirmed (CRS R47200; phased termination 1 July 2022, no new
certifications; FY2022 pre-sequestration appropriation $540M). But the peak was **$710.6M (FY2015)**,
with $685M in FY2009–10 — "~$600M" understates it. The FY2021 ≈ $634M figure is **UNRESOLVED**: no
primary document confirming it could be opened.

## 19. "In trade-exposed regions, the rise in SSDI payments was more than 30x the rise in TAA."
**CONFIRMED — and the citation upgrades.** This is verbatim in the *AER* paper itself, not merely in a
summary: "the estimated dollar increase in per capita SSDI payments is more than thirty times as large
as the estimated dollar increase in TAA payments." It compares **per-capita transfer increases across
commuting zones**, not per-worker benefit levels. Strongest-sourced claim in the adjustment cluster.

## 20. "Janesville retrainees did worse than those who did not retrain."
**CONFIRMED, with the selection caveat that decides its weight.** 1,740 Blackhawk Technical College
enrollees against ~28,000 Rock and Green county UI claimants who did not enroll. By mid-2011, about a
third of enrollees were employed year-round against about half of non-enrollees; earnings **−36%**
against **−8%**. ⚠ The authors could not control for quality differences — no education data on
non-enrollees — so **selection bias is unresolved** and the episode says so.

## 21. "Displaced-worker reemployment runs 75% at ages 25–54, 67% at 55–64 and 44% at 65+, and over 21% take a 20%+ pay cut."
**CORRECTED — two survey years were spliced.** The 75/67/44 figures are **January 2020** (2017–19
displacements, USDL-20-1620) — but in that same release only **17.1%** of reemployed full-timers
reporting earnings took a 20%+ cut. The ~21%+ figure is closer to January 2024 (24.9%) or January 2018
(27.1%). January 2024's age figures read 74.5 / **55.3** / **34.4**. **One survey year must be chosen
and stated.**

## 22. "Reshoring announced 244,000 jobs in 2024 and 1.7 million filled since 2010."
**APPROXIMATE — advocacy self-reported.** Reshoring Initiative figures confirmed as reported
(244,940 announced 2024; ~174,000 projected 2025; "over 2 million… since 2010. Of those, an
**estimated** 1.7 million have been filled"). But the method is the organisation's own press-clipping
library "adjusted for under-reporting," with details "available upon request" — **no independent
audit and no published method for the 1.7M.** ⚠ Against this, BLS counts manufacturing employment
**down ~87,000 in 2024**. Announcements are not payroll, and the episode does not treat them as such.

## 23. "Foxconn promised 13,000 Wisconsin jobs and delivered 1,242."
**CONFIRMED, with one nuance.** 13,000 promised in 2017; **1,242 jobs and ~$717M invested as of 31
December 2024** per WEDC verification. ⚠ The contract was renegotiated in 2021 to a 1,454-job target,
so 1,242 is measured against the *original* promise — which the episode states.

## 24. "Deaths of despair in trade-exposed communities were caused by trade."
**UNRESOLVED.** Pierce & Schott (*AER: Insights* 2020) link China-trade exposure to suicide and
overdose deaths; Ruhm (*J. Health Econ.* 2019) finds economic conditions explain **under 10%** of the
210% rise in drug deaths (1999–2015), with drug supply dominating. Two independent lenses flagged this
as the weakest link in the chain before verification began. **This is the most emotionally powerful
claim available to the episode and it is not used.**

## 25. "British coalfields never recovered."
**CORRECTED — and the original lens was wrong to exclude this as unverifiable.** Beatty, Fothergill &
Powell, *Environment and Planning A* 39(7):1654–1675 (2007), peer-reviewed: 222,000 male coal jobs
lost, **132,400 replaced — about 60% after 23 years**, with a range from **0% (Lancashire,
Northumberland) to 100% (Durham)**. Hidden unemployment: 400,000+ incapacity claimants, 11.3% against
8.1% for Great Britain. Scope on air: male jobs, England and Wales, to 2004 — **never as a single
national average**.

## 26. "Smoot-Hawley barely raised tariffs and did not cause the Depression."
**CORRECTED — messier than the popular debunking.** The 38.5%→41.1% figures come from O'Brien's EH.net
entry (a *statutory* 1922-vs-1930 comparison); Irwin reads the same source as **35.65%→41.14%**, so
the "2.5 percentage points" framing is not settled. Real exports falling ~1.7% of 1929 GDP is
confirmed, but the "~21% of the GDP decline" is that 1.7% times an **assumed multiplier of 2** — an
upper bound, not an estimate. ⚠ And realized rates hit **59.1% by 1932** as deflation inflated
specific duties, so 41.1% *understates* the protection. Retaliation was real: exports to retaliating
countries fell 28–33% (Mitchener, O'Rourke & Wandschneider, *EJ* 2022).

## 27. "Japanese auto export restraints cost consumers $13bn while producers gained $2bn."
**CORRECTED — a unit mismatch.** The real source is Berry, Levinsohn & Pakes, *AER* 89(3):400–430
(1999), peer-reviewed, not the think tank it was cited to, and the figures cover **1986–90, not
1981–**. The $13bn consumer loss and ~$3bn net welfare loss are **five-year cumulative**; the $2bn
producer gain is **annual**. Quoting them side by side compares five years against one. Crandall's
cost-per-job figure is **$160,000/yr** (*Brookings Review*, Summer 1984, not peer-reviewed); a
circulating $241,000 figure is unsourced and is not used.

## 28. "NAFTA cost high-school dropouts in vulnerable localities up to 9pp in wage growth."
**CORRECTED to 8pp, with a robustness caveat.** Hakobyan & McLaren, *Review of Economics and
Statistics* 98(4):728–741 (2016), peer-reviewed. The current draft gives **8pp**, not 9 (the 9 is a
2011 vintage); the 16pp figure is a **separate industry channel**, not a within-locality effect; the
result is null for college graduates. ⚠ **The locality effect "essentially disappears" under the
paper's own import-share robustness check** — that caveat travels with the claim.

## 29. "US manufacturing employment peaked before NAFTA and China."
**CONFIRMED — verified twice, via the BLS API and FRED independently.** Absolute peak **June 1979 =
19,553,000** (CES3000000001). Share peak **May 1953 = 32.4%**, matching BLS's own statement. ⚠ On the
full series, 1943 (38.8%) and November 1946 (33.0%) were higher — BLS excludes the war era, so say
**"postwar peak."** Both dates comfortably precede NAFTA (1994) and WTO accession (2001).

## 30. "The Corn Laws repeal left Britain no better off, with landowners losing 3–5%."
**CORRECTED to 3–4%.** Irwin & Chepeliev, "The Economic Consequences of Sir Robert Peel," *Economic
Journal* 131(640):3322–3337 (2021), peer-reviewed. Welfare **−0.1% to −0.3% Hicksian equivalent
variation**, not percent of GDP. Landowners **−3.1% to −3.9%** (the "3–5%" appears only in the working
paper abstract); bottom 90% **+0.3–0.6%**; top 10% −1.4%. Method is a static CGE on an 1841
input-output table. ⚠ Their 4% land-rent fall contradicts Williamson (12%) and O'Rourke (9%).

## 31. "87% of manufacturing job losses were caused by automation and 13% by trade."
**CORRECTED — the arithmetic is real, the causal reading is not.** Hicks & Devaraj, *The Myth and the
Reality of Manufacturing in America*, Ball State CBER, June 2015 — an ~8-page policy brief, **not
peer-reviewed**. Table 4, all manufacturing, **2000–2010 only**, 5,647,700 job losses: productivity
**87.8%**, trade **13.4%**, domestic demand **+1.2%** (a job *gain*; the shares exceed 100% because
demand offsets). The method is an **accounting identity**, with "productivity" defined as output per
worker — a residual decomposition with no instrument and no counterfactual. The authors' own caveat is
in the report; Hicks told NPR (24 Oct 2019) that productivity is not automation, since it also
captures education, process improvements and other technology. **Airable phrasing is fixed in
`sources.md`. For a causal automation number the episode uses Acemoglu & Restrepo instead.**

## 32. "One additional robot per 1,000 workers cuts the employment-to-population ratio by 0.2pp and wages by 0.42%."
**CONFIRMED.** Acemoglu & Restrepo, "Robots and Jobs," *JPE* 2020, 128(6):2188–2244, peer-reviewed,
verbatim in the published abstract. The "identified separately from trade" claim is genuine: results
hold controlling for exposure to Chinese *and* Mexican imports, routine-job decline and offshoring,
and robot exposure is only weakly correlated with China exposure. ⚠ The 2017 NBER draft gave ranges
(0.18–0.34pp; 0.25–0.5%); cite the *JPE* figures. **The only claim in this cluster needing no
correction at all.**

## Corrections applied to source

- ADH's "one quarter" → 21% over the full period, per the paper's own body.
- "2.0–2.4 million" → presented as two distinct estimates at different levels of aggregation, not a range.
- "533k, 1991–2011" → cut as misdated; the 1991–2011 import effect is −644k.
- The ADH-vs-Feenstra "contradiction" → dissolved: Feenstra replicates ~2.0M in losses and adds ~2.64M
  export gains ADH never estimated. Both figures now stated together, with the geographic caveat.
- 63%/28% consumer gains → labelled a 40-country average, not a US figure.
- "Flaaen & Pierce: 75,000 jobs" → removed; re-attributed to Cox & Russ (EconoFact), and the spliced
  employment/price figures separated by paper vintage.
- "$1.4bn/month" → identified as the deadweight loss; the $4.7bn/month total cost stated alongside.
- "$7.2bn net loss" → flagged as not statistically significant; not used as a headline.
- TAA "$3,300 less" → restated as the lower bound of a range running to "about the same."
- TAA peak "$600M" → $710.6M (FY2015); the FY2021 figure withheld as unresolved.
- SSDI 30× → citation upgraded from a summary to the AER paper itself.
- BLS displaced-worker figures → one survey year selected; the spliced 21% pay-cut figure corrected.
- "89,000 jobs" → −79,000 from the BLS series directly; "statistically indistinguishable" dropped as
  rhetorical.
- Reshoring totals → labelled advocacy self-reported, with the BLS counter-figure stated.
- Japan VER figures → five-year cumulative and annual figures separated; source corrected to Berry,
  Levinsohn & Pakes.
- NAFTA "9pp" → 8pp, with the industry channel separated and the robustness caveat attached.
- Corn Laws "3–5%" → 3–4%, and welfare restated as Hicksian equivalent variation.
- Smoot-Hawley → the rate comparison flagged as contested, the 21% flagged as an assumed-multiplier
  upper bound, and the 59.1% realized-rate figure added.
- "87% automation" → restated as an accounting share for 2000–2010, never as a cause.
- Coalfield replacement rate → restored after the research lens wrongly excluded it as unverifiable.

## Fairness audit

- **Neither side written beyond its evidence?** Both remedies fail on their own evidence, and the brief
  says so symmetrically: TAA's earnings effect never turns positive, and tariffs cost $815,000 per
  washing-machine job. Neither side's flagship number survived intact — the pro-trade side lost the
  clean US framing of the 63/28 figure and the Broda-Romalis inflation gap; the protectionist side lost
  the 75,000-jobs attribution and the reshoring tallies.
- **Straw-manning / cherry-picking?** The strongest emotional claim available to the anti-trade side —
  deaths of despair — is excluded as unresolved despite being highly quotable, and two lenses warned
  against it independently. The strongest pro-trade consumer statistic is cut to a third of its
  circulated size. Both cuts run against the episode's dramatic interest.
- **The both-sides-quoting-past-each-other finding is the episode's spine and it emerged from
  verification, not from either camp.** ADH and Feenstra measure the same economy two ways; "places
  recovered" and "adjustment never happened" describe the same data with different nouns. That is the
  same structural lesson as ep07's Austin baselines and ep08's St. Paul rebound, reached independently
  in a third literature.
- **A live threat is disclosed rather than buried:** the Kaestner critique in *Econ Journal Watch*
  targets the instrument shared by claims 8 and 9, so the two headline persistence findings stand or
  fall together. The episode names this rather than presenting ADH as settled.
- **Identity↔stance rotation:** not yet applicable — cast rotation is step 4, not run in this pass.

## Open questions this episode could not settle

- **The 2026 argument was never engaged.** Every lens litigated the 2001–2010 China shock; none
  addressed supply-chain resilience, semiconductors, strategic autonomy or pandemic exposure — the
  case actually being made for trade restriction today. This is the largest gap in the research and
  the strongest candidate for a follow-up episode.
- Does the Kaestner critique hold? If ADH's instrument is picking up globalization generally rather
  than China specifically, the magnitude survives but the attribution does not.
- Is there a case anywhere of a successful, deliberate regional transition — a place that lost its
  industry and whose *original residents* recovered? No lens produced one, and the coalfield range
  (0% to 100% replacement) suggests the variation is large and unexplained.
- Would the transfer have worked if it had been funded at scale? Every lens converged on "compensation
  was never made," but no evidence establishes what adequate compensation would have achieved.

# Re-run — 2026-08-27 (currency + news peg)

Scope: **targeted refresh only.** Nothing in § Settled or § Contested was re-argued — the 2026-08-25
adversarial pass stands. This run did two things: went looking for the peg (the research-phase step
the peg check now enforces), and re-checked the two live-data facts the first pass flagged as
having a short shelf life. Method: direct web verification against primary sources, no lens agents.

## ✅ The peg — found, and it is structural rather than decorative

**On 1 July 2026 the United States declined to renew the USMCA.** Verified against the primary source:
USTR, *Ambassador Greer Issues Statement on the USMCA Joint Review*, 1 July 2026 — *"The United States
did not agree to renew the USMCA in its current form. As a result, the USMCA is not renewed."* Canada
and Mexico each confirmed they wanted the 16-year extension. The agreement does not lapse; Article
34.7.4 sends it to a joint review annually until the parties agree or it expires 1 July 2036.
Corroborated by CRS R48964 and CSIS.

Why it is the right peg rather than a topical ornament: the episode's question is whether free trade
was worth what it cost the places that paid, and NAFTA/USMCA is the agreement that question is usually
about. The negotiation's headline issue — automotive rules of origin — is an argument about where the
jobs sit. Recorded in the episode metadata under `news_peg`, in § Why now, and carried into the description.

## ⛔ CORRECTED — "manufacturing employment has risen for five consecutive months"

**FALSE as a streak; the magnitude is right.** BLS CES3000000001, retrieved 2026-08-27:

| Dec 25 | Jan 26 | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul (p) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12,580 | 12,582 | 12,583 | 12,598 | **12,597** | **12,595** | 12,606 | 12,611 |

April and May both **fell**. Feb → Jul is indeed **+28,000**, so the number the first pass gave is
correct and the description of it is not. Corrected in § Currency check. The trough is **December

# Re-run, part 2 — 2026-08-27 (the peg was wrong, and the method is why)

## ⛔ THE MISS, recorded first because it matters more than the facts below

The 2026-08-27 currency pass was run **specifically to find the news peg** — the one job
the peg check exists to force — and it settled on the USMCA non-renewal of 1 July 2026 while
**missing an escalation four days old**: a 50% tariff on US$20bn of Canadian goods under a statute
never used in 96 years, the collapse of Canada–US talks, and a C$27.6bn retaliation. Lucas found it in
one question.

⛔ **The peg check went GREEN on that peg**, exactly as designed — its header says it reads structure,
not truth or quality. A peg existed, was dated, was sourced and was carried into the description. The
check cannot tell a *stale-but-valid* peg from the *best available* one, so a green check gave false
comfort. That is not a defect in the checker; it is the limit the checker names about itself, and it
means the guarantee has to come from the research method upstream.

**Diagnosis — entity-driven search cannot find an entity you do not know exists.** All eight queries in
the first pass were seeded from vocabulary already in the brief: USMCA, IEEPA, Section 232, Section 122,
BLS, TAA. That finds *updates to known things*. It structurally cannot surface **Section 338**, because
you cannot search for a statute you do not know was invoked. Three specific gaps:

1. **No date-first query.** Never asked "what happened in trade this week" — only "what happened to
   \<thing I already knew about\>".
2. **No counterparty enumeration.** The episode is about trade and local communities; the largest US
   trading partner was never queried by name. One query naming Canada found everything.
3. **No front page read.** Search ranks against the querier's terms; a news front page ranks by what
   happened. Only the former was used.

⚠ Same root cause as the first pass's miss of the SCOTUS IEEPA ruling by all five research lenses. That
was logged as "the lenses missed it" and fixed by adding verification. **It was never the lenses; it is
the query discipline**, and adding a sixth lens without changing that discipline would reproduce it.

# Conflict resolution — 2026-08-30

The `/deep-research` run of 2026-08-30 killed three claims 0–3 under 3-vote adversarial review, two of
which this brief carries as **settled**. Both passes could not be right. All three were taken to the
primary PDF and read in full text (`pypdf` extraction, not summaries, not search snippets).

★ **Result: 2 of the 3 kills were FALSE NEGATIVES. This brief was right and the newer, more elaborate
method was wrong.** That is the whole reason [[verify-the-claim-not-the-claimant]] exists — a
110-agent adversarial pipeline is not evidence, and "more thorough" is not "more correct."

## ✅ CONFLICT 1 — "2.0–2.4 million net job losses, 1999–2011" — **OUR BRIEF STANDS**

Killed 0–3 **twice** by the dossier, on both magnitude and the "net of reallocation" characterisation.
It is **verbatim in the abstract** of Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson & Price (NBER w20395):

> *"Our central estimates suggest net job losses of 2.0 to 2.4 million stemming from the rise in import
> competition from China over the period 1999 to 2011."*

The word "net" is the authors'. Their design explicitly includes *"the net effects of conventional
labor reallocation… and Keynesian multipliers."* Every sub-figure this brief carries is reproduced in
the paper's own summary table: Table 6B cols 6/9 → −985k manufacturing, −994k non-manufacturing,
−1,979k total (1999–2011); Table 7 cols 6/9 → −2,367k at CZ level; direct effect alone −560k.

⚠ **One real error found on our side, and it is a citation error, not a factual one.** The estimate
originates with **AADHP** (*JOLE* 34(S1) 2016 / NBER w20395); `sources.md` cited the ADH *Annual Review*
restatement. Corrected.

## ✅ CONFLICT 2 — "SSDI rose >30× the rise in TAA" — **OUR BRIEF STANDS**

Killed 0–3, with the instruction *"do not use it without an independent check."* Verbatim in ADH,
*AER* 103(6):

> *"For regions affected by Chinese imports, the estimated dollar increase in per capita SSDI payments
> is more than thirty times as large as the estimated dollar increase in TAA payments."*

And the underlying numbers support it independently: per $1,000 of growth in a CZ's per-worker import
exposure, TAA rises **$0.23** per capita against SSDI's **~$8** — ≈35×. The paper adds that average TAA
payments were *"a mere $2 per capita, which is less than 0.05 percentage points of total transfers."*

## ⛔ CONFLICT 3 — "223 CZs, 32.8% of the population" — **REAL, and BOTH circulating versions are wrong**

The dossier was right that this pass had not verified it. It is real, verbatim in ADH's persistence
paper (NBER w29401) — but it is **not** a gross figure later corrected to 6.3%. All three numbers are
**net**, and they are a sensitivity ladder on one assumption:

| assumed aggregate gain from China trade | CZs with a net absolute loss | US population share |
|---|---|---|
| 0.22% (Caliendo et al. / Galle et al.) | 223 | 32.8% |
| 0.44% (doubled) | 173 | 15.9% |
| 1.25% (Jaravel–Sager) | 82 | 6.3% |

⛔ **Two advocacy sources quote opposite ends as if they were the answer** — a protectionist outlet
gives 32.8%, a free-trade think tank gives 6.3%. Neither discloses the ladder. Adopted into § Settled
**with all three rungs**, and flagged: presenting either number alone is a half-quotation.

⚠ Not to be confused with **Galle, Rodríguez-Clare & Yi** (*ReStud* 2023), checked and found to say
something different: *"a modest but non-negligible number of groups representing 13% of the population
suffer welfare losses, and… those losses can be up to four times as high as the average gains."*

## ⛔ AND ONE CORRECTION THE DOSSIER GOT RIGHT — we had it backwards

This brief read *"~21% … (the abstract's 'one quarter'; the body is more conservative)."* The body is
**less** conservative. ADH 2013's main specification explains **33% (1990–2000), 55% (2000–07), 44%
(1990–2007)**; ~21% arrives only after a further supply-shock-only adjustment. The abstract's *"in our
main specification, import competition explains one-quarter"* does not describe the main specification
— the paper's own wording is loose and we repeated it. ⏭ The 21%–55% spread is a **contested** range
and belongs in § Contested; flagged rather than restructured.

## What this changes about the research-engine decision

⛔ The dossier did not merely *miss* things — its adversarial verification **actively killed two true,
verbatim, abstract-level claims**. A 3-vote refutation pass that produces false negatives on sentences
printed in a paper's abstract is a different and more dangerous failure than under-coverage: an
under-covering method leaves gaps you can see, a false-negative method deletes facts you already had.
Recorded in the lab's research-engine test, run 01 (scored).

★ Net ledger from the whole exercise: the dossier contributed **one better reframe**, **two genuine
additions** (Caliendo–Dvorkin–Parro ~15%; Borusyak–Jaravel on distributional neutrality), **one real
correction** (the one-quarter wording) — against **two false kills** and **one missed live critique**
(Kaestner). Useful, and not authoritative.
