## The research · Same shock, opposite answers
**Walt:** Eight days ago a fifty percent duty took effect on about twenty billion dollars of Canadian goods. Cars, dairy, alcohol.
**Cole:** Fifty percent is steep. What's the legal basis?
**Walt:** Section three thirty-eight of the Tariff Act of nineteen thirty. It had sat on the books for ninety-six years without ever once being used to impose a tariff.
**Cole:** Never used. So why now?
**Walt:** Because it needs almost nothing. Other tariff powers require an investigation or a national-security finding. This one lets a president act by proclamation alone.
**Cole:** And Canada answered.
**Walt:** Three days later, dollar for dollar — about twenty-eight billion Canadian on American goods, from the eighth of September.
**Cole:** That's the part I'd expect. Tariffs, then counter-tariffs.
**Walt:** It's the other half of their announcement that matters here. Alongside the retaliation, Canada put seven and a half billion dollars behind the workers and firms the shock lands on.
**Cole:** Behind the workers. Not the companies?
**Walt:** Both, but the largest single piece — three and a half billion — is income support, retention and retraining. And the release says it builds on nearly twenty-five billion already spent since the tariffs started.
**Cole:** So call it thirty-two billion. What's the American equivalent?
**Walt:** The programme built for exactly this purpose is called Trade Adjustment Assistance. It peaked at about seven hundred million dollars a year, and it was terminated in July twenty twenty-two.
**Cole:** Hold on. Same shock, same week, same border. One country spends thirty-two billion and the other one's programme is switched off?

## The research · The ledger nets to roughly zero
**Walt:** That's where we're going. But start with what genuinely isn't in dispute, because it's more than the shouting suggests.
**Walt:** Chinese import competition destroyed a large number of American jobs, and the number is not small.
**Cole:** How large?
**Walt:** About two million, economy-wide, between nineteen ninety-nine and twenty eleven. That's Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Price. Just under a million of those were in manufacturing itself.
**Cole:** And nobody disputes that?
**Walt:** Nobody serious. What's disputed is what you put next to it. Count the export side too, and Feenstra, Ma and Xu find gains of about two point six million.
**Cole:** So it nets to roughly zero.
**Walt:** Nationally, roughly zero. A small plus one way of measuring it, a small minus the other. Both are real, and both are on screen.
**Cole:** But the losses and the gains didn't land in the same towns.
**Walt:** They did not. And the second settled thing is that American manufacturing employment peaked long before any of this. The absolute peak is June nineteen seventy-nine.
**Cole:** Before NAFTA. Before China joined the World Trade Organization.
**Walt:** Fourteen years before one and twenty-two years before the other. Anyone dating the decline to a trade deal is starting the clock late.

## The research · The places came back, the people didn't
**Cole:** So if the national ledger is roughly zero, where's the argument?
**Walt:** In the places. And this is the finding that reorganised the whole file for me. The towns recovered. Employment levels in trade-exposed places more than fully rebound after twenty ten.
**Cole:** That sounds like the story ending well.
**Walt:** It's the employment-to-population ratio that doesn't come back. The jobs returned. The share of working-age people holding one didn't.
**Cole:** How does that happen?
**Walt:** The recovery is compositional. New, younger cohorts move in and fill lower-paid healthcare, education, retail and hospitality work. The people who lost the manufacturing jobs neither recover their earnings nor move away. They age in place.
**Cole:** So the place healed and the population changed underneath it.
**Walt:** Which is why the follow-up paper by Autor, Dorn and Hanson is titled Places versus People. And it's the reason both sides can quote a true recovery statistic at each other forever.
**Cole:** Then what happened to the compensation? There's supposed to be compensation.
**Walt:** That's the third settled thing, and it's the one I'd put in front of anyone who argues about this. In trade-exposed regions, the per-person rise in disability payments was more than thirty times the rise in trade adjustment assistance.
**Cole:** Thirty times. So the safety net that caught people wasn't the trade one.
**Walt:** It was disability. And the trade programme was terminated in twenty twenty-two. Meanwhile about a hundred billion dollars in tariff refunds has been certified — to importers — and it's clearing in months.
**Cole:** Refunds to importers in months, and the workers' programme is four years dead. I want to be careful here, but that is a choice somebody made.

## The research · Every headline number is a band

**Cole:** Go on.
**Walt:** Ask how much of the manufacturing collapse trade actually caused, and the honest answer is a range, not a number. Sixteen percent at the low end. Fifty-five at the high end.
**Cole:** That's not a range, that's two different arguments.
**Walt:** It is. And I want to flag something, because we got this wrong ourselves in an earlier draft. The most-quoted figure in this entire debate is one quarter, and it comes from the abstract of the Autor, Dorn and Hanson paper.

**Walt:** The abstract says one quarter. The paper's own main specification says forty-four percent over the same window. Anyone citing one quarter as the headline number is repeating the paper's loose wording, and we did it too until we read the table.
**Cole:** What's at the other end?

**Cole:** One result, two denominators, and you get sixteen or thirty-six.

## The reframe
**Lucas:** I went looking for a fight about whether trade was worth it, and I came back with a fight about a payment nobody ever made.
**Lucas:** Here's what I mean. The case for free trade was never that nobody gets hurt. It's that the winners gain enough to compensate the losers and still come out ahead.
**Lucas:** That's a real result. But it's a theorem about a transfer. It never claimed the transfer actually happens. And in the United States, it didn't. About seven hundred million a year at its peak, against a shock that moved disability payments thirty times more.
**Lucas:** So when somebody tells you trade was worth it, or that it wasn't, ask them the same question: worth it to whom, and measured where?
**Lucas:** And the thing neither side can wriggle out of is that this stopped being hypothetical eight days ago. One country attached a worker package to its tariffs. The other refunded the importers and left the workers' programme switched off.

## The debate · Team Green · WORTH IT

**Erin:** Third, and this is the one I'd ask you to sit with: even the largest careful estimate of trade's share of the manufacturing decline leaves most of it unexplained. Sixteen percent at the low end. Even at the high end, most of that collapse is not trade.
**Erin:** Fourth, the alternative has a price and we have measured it. The washing-machine tariffs cost about eight hundred thousand dollars per job created.
**Erin:** And the twenty eighteen tariffs passed through completely into American prices. Not partly. Completely — nearly five billion dollars a month by that December.
**Erin:** So the remedy on offer costs consumers more per job than the job pays, several times over, and it does not bring the factories back.
**Erin:** Now the concession, and it's a real one. The compensation that makes my case work was never funded. About seven hundred million a year at peak, terminated in twenty twenty-two.
**Erin:** My opponent will say that a national net of zero is no comfort to the town that lost its plant. He's right, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.
**Erin:** But the answer to a transfer that was never made is to make it. Not to tax the imports and hand the refund to the importer.

## The verdict
**Lena:** This one doesn't resolve, and I want to be exact about where it stops rather than call it a draw and move on.
**Lena:** Both teams accepted the same aggregate. About two million jobs lost, about two point six million gained, netting near zero nationally. Neither debater disputed it in either direction.
**Lena:** So the disagreement isn't about what happened to the country. It's about whether a national total is an answer to a question asked about a place — and that is a values question wearing an evidence question's clothes.
**Lena:** Both sides also conceded their weak flank. Green conceded the compensation that makes her case work was never funded. Gold conceded the aggregate nets to roughly zero and that the count of harmed places swings five-fold on one assumption.
**Lena:** The case that it was worth it rests on a ledger that nets to zero, on a decline that began in nineteen seventy-nine, and on a remedy measured at about eight hundred thousand dollars per job.
**Lena:** The case against rests on a loss that never reaches zero on any published assumption, on adjustment that outlasts a career, and on sixty percent replacement after twenty-three years being the good outcome.
**Lena:** And the thing both cases are downstream of is a payment nobody made. The compensation principle is a theorem about a transfer, not a promise that it happens.
**Lena:** Eight days ago one country made that transfer and another one didn't. So the question we're handing you isn't whether trade was worth it. It's whether you'd pay what would make it worth it to the people who paid for it. That one's yours.
