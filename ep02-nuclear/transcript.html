Cold open

Ask the internet whether nuclear power should anchor our clean energy future, and you'll get a shouting match. Ask the actual evidence, and something stranger happens: half the argument turns out to be over. And nobody told the shouters. So for the next few minutes, let's do the thing the internet won't. Let's separate what's settled from what's actually worth debating.

Research 1

First, where things actually stand. Nuclear supplies about nine percent of the world's electricity, from roughly four hundred forty reactors. And just five countries run most of them. Only five? That's more concentrated than I'd have guessed. Much more. In the United States, it's about one-fifth of the country's power, from ninety-four reactors, averaging forty-four years old. Forty-four years. That sounds ancient for a power plant. Older than most. But here's the number that drives this entire debate. In twenty twenty-four, America's reactors ran at ninety-two percent of maximum output. Wind ran at thirty-four. Solar, twenty-three. So nuclear's real edge isn't that it's clean. It's that it runs almost all the time. And demand is suddenly surging, for AI data centers. Microsoft is restarting Three Mile Island. Amazon bought into a Pennsylvania plant. Google and Meta have signed for reactors that don't exist yet. Up to six point six gigawatts by twenty thirty-five. But none of that is running today, is it? Not yet. Every deal is a restart ramping up, or a build years away. Which brings us to the first thing that's genuinely settled.

Research 2 (settled)

On climate, nuclear's lifecycle emissions are about twelve grams of CO₂ per kilowatt hour. The same class as wind, far below gas or coal. And on safety, the numbers are stark. Coal kills about twenty-five people per terawatt hour. Gas, nearly three. Nuclear? About zero point zero seven, including Chernobyl and Fukushima. Hold on. Zero point zero seven, even counting Chernobyl? That sounds too low. It's the mainstream figure, but you're right to push. It sits at one end of a genuinely disputed range. At Chernobyl, fewer than a hundred deaths are directly confirmed. The World Health Organization projects up to four thousand eventual. Greenpeace, using broader modeling, estimated tens of thousands. So the experts don't even agree on the number. They don't, and we won't pretend they do. At Fukushima, radiation is linked to a single death. But the evacuation itself, to around two thousand two hundred. A policy failure, not a radiation one. Even counted most cautiously, nuclear lands among the safest sources we have. That part isn't the debate.

Research 3 (settled)

The other settled fact cuts the other way. In the West, building new reactors has been a financial disaster. Georgia's Vogtle plant: an estimated fourteen billion dollars became about thirty-five, seven years late. Britain's Hinkley Point C: from eighteen billion pounds toward fifty. France's Flamanville: from three billion euros to over thirteen, twelve years late. That's three for three. Is it just the West that can't build these? That's exactly the right question. Because elsewhere, the same reactors tell a different story. South Korea's Barakah built its fourth unit for about forty percent of the first's cost. China connects reactors in roughly six years, versus nine worldwide. So it's not the technology. It's who's building it. That's the heart of the whole debate. And by Lazard's twenty twenty-five numbers, new Western nuclear runs up to two hundred twenty dollars a megawatt hour, against as little as thirty-seven for wind. Though those figures don't price in backup for when the wind stops. Is there any cheap nuclear, then? One kind. Not building new at all, just extending an existing plant. Thirty to fifty dollars a megawatt hour. Cheaper than building anything.

Research 4

Two more things before the debate, because both cut against easy answers. First, the key word in the original question. Baseload. The always-on power nuclear is supposed to provide. That word's not controversial, surely? It is, actually. Researchers at NYU argue baseload describes a level of demand, not a type of plant. The label quietly tilts the debate toward always-on generation before it even starts. Second: waste. You've heard it's an unsolved problem. The truth is more interesting. In Finland, a repository called Onkalo is on the verge of burying spent fuel in bedrock, engineered to last a hundred thousand years. So it is solved? The technology is. The politics aren't. America spent over twelve billion dollars on Yucca Mountain, then abandoned it for political, not scientific, reasons. Seventy thousand tonnes of waste now sit in casks at seventy-six sites, waiting. Solved, and unsolved, turn out to be answers to two different questions.

That's not fifty-fifty, and I'm not going to stage a fake fight over it. And those blown budgets in the West? Also not up for debate. Both AI teams had to accept the settled facts. Neither one gets to fight the data. Here's the case for.

Debate — Green

Nuclear's case doesn't rest on emissions or safety. Both already favor it. That's settled ground. It rests on a harder question. What covers the grid during the still, cloudless week in January, when wind and sun fall short for days at a time? Modeling from MIT and Princeton, across a thousand scenarios, found that adding firm, always-on capacity to a renewables-heavy grid cuts total system cost by ten to as much as sixty-two percent. Now, honestly, that finding covers any firm clean source, not nuclear specifically. But of the options on that menu, nuclear is the only one already running at hundreds of gigawatts worldwide. Carbon capture and giant batteries are still mostly promises. Yes, Vogtle, Hinkley, and Flamanville were disasters. This case doesn't dodge that. But look closer: that's a Western, first-build-in-a-generation problem. Korea and China build the same reactors again and again, faster and cheaper each time. The variable isn't the technology. It's whether you kept building. And the cheapest firm clean power on the grid today? It's the reactors already running. Extending them costs thirty to fifty dollars a megawatt hour, less than building anything new. Even the biggest tech companies are signing decades-long contracts for firm nuclear power, betting their own money on it. Fix the build. Don't abandon the tool.

Debate — Gold responds

But notice what that study actually says. It says firm, not nuclear. And the cheapest firm nuclear is the fleet already running, which nobody's proposing to shut down. The real dispute is new construction. Korea and China do build cheaply, after decades of continuous building the West simply doesn't have. And America's one serious attempt to restart that assembly line, the NuScale small-reactor project, was cancelled when its cost more than doubled, from four billion dollars to over nine. Rebuilding that whole industrial base would itself take decades. And the climate clock is already running.

Debate — Gold

The case against centering our plans on new nuclear starts with the receipts. And the receipts are consistent. Three countries, three different systems, the same result. Vogtle: two and a half times over budget. Hinkley: climbing toward fifty billion pounds. Flamanville: twelve years late. Three-for-three isn't bad luck. It's a pattern. And speed isn't a side issue. It's the whole argument, because climate is a clock. Wind and solar go from decision to power in one to four years. New Western nuclear? Around eight on average, and up to seventeen for Europe's Olkiluoto plant. Every year spent waiting for a reactor is a year not spent cutting emissions. Fast, imperfect capacity beats perfect capacity that arrives too late. Then there's the mental model. A nuclear plant can't easily throttle up and down to follow a grid full of wind and solar. And researchers modeling fully-renewable systems find them feasible, and cheaper, by twenty fifty. And the fix nuclear keeps promising, cheap, small, modular reactors, hasn't arrived. The furthest-along American project was cancelled in twenty twenty-three. Today, not one commercial small reactor is running in the United States. Storage, wind, solar, and transmission aren't a consolation prize. For most of a clean grid, they're the cheaper, faster, already-arriving answer.

Debate — Green responds

The cost-and-speed case is real, for the majority of the grid both sides already give to renewables. But it doesn't reach the contested ground. Those multi-day winter gaps? You can't close them by building more of the very thing that went dark. Adding faster wind and solar doesn't make wind and solar firm. And the alternatives offered there, carbon capture, long-duration storage, are actually less proven at scale than nuclear is. There are only a handful of carbon-capture power plants on Earth. The fastest, cheapest firm clean power still requires no new construction at all: just keep today's reactors running. Both sides, quietly, agree on that.

Verdict — judge

So where does that leave us? Start with what's not in dispute. Nuclear is about as clean and as safe as wind. That's settled. It's also settled that recent Western reactor projects were budget disasters, while Korea and China build the same machines far faster and cheaper. Both sides accepted all of it. The real disagreement is narrower than the shouting suggests, and more interesting. Both sides agree renewables and storage will carry most of a clean grid. The fight is over the hard last stretch: those still, dark weeks when wind and sun underdeliver for days. The case for says firmness has real, measurable system value, and nuclear is the only clean version already running at scale, though its claim that carbon capture and long-duration storage are less proven is an extrapolation, not a settled fact. The case against says the West's cost and speed record makes new nuclear the riskiest, slowest way to buy that firmness, though its warning that rebuilding the industry takes decades is a projection, not a certainty. And notice what both sides quietly agreed on, without ever meaning to: keeping today's reactors running is the cheapest firm clean power there is. When two opponents reach for the same fact, pay attention to it. So here's the charge, and you're the jury. Remember where we started: half this argument was already over before the shouting began. That half is still settled, nothing there to deliberate. But the real question, the one even the experts split on, is yours to weigh. You've heard both cases. Now you decide.
