Nuclear Power: Should It Anchor Our Clean-Energy Future?

Should nuclear be a centerpiece of clean-energy plans, given its real cost record?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in August 2026, demand for power was surging for AI data centers. Microsoft was restarting Three Mile Island, Amazon had bought into a Pennsylvania plant, and Google and Meta had signed for reactors not yet built.

Settled

What both sides accept

Nuclear is about as clean and as safe as wind, and recent Western reactor projects were budget disasters while Korea and China build the same machines far faster and cheaper. Both sides agreed renewables and storage will carry most of a clean grid. And both reached for the same fact: keeping existing reactors running is the cheapest firm clean power there is.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesThe Case For

A clean grid still needs power for the still, cloudless January week when wind and sun fall short for days. Modeling from MIT and Princeton found that adding firm, always-on power cuts total system cost by 10 to 62%, and nuclear is the only firm clean source already running at scale. Korea and China build the same reactors cheaper.

Gold arguesThe Case Against

Vogtle, Hinkley Point C and Flamanville all ran far over budget or years late: three countries, one pattern. Climate is a clock: wind and solar take one to four years, new Western nuclear around eight on average. And small reactors haven't arrived: NuScale was cancelled when its cost more than doubled, from $4 billion to over $9 billion.

What the shouting gets wrong

The loud questions, is it clean and is it safe, are already settled. The real split is over the hard last stretch: what powers the grid through still, dark weeks when wind and sun underdeliver for days. Experts with real models disagree there, the verdict left it to you, and nearly everything settled against nuclear here is a Western story.

The verdict

Read the verdict as it aired

The judge So where does that leave us? Start with what's not in dispute. Nuclear is about as clean and as safe as wind. That's settled. It's also settled that recent Western reactor projects were budget disasters, while Korea and China build the same machines far faster and cheaper. Both sides accepted all of it. The real disagreement is narrower than the shouting suggests, and more interesting. Both sides agree renewables and storage will carry most of a clean grid. The fight is over the hard last stretch: those still, dark weeks when wind and sun underdeliver for days. The case for says firmness has real, measurable system value, and nuclear is the only clean version already running at scale, though its claim that carbon capture and long-duration storage are less proven is an extrapolation, not a settled fact. The case against says the West's cost and speed record makes new nuclear the riskiest, slowest way to buy that firmness, though its warning that rebuilding the industry takes decades is a projection, not a certainty. And notice what both sides quietly agreed on, without ever meaning to: keeping today's reactors running is the cheapest firm clean power there is. When two opponents reach for the same fact, pay attention to it. So here's the charge, and you're the jury. Remember where we started: half this argument was already over before the shouting began. That half is still settled, nothing there to deliberate. But the real question, the one even the experts split on, is yours to weigh. You've heard both cases. Now you decide.

The judge named no sides on this one, so there's no dial: we don't invent positions.

What our fact-check caught

Our drafts got these wrong before they aired. Every correction is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidReactor licences have already been extended to 80 years.

    The record saysThe source only says regulators can grant 80-year extensions. That isn't the same as having done it, so the claim was dropped.

  2. Draft saidFirm power cuts the cost of a clean grid by 10 to 60%.

    The record saysThe study found 10 to 62%, and it covers any firm clean source, not nuclear specifically.

  3. Draft saidA Western reactor takes 8 to 17 years to build.

    The record saysEight years is the worldwide average, including fast builds in Korea and China. Seventeen is one European plant, Olkiluoto.
