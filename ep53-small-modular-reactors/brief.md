## The pitch, in one line

**"On September 28 the US regulator approved what it called the nation's first commercial small modular
reactor, at Clinch River in Tennessee. The permit's earliest completion date is October 1, 2031; the owner
has given the regulator its own estimate of what it will cost and kept the figure confidential; and the
only North American owner that has published one, in Ontario, budgets 7.7 billion Canadian dollars for its
first 300 megawatts and the infrastructure its later units will share. Even the nuclear agencies' own
tables say the first ones are the expensive ones. So is this the start of a fleet, or a permit with its
price blacked out?"** (C1, C6; T1-7; A1; A23, A6.) The licensing clock is real (C3) and the peg reactor uses ordinary fuel
(D1), which is the supporters' strongest ground; the build record abroad ran years late (B5, B9, B13,
B19), which is the critics'.

## The question

**Are small modular reactors a real breakthrough or hype?** The debate it stages, as the record shapes it:
**the permits are real and the prices aren't known yet; will the first expensive ones buy a cheaper fleet,
and who pays while we find out?** (Working side labels below.)

## Topic shape (the guardrail verdict)

**Lopsided on the build record and on today's price; genuinely two-sided on whether a series will bring the
price down, on whether the grid needs what these plants sell, and on who should carry the risk of the
first ones.**

- **Lopsided: "has an SMR been built on time and on budget, anywhere?"** The record has none. The two
  SMR-class power plants running anywhere in it took about 13 years (Akademik Lomonosov, 2007–2020: B5)
  and about 11 years (HTR-PM, 2012–2023: B9) from start of construction to commercial operation, each
  years past its stated date (B6, B12). China's Linglong One planned start-up by May 31, 2025 and had not
  loaded fuel on October 22, 2026 (B13, B19). Argentina's CAREM-25 halted in September 2024 (B20). In
  North America, Darlington's first unit has slipped from "as early as 2028" to grid connection by the end
  of 2030 before any reactor building exists (B2, B3), and Natrium from 2028 to completion in 2030 (B22,
  B23). **The verdict says plainly: no SMR in the record has yet been built on its first stated schedule.**
  It never says "SMRs can't be built": the operating examples are state demonstrators under very
  different regimes (B pass trap 9), and Darlington is on its budget so far by its owner's word (A2).
- **Lopsided: "is it cheaper today?"** On the agencies' own like-for-like tables, no. The OECD Nuclear
  Energy Agency's 2025 medians put a near-term SMR at $146/MWh against $98 for a gas combined-cycle plant,
  $94 for solar with batteries and $59 for onshore wind, all in the same study (A23; W3). The US EIA's
  overnight cost for a generic SMR is $8,936/kW against $868 for a combined-cycle gas plant (2023 dollars,
  A33). The vendor publishes no price (A32); TVA's own plan assumes $17,263/kW for a first-of-a-kind unit
  (2026 dollars, overnight, before tax credits: T1-3), and its project estimate is withheld (T1-7).
- **Genuinely two-sided (1): will the tenth one be cheap?** The same NEA table puts an nth-of-a-kind SMR
  at $84/MWh, below its gas and solar-plus-battery figures (A23; W3), but only "should the NOAK promise be
  realised", which needs "standardised designs and repeat construction" (W9). OPG projects its fourth unit
  about a third cheaper than its first (A6, a projection). Against that: US nuclear costs rose, not fell,
  from early plants to later ones (A14, A15), a finding contested by a 2016 study and its critics (A17,
  A18); the SMR learning rates in the cost models are assumptions (A24), and no factory-built SMR learning
  data exist in the record (A pass trap 12).
- **Genuinely two-sided (2): does the grid need what it sells?** Demand forecasts rose sharply (NERC:
  over 224 GW of summer-peak growth in ten years, E28; PJM: +96.7 GW by 2046, E27), but grid planners are
  discounting speculative data-centre load (E27, E28). System models find firm low-carbon power lowers
  the cost of deep decarbonisation (E17, E18, E19), and threshold studies find nuclear competitive only
  in deep decarbonisation or at much lower costs (A25, A26).
- **Not a question of evidence: who should carry first-of-a-kind risk?** Bill-payers (Ontario, A4;
  Indiana, E24), taxpayers (E8, E11, E15), the tech buyers (E1–E7) or nobody until one runs (A12, E26).
  The episode says this is a judgement and hands it over.

**Rejected framings.** "Is it safe?" as the spine: there is no incident record to argue from, the
regulator's advisory committee supports the permit (C12), and the critics' filings are about process and
rules, not a documented failure (C21, C27); it is one beat. "HALEU will strangle SMRs": wrong for the peg
reactor, which uses ordinary fuel (D1, D2, D3); it is a beat about the advanced designs. "Is the regulator
the bottleneck?": the record settles it has sped up (C3, C6); whether it sped up safely is the critics'
point, made with attribution (C21–C27), and it is a beat.

## The spine (tested against the record)

**Candidate (main session): "permits and promises vs steel and prices."** Verdict: **it holds as the
set-up, and the record forces one refinement for the debate itself: "prices" splits in two (what it costs
to build, and what the power will cost), and the contested hinge is not whether the first ones are
expensive (both sides' own sources say they are) but whether they buy a cheaper fleet, and who pays while
we find out.** Proposed on-air form: **"The permit's real. Is the price?"**, with the debate's hinge line
**"The first one or the tenth one?"** ✎ After top-up T1 the spine still holds, with one sharpening: the
price is not missing, it is **filed and hidden**. TVA gave the regulator its own estimate and withheld the
figure (T1-7), while its public plan assumes nearly twice the top of the generic range its permit paperwork
borrowed (T1-3, T1-6, W14). "Is the price?" now asks whether the public gets to see it before it pays.

What supports the set-up:
- **Permits:** Clinch River, about 14.6 months from docketing (C3), no intervenors (C7), the Advisory
  Committee "supports issuance" (C12); Kemmerer approved in March (B24, C8); Darlington licensed to
  construct in April 2025, first hold point lifted March 2026 (B1).
- **Promises:** Amazon's X-energy "5 GW" is priority rights, "not obligated to purchase" (E1); Indiana is
  a feasibility study (E6); Oklo's customers are "prospective" (E3); Meta's 6.6 GW is "up to", new and
  existing, about 2.5 GW of it existing plants and uprates (E4; W2); DOE's $400 million for TVA was a
  selection to negotiate (A29). Worldwide, Wood Mackenzie counted 2.5 GW under construction or in
  development against a 47 GW announced pipeline in early 2025 (B34).
- **Steel:** Darlington placed its basemat in April 2026 and its first reactor-building level is due at
  the end of 2026 (B2); Natrium's nuclear construction began April 23, 2026 (B25); its lead contractor left
  in September (B26); no SMR in the record runs in North America (B2, B23, C1).
- **Prices (to build):** the only owner-published SMR budget in North America is OPG's C$7.7 billion for
  unit 1 and shared infrastructure, about C$25,700 per kW (A1, our arithmetic); TVA filed its Clinch River
  estimate with the NRC and withheld it as proprietary, publishing only a planning assumption of $17,263/kW
  in 2026 dollars for a generic first unit (T1-3, T1-7; never set beside the Canadian-dollar figure); the
  vendor's own spec sheet leaves the cost row blank (A32); the one fully costed US project rose
  to $9.3 billion including financing for 462 MW before it ended (A9, A11).
- **Prices (of power):** no SMR power price is public, from any buyer (E pass trap 1).

The supporters' answer, built from the record (equal weight):
- **The permit clock is the breakthrough.** 14.6 months against Vogtle's 44.4 (C3, C9; not like-for-like:
  Vogtle was a two-unit construct-and-operate licence), with no petitions (C7) and the safety committee's
  support (C12).
- **The peg reactor doesn't need the scarce fuel.** BWRX-300s at Clinch River and Darlington run on
  uranium dioxide under 5% enriched (D1, D2).
- **Ontario is building, on budget so far.** C$1.885 billion of C$7.7 billion spent by December 2025, the
  budget unchanged in March 2026 (A2); basemat placed (B2); the same owner's refurbishment of the old
  Darlington units came in about C$150 million under its C$12.8 billion budget, ahead of schedule (A7, a
  different project).
- **The first is meant to cost more.** OPG's per-unit estimates fall to C$4.1 billion for unit 4 (A6);
  the NEA's nth-of-a-kind SMR is $84/MWh (A23).
- **The grid needs firm power.** NERC: 13 of 23 areas face adequacy challenges in the next ten years (E28).

**Why not a different spine.** "Safe or not?" has no incident record to argue (above). "Fuel shortage"
misfires on the peg reactor (D1). "Data centres will pay" is a beat, not a spine: the deals are options
(E1–E3). "Permits vs prices" is the one both teams must answer, and "who pays if it runs over" is where the
viewer with a power bill lives.

## What an SMR is: the primer, early (his ruling 2026-11-02; top-up T5)

_His words: "what these reactors are and how they are different … changes the scope of this vs the usual
nuclear good vs bad debate." It goes right after the cold open, before the first beat, about 30 to 45
seconds._

- **Size:** "small" is up to 300 MW of electricity per module by the IAEA's line (T5-1); the US regulator
  draws its line in heat (1,000 MWt) and DOE gives only a range (T5-2, T5-3). Every US reactor running is
  bigger: the smallest is about 593 MW, and one Vogtle unit (1,114 MW) is nearly four BWRX-300s (T5-4,
  T5-5).
- **The peg reactor is a smaller version of a familiar kind, not new physics.** The NRC calls it a "small
  modular boiling light-water reactor" (T5-6); it descends from a big certified design, the ESBWR, never
  built in the US (T5-7, T5-8), and burns the same low-enriched fuel as today's plants (T5-9). Boiling-water
  reactors have run in the US since 1960, and small reactors aren't new either (Shippingport, about 60 MW)
  (T5-21, T5-22).
- **What IS different:** its size; no pumps to push the water round (natural circulation at this scale);
  and simplified safety systems that cool the core for 72 hours with no power or operators, the NRC's
  figure (T5-8, T5-11; "seven days" is the vendor's, T5-12). The safety advisers list natural circulation
  at this scale as a "validation data gap" for the operating licence (T5-13). Passive cooling itself isn't
  new: Vogtle's AP1000s have it (T5-24).
- **The genuinely new types are the others:** Natrium in Wyoming (sodium-cooled, 345 MW: an SMR by the US
  regulator's measure, not the IAEA's), X-energy's Xe-100 (helium gas, pebble fuel; Indiana's design and
  Dow's in Texas) and Kairos's Hermes (molten salt; a test reactor, not a power plant) (T5-16 to T5-20).
- **Two corrected claims:** never "can't melt down" or "walk-away safe" (T5-14, CORRECTED: the design has a
  core catcher; severe accidents are reviewed at the operating licence, T5-15); never "built in a factory"
  for the BWRX-300: "large prefabricated pieces, assembled on site" (T5-27, CORRECTED). The smaller
  emergency zone is optional and Clinch River's isn't chosen yet (T5-25, T5-26).
- **Why this changes the debate (T5's synthesis, not a quote):** this isn't "is nuclear safe?". The bets
  are narrower and testable: that small, simpler and repeated makes each reactor cheaper and faster; that a
  smaller zone lets reactors sit on old coal sites; and that the new-fuel designs can get their fuel. Gold
  and Green fight over those, not over nuclear itself.
- **Sayable primer** (each sentence tagged): "Every reactor running in America today is big: the newest, at
  Plant Vogtle in Georgia, is about eleven hundred megawatts. [T5-4, T5-5] A small modular reactor is, by
  the international definition, three hundred megawatts or less, so one Vogtle reactor is nearly four of
  the one the regulator just approved for construction in Tennessee. [T5-1, T5-5, T5-6] That Tennessee
  reactor isn't exotic: it's a smaller boiling-water reactor, the same water-cooled kind America has run
  since 1960, burning the same fuel. [T5-6, T5-9, T5-22] What's different is how it's simplified: no pumps
  to push the water round, and safety systems that cool the core for about three days with no power and no
  operators. [T5-8, T5-11] Some other small reactors really are new: one cooled by liquid sodium in
  Wyoming, one by helium gas in Texas and Indiana, a test reactor cooled by molten salt in Tennessee.
  [T5-16, T5-18, T5-19, T5-20] So this isn't the old fight about whether nuclear is safe. It's about
  whether smaller, simpler and repeated can finally make nuclear cheap and fast to build." [T5 synthesis]

## Contested (the real fight)

**Proposed side labels (data-driven, not pro/con; a pitch question):**
- **Team Green — "A permit isn't a power plant."** The paperwork moved; the steel and the price didn't.
  Every SMR in the record ran late, the only fully costed US one ended, and the owner of Clinch River gave
  the regulator its estimate and kept it from the public. Until one runs at a known price, it's a promise,
  and somebody's bill is underwriting it.
- **Team Gold — "The first one is the down payment."** The regulator is no longer the wall, the peg
  reactor uses ordinary fuel, Ontario is building on budget so far, and the grid needs firm low-carbon
  power. First units always cost more; the agencies' own tables say a series gets cheap. Somebody has to
  build the first ones.
- **Why these labels:** both name the same object (the first unit), so the clash is about one thing the
  viewer can picture. Neither asks its team to fight a settled fact: Gold isn't asked to claim an SMR has
  been built on time (none has, in the record), and Green isn't asked to claim the licensing or the fuel
  for the peg reactor is a problem (the record says it isn't).

### Team Green — "A permit isn't a power plant" (steelmanned)

1. **Paper moved; steel didn't.** Clinch River: earliest completion October 2031, operation needs another
   licence, and TVA's board had not decided to build as of its March 2026 report (C1, C2, E9). Darlington:
   two years later than its own first target before a reactor building exists (B2, B3). Natrium: slipped
   on fuel, lost its lead contractor (B22, B26, B27).
2. **The plants that run took over a decade.** Lomonosov about 13 years and a decade late, its cost
   reportedly up more than 300% in roubles by 2015 (B5, B6); HTR-PM about 11 years, rated below design,
   often unavailable, at about twice its expected cost in a 2016 estimate (B9, B10, B12); Linglong One past
   its 2019 start-up date and still not fuelled (B13, B19); CAREM halted (B20).
3. **The price, where anyone has shown one, is high.** C$7.7 billion for Darlington's first 300 MW (A1);
   $9.3 billion with financing for NuScale's 462 MW (A9). On the agencies' own like-for-like tables, a
   near-term SMR costs about one and a half times a gas plant per MWh (NEA, A23, W3). Per kW to build,
   EIA's 2023-dollar table puts a generic SMR at about ten times its gas-plant figure, but that gas figure
   predates today's turbine prices: on Lazard's $2,400–2,600/kW turbine quotes the gap is about 3.6 times,
   and against a large reactor in EIA's same table the SMR is about 14% more per kW (A33, A22, W4; our
   arithmetic, W11). The vendor won't print a number (A32). TVA filed its Clinch River estimate with the
   regulator and withheld it as proprietary (T1-7); its own planners assume $17,263/kW for a first unit,
   in 2026 dollars, "all-in costs plus risk contingencies", nearly twice the top of the generic range its
   permit paperwork borrowed (T1-3, T1-6, W14). Even the owner assumes it's expensive. (Gold's reading of
   the same line: TVA priced the risk in.)
4. **"It'll get cheaper" hasn't happened in US nuclear.** Later US plants cost more than early ones (A14);
   nuclear's learning rates run from negative to 6% (A15); shrinking NuScale's plant from 8 to 6 modules
   raised its price because costs "do not decrease proportionally" (A10). The SMR learning rate in the INL
   model is an assumption (A24).
5. **The promises are options.** Amazon "not obligated to purchase" (E1); Dow can walk away "for
   convenience" (E2); Oklo's buyers are "prospective" (E3); Indiana is a feasibility study whose own risk
   list includes not going ahead (E6, peg); about 2.5 of 47 GW announced worldwide was under construction or
   in development (B34); about 30 US reactors applied for in 2007–09, two built (B38).
6. **The bill-payer is on the hook first.** Ontario's customers pay interest before service (A4); Indiana's
   law recovers 80% of development costs through rates (E24), which consumer advocates say "puts the
   financial cost... on ratepayers" (E25); NuScale's Utah cities each spent hundreds of thousands before the
   project ended (A12). The spent fuel has nowhere to go, and taxpayers have paid $11.1 billion for that
   already (D8, D9).
7. **The advanced designs need fuel the US barely makes.** HALEU: one demonstration cascade at 900 kg (as UF6) a
   year, now winding down, against more than 40 tonnes DOE says is needed by 2030 (D12, D13); Natrium already lost two years to it (B22); Indiana's Xe-100 needs it (D5).
8. **The rules are being rewritten as fast as the permits.** Eleven state attorneys general urged
   withdrawal of the July proposal, warning offsite plans could become "coordination descriptions" (C21);
   the Union of Concerned Scientists says security inspection resources were cut by over half in some
   cases (C27, advocacy, attributed); the regulator lost about 500 staff on net (C22).

**Green's limits, stated:** Ontario's budget is unchanged to March 2026 by its owner's word (A2), and the
same owner delivered its refurbishment under budget (A7). Lomonosov's low output is mostly a small town's
demand, not breakdowns (B8). The operating plants are state demonstrators that can't settle a Western
series cost (B pass trap 9). A big announced-vs-built gap is normal in energy: 14% of capacity entering US
interconnection queues 2000–18 reached operation (B35), about 14% of CCS projects by count operate (B37).
The peg reactor doesn't need HALEU (D1). GAO and the Inspector General found staffing problems, not worse
reviews (C22, C23); the critics' regulatory claims are attributed views (C21, C27). No court challenge to
the 2026 rules was found (C14, UNRESOLVED: off air as a claim).

### Team Gold — "The first one is the down payment" (steelmanned)

1. **The regulator is no longer the wall.** About 14.6 months from docketing to permit (C3; the NRC's own
   "14 months, ahead of schedule", C6), against about 44 for Vogtle (C9); no petitions (C7); the safety
   committee "supports issuance" (C12); contested cases due to settle in about a year once the new rule takes effect in December (C19, C20); test-reactor data can
   feed commercial reviews from November 25 (C18).
2. **The peg reactor runs on ordinary fuel.** Under 5% enriched uranium dioxide, the GNF2 assembly type
   already in use (D1, D2); NuScale's US460 too (D3). The HALEU problem belongs to the advanced designs,
   not to Clinch River.
3. **Ontario is building, and has built.** C$1.885 billion spent of C$7.7 billion by December 2025, budget
   unchanged (A2); licence, first hold point lifted, operating-licence application filed (B1); basemat placed
   (B2). The same owner's refurbishment: about C$150 million under its C$12.8 billion budget, ahead of
   schedule (A7, a different project).
4. **The first one is supposed to cost more.** OPG's estimates fall unit by unit to C$4.1 billion for unit 4,
   about a third cheaper than unit 1 (A6, a projection); in the NEA's own table an nth-of-a-kind SMR comes in
   at $84/MWh, below its gas combined-cycle ($98) and solar-plus-battery ($94) figures (A23, W3); INL's
   meta-analysis expects SMR overnight costs to fall by 2050 (A24); Vogtle's second unit was cheaper than its
   first by DOE's account (A19, data inconclusive per INL). A 2016 study of 349 reactors found "no inherent
   cost escalation trend", with South Korea's costs falling (A17).
5. **The grid needs firm power.** NERC: over 224 GW of peak growth in ten years, 13 of 23 areas facing
   adequacy challenges, and utility plans in one region listing SMRs among new resources (E28); PJM's peak
   +96.7 GW by 2046 (E27).
6. **Firm low-carbon power lowers the cost of the whole system.** In nearly 1,000 modelled cases, firm
   low-carbon resources (nuclear, gas with carbon capture, bioenergy) cut fully decarbonised system costs by
   10–62% (E17); MIT finds deep-decarbonisation costs rise "significantly" without nuclear (E18); a 2021 study
   cited in DOE's draft found California 2045 costs of $80–94/MWh with nuclear against $129–150 without
   (E19). Lazard says wind and solar plus firming is "not... a 24/7 firm resource on a single project site"
   (A22).
7. **Smaller plants are smaller bets.** DOE's draft: large reactors are cheaper per kW, but SMRs "may offer
   smaller overall project costs" (A20); MIT's projection puts a phased SMR build at about $6 billion at risk
   up front against $20–30 billion for a pair of AP1000s (T3-21, 2024 USD; attribute it). Vogtle's two units
   cost about $35 billion (A19, mixed US dollars);
   Darlington's first unit is budgeted at C$7.7 billion (A1, Canadian dollars) (never a per-kW comparison).
8. **The people who want the power are paying to start it.** Amazon funds Indiana's development and would be
   the first buyer (E7); Meta funds two Natrium units (E4); the Army pays only on milestones (E15). Old coal
   sites: 80% of those evaluated could host a reactor, at a 15–35% saving on overnight capital in one case
   study (E20), with more operating jobs than a same-size coal plant (E21).
9. **Built, this technology runs.** The US nuclear fleet ran at 91.1% in 2025 (E29: large reactors, not
   SMRs).

**Gold's limits, stated:** the first-of-a-kind premium is real and large (A1, A23); the nth-of-a-kind
price is conditional on standardised designs and repeat construction (W9) and on learning rates that are
assumptions (A24); the 2016 "no escalation" finding is disputed (A18). The deals are not binding (E1–E3).
Demand is partly speculative by the grid planners' own account (E27, E28). The system models are about
"firm low-carbon" resources generally, not SMRs (E17, E18), and only their abstracts or summaries were read;
threshold studies find nuclear competitive only beyond about 80% emission cuts or at much lower costs (A25,
A26). Indiana's own Xe-100 needs HALEU (D5). Darlington's on-budget claim rests on its owner's word and has
no 2026 primary after March (A2, A3, B4). The refurbishment is a different project (A7). The 91% is the big
fleet (E29). On carbon, an SMR is about as clean as a big reactor, not cleaner (T2-9, T2-10), so "clean"
is a case for nuclear, not for small nuclear.

### The two loudest rebuttals, as Green/Gold exchanges (top-up T3)

The council's harvest found two viewer rebuttals with no row in the five hand passes: the Navy (about 65
comments) and "just build the proven big one" (about 30). Each is staged as an exchange; neither is a side.

**1. "The Navy has run small reactors for decades. What's the problem?"**
- **Gold opens:** the Navy's own count: more than 7,600 reactor-years and 97 reactors running, as of
  December 2024, and, the Navy says, never a reactor accident (T3-1, T3-2). Small reactors work.
- **Green answers:** the Navy proves small reactors can be safe; it doesn't prove they can be cheap. Its
  fuel is 93% enriched, a level no commercial fleet can use, and the Navy itself rejected a 5% core as
  "infeasible and cost prohibitive" (T3-4). It builds to battle-shock and quiet standards, heavy parts take
  4–8 years (T3-6, T3-7), it is its own overseer (T3-3), and it weighs the warship's cost, not the price of
  a megawatt-hour (T3-5, T3-8). No one has published what a naval reactor costs (T3-9).
- **Gold's comeback:** then take the recipe, not the reactor. A few standard designs, one technical
  authority, repeat suppliers and trained crews is the Navy's own account of its success, and it is exactly
  the SMR plan (T3-11); today's commercial reactors already descend from the Navy's Shippingport (T3-10).
- **Where it lands:** the analogy holds on how to build and fails on what it costs. The honest line: "the
  Navy was never buying electricity."

**2. "Why not just build more of the proven big reactor?"**
- **Green opens (with the pro-nuclear critics):** per kilowatt, big is cheaper. MIT puts the next two
  AP1000s at about $8,300/kW (2024 dollars, overnight, owner's cost included), and finds an SMR's
  mature-series cost still about 45% above an AP1000's (T3-21); DOE's draft says the same direction (A20);
  in EIA's own table a generic SMR costs about 14% more per kW than two AP1000s (A33, W11).
- **Gold answers:** the size of the bet. Vogtle 3 and 4 cost about $35 billion by independent estimates,
  financing included (T3-12, A19); Georgia Power's customers carry $7.562 billion of construction cost plus
  financing (T3-13), and South Carolina's are still paying for two AP1000s that were abandoned, over 20
  years (T3-16). A phased SMR programme puts about $6 billion at risk at a time against $20–30 billion for a
  pair of AP1000s (MIT's projection, T3-21).
- **Green's comeback:** and nobody is waiting for SMRs to try: the administration has a goal of 10 large
  reactors under construction by 2030 (T3-18), and the offers are already on the table: a framework for
  "at least $80 billion" of Westinghouse reactors and $17.5 billion of conditional loans for ten AP1000s
  (T3-19, T3-20).
- **Where it lands:** the government is backing both, and not one new big reactor is ordered either
  (T3-19, T3-20). The independent analysis leans large on price per kW; the small side's real argument is
  how much a utility, and its customers, can lose on one project.

### Where the record leans, said plainly (no false balance)

On **what has happened**, the record leans toward Green. No SMR in the record has yet been built on its
first stated schedule; the two SMR-class power plants running anywhere took 11 to 13 years and ran years
late (B5, B6, B9, B12); the one fully costed US project rose in price and ended (A9, A11); the only
owner-published North American budget is high per kilowatt (A1); the owner of the plant the regulator just
approved filed its estimate and withheld it, while its own plan assumes about $17,000 a kilowatt (2026
dollars) for a first unit (T1-3, T1-7); and on the agencies' own tables a first SMR's power costs more than
gas or solar-plus-batteries (A23). On **licensing and fuel for the peg reactor**, the record leans toward
Gold: the review was fast and uncontested (C3, C7), the safety committee supports it (C12), and it uses
ordinary fuel (D1). The record does **not** settle the question Gold leans on hardest: whether a series
will bring the price down (NEA's nth-of-a-kind $84 is conditional, A23, W9; OPG's falling unit costs are
projections, A6; US history points the other way, A14, A15, and is disputed, A17, A18). Nor does it settle
whether the grid needs firm nuclear power badly enough to pay a first-of-a-kind premium: the models split
by how deep the decarbonisation goal is (E17–E19 against A25, A26), and demand forecasts are rising and
being discounted at the same time (E27, E28). On **"cleaner"**, the record settles that nuclear's life-cycle
carbon is about wind's and far below gas's (T2-1), and that small reactors come out about the same as big
ones, not cleaner (T2-9, T2-10). On **"why not big reactors?"**, the independent analysis leans large on
price per kW and small on the size of the bet (T3-21). Who should carry the risk is a judgement, not a
finding. The episode says the lean once, plainly, and does not pretend the future price is known.

## The reframe (tested against the record)

**As written:** *The fight sounds like "is this new technology real?" For the plant the regulator just
approved, the record says the technology is the familiar part: a water-cooled reactor on ordinary fuel,
licensed fast. What nobody has shown is the business: building the first few on time, at a price, and
getting the next ones cheaper. So the question isn't "breakthrough or hype?" It's "the first one, or the
tenth one?", and who pays for the first one while we find out.*

**Test 1, "the technology is the familiar part": holds for the BWRX-300, not for every SMR.**
- For it: ordinary low-enriched fuel of a type in use today (D1, D2); licensed in about 14.6 months (C3);
  the safety committee supports it (C12).
- Limit: the committee left real questions for the operating licence (C12); the advanced designs (Natrium,
  Xe-100, Kairos) use new fuels and coolants (D4–D6). Say "for the reactor approved in Tennessee", never
  "SMR technology is proven".

**Test 2, "nobody has shown the business": holds.**
- For it: no SMR built on its first schedule in the record (B2, B3, B5, B9, B13, B19); TVA's estimate
  withheld (T1-7), only a planning assumption public (T1-3); no vendor price (A32); no buyer's power price
  (E pass trap 1).
- Limit: Darlington is on budget so far by its owner's word (A2). Say "not yet shown", not "failed".

**Test 3, "the first one or the tenth one": holds, and it is the agencies' own framing.**
- For it: NEA near-term SMR $146 vs nth-of-a-kind $84/MWh (A23); its sub-$100 round-the-clock figure holds
  only "should the NOAK promise be realised" (W9); OPG's unit-4 estimate about a third below unit 1 (A6);
  INL's first-build SMR costs above its 2050 costs (A24).
- Limit: nth-of-a-kind figures are projections; the NEA's dollar-year isn't stated in the sections read
  (A23); no factory-built SMR learning data exist (A pass trap 12).

## Verdict framing

**No winner.** The judge says what the record settles (the permit is real and fast; the peg reactor uses
ordinary fuel; no SMR in the record has yet been built on its first stated schedule; the first ones cost
more, by the agencies' own tables and by TVA's own planning figure; Clinch River's price is filed with the
regulator and withheld from the public; an SMR is about as clean over its life as a big reactor, not
cleaner; the deals are options) and
hands the viewer **one dial: "What should have to be true before your power bill helps pay for a small
reactor?"** It runs from Green to Gold. Every setting has a real institution behind it and a documented
failure. The judge never sets it. (A proposal; settings and order are pitch questions.)

1. **"Show me one running."** (Green, full.) Nobody commits until a first unit runs at a known cost. Behind
   it: the Utah cities in NuScale's project, who had negotiated off-ramps to quit before issuing bonds and
   used them (E26, A11, A12). Documented failure: nobody goes first: the project ended for lack of
   subscribers (A11), and TVA, the next US owner to get a permit, has kept its estimate from the public
   (T1-7).
2. **"The buyers carry it."** The companies that want the power fund the first plants. Behind it: Amazon's
   development funding in Indiana and its ratepayer pledge (E6, E7); Meta's funding of two Natrium units
   (E4). Documented failure: the deals so far are options and intentions: Amazon "not obligated to
   purchase" (E1), Dow free to walk away "for convenience" (E2), Oklo's buyers still "prospective" (E3).
3. **"Taxpayers share it, capped."** Federal money takes part of the first-of-a-kind risk. Behind it:
   DOE's $400 million cost-shared selections for TVA and Holtec (E8, A29); the Army's pay-on-milestone Janus
   awards (E15). Documented failure: federal help depends on next year's money: ARDP's $3.2 billion was
   "subject to the availability of future appropriations" (E11), DOE's FY2027 request doesn't fund the one
   HALEU demonstration cascade (D13), and taxpayers have paid $11.1 billion for the Energy Department's failure to
   take utilities' spent fuel (D9).
4. **"Bill-payers carry it, with a referee."** A utility recovers costs from customers, with a regulator
   checking prudence. Behind it: Ontario's rate regulation of Darlington (A4); Indiana's SEA 424, 80% of
   development costs with "reasonable, necessary, and prudent" findings above the estimate (E24).
   Documented failure: the last US reactors finished, Vogtle 3 and 4, went from about $14 billion to about
   $35 billion (A19, T3-12); the referee in Georgia let $7.562 billion of Georgia Power's construction cost
   into rates, plus financing, and left $2.6 billion with its shareholders (T3-13, W17), about $8.95 a month
   on a typical home by the company's own pre-vote estimate (T3-14). And in South Carolina the same design was
   abandoned, its customers still paying $2.8 billion over 20 years (T3-16), after executives misled the
   regulators (T3-17). ✎ Top-up T3 filled this gap; the co-owners' customers (about 54% of Vogtle) and
   Santee Cooper's were not researched.
5. **"Order the fleet now."** (Gold, full.) Commit to a series up front so the price can fall. Behind it:
   OPG's four-unit plan, about C$20.9 billion, units 2–4 approval expected in 2028 (A1, B2); New York's
   direction to its power authority to build advanced nuclear (E28). Documented failure: the 2007–09 US
   wave, about 30 reactors applied for and two built (B38); Wood Mackenzie's 47 GW pipeline against 2.5 GW
   under construction or in development (B34).

⚠ Settings 3–4 are where the teams nearly meet: both accept that the first units are expensive and that
someone other than the vendor carries part of the risk; say so. Ontario and Indiana already sit at
setting 4 in law (A4, E24); Tennessee's TVA decides for itself (E9).
