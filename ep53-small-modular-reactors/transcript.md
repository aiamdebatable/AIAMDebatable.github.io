## The research · What a small modular reactor is
**Kate:** Before anyone argues: what makes a reactor small, and what's new about it?
**Ryan:** Every reactor running in America is big: the newest, at Plant Vogtle in Georgia, is about eleven hundred megawatts. By the international definition, a small modular reactor is three hundred or less. One Vogtle reactor is nearly four of the one approved in Tennessee.
**Kate:** So it's a whole new kind of reactor?
**Ryan:** That one isn't. It's a smaller boiling-water reactor, the water-cooled kind America has run since nineteen sixty, on the same fuel. What's simplified: no pumps to push the water round, and safety systems built to cool the core for about three days with no power or operators.
**Kate:** Built in a factory?
**Ryan:** In big prefabricated pieces, then assembled on site. The genuinely new designs are others: one cooled by liquid sodium in Wyoming, one by helium gas planned for Texas and Indiana, and a molten-salt test reactor in Tennessee.
**Kate:** So this isn't the usual fight over whether nuclear is safe?
**Ryan:** Not really. It's whether smaller, simpler and repeated can finally make nuclear cheap and fast to build.

## The research · What it costs: the first one or the tenth
**Ryan:** Only one owner in North America has published a budget for a small reactor. Ontario Power Generation: seven point seven billion Canadian dollars for its first unit and the infrastructure later units will share, including interest and contingency.
**Kate:** Has anyone in the US got that far?
**Ryan:** One project had a full cost estimate: NuScale's power plant for a group of Utah towns. It rose to nine point three billion dollars, including financing, for under five hundred megawatts. It ended in twenty twenty-three because too few towns signed up: twenty-six percent of the power, against eighty needed.
**Kate:** So it was too expensive?
**Ryan:** The partners named lack of subscription, not price. Its target price had been raised to eighty-nine dollars a megawatt-hour, a threshold the project had to meet. That price drove the towns off is a fair guess, but it's not what they said.
**Kate:** And what does TVA assume for Tennessee?
**Ryan:** Its long-term plan assumes about seventeen thousand dollars a kilowatt for a first small reactor, in twenty twenty-six dollars, before tax credits. TVA calls that all-in costs plus risk contingencies. It's nearly twice the top of a generic range its own permit paperwork borrowed.
**Kate:** So will they get cheaper?
**Ryan:** That's the hinge. In the medians of the OECD's Nuclear Energy Agency, power from a first small reactor costs about one and a half times a gas plant's. From a mature series, a bit less than gas, but only if standard designs get built again and again.
**Kate:** Has nuclear ever actually gotten cheaper with practice?
**Ryan:** Not in America's record. Later US plants cost more than earlier ones, and nuclear's measured learning rate runs from negative to six percent; the higher rates you hear are model assumptions. A study of several hundred reactors found South Korea's costs fell, but that finding is disputed.
**Kate:** People say these will roll off assembly lines, like airliners. Is anyone building that line?
**Ryan:** Not yet. No reactor factory exists. Darlington's reactor vessel is a one-unit order at an existing shop, and its modules are steel sections from several shops, assembled on site. The one small-reactor factory under construction makes fuel.
**Kate:** And if they're all one design, couldn't one flaw hit all of them at once?
**Ryan:** It's happened to big reactors. In twenty twenty-two, a single kind of crack took a big slice of France's reactors offline. EDF put its own cost at about twenty-nine billion euros, and France imported power for the first time since nineteen eighty. No small-reactor fleet exists yet, so that's an analogy.
**Kate:** Then why not just build more of the big ones we know?
**Ryan:** Per kilowatt, big is cheaper. MIT's projection puts the next two big Westinghouse reactors at about eighty-three hundred dollars a kilowatt, and a small reactor's mature-series cost still about forty-five percent higher.
