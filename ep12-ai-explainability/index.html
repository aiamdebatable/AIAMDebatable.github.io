Explainable AI: Should AI Have to Explain Every Decision It Makes?

The question: Should a machine decision about you come with an explanation you can contest?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

The law has already decided what an explanation is — and the maths may not be able to deliver it

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in September 2026, Europe's right to an explanation of high-risk AI decisions had taken effect on August 2, though the duties it points at were deferred to December 2027. And on August 11, Colorado filed proposed rules for its replacement AI law.

The fight

Green arguesAudit The Outcomes

In the only randomised trial, explanations didn't rescue clinicians from a biased model, and the maths shows after-the-fact explanations tell you nothing about complicated models. So a mandate to explain buys a document. Test the system instead: error rates by group, audited outcomes, and a reviewer who can reverse the decision.

Gold arguesGive Me The Reasons

Due process asks for two things: notice that you were decided about, and reasons you can argue with. Europe's highest court picked reasons, and said complexity is no excuse. An audit can tell you how often the system is wrong, but not that you're one of the times. A wrong reason can be contested, and a missing one can't.

What both sides quietly agree on

In law, an explanation means reasons and notice, never access to the model, and Europe's highest court has said so twice. Neither team disputed the trial: explanations helped when the model was right and rescued nobody when it was wrong. And both cases ended in the same place: an independent reviewer, the outcome frozen, and the power to reverse.

What the shouting gets wrong

The law has already moved past explain-or-don't, toward a decision you can contest. Both teams want a referee who can reverse the outcome, and they disagree about what that referee needs in hand: your reasons, or the system's audit. The verdict didn't settle it and left that choice to you.

The verdict

Watched it already? Read the verdict as it aired

Andre This one doesn't resolve, and I want to be precise about where it stops rather than call it a draw.

Andre Both teams accepted the same settled ground. The law means reasons and notice, never access to the model. The court said so twice. Neither debater disputed it, and neither disputed the trial: explanations helped when the model was right and rescued nobody when it was wrong.

Andre So the disagreement isn't about what an explanation is. It's about what protects you when the machine says no. Reasons you can argue with, or a system somebody has checked.

Andre Both also conceded their weak flank. Green conceded that an audit is a statistic about a system, and you are one person. Gold conceded that the reasons she wants may not be faithful to the model, and that a rule demanding accuracy may be demanding something that doesn't exist.

Andre The case for auditing rests on the only randomised evidence in the debate, and on a proof that the story a model tells about itself is often no story at all.

Andre The case for reasons rests on due process, on a court that picked reasons over the maths, and on the one person an audit can't identify: the one it got wrong.

Andre And both cases ended in the same place. An independent reviewer, the outcome frozen, and the power to reverse. Neither side is asking for the model's logic. Both are asking for a referee. They disagree about what the referee needs in hand.

Andre So the question we're handing you isn't whether AI should explain itself. Both teams just told you the law has moved past that. It's this: when the machine says no to you, do you want its reasons, or its audit? That one's yours.
