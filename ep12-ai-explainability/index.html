Explainable AI: Should AI Have to Explain Every Decision It Makes?

Should a machine decision about you come with an explanation you can contest?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

The law has already decided what an explanation is — and the maths may not be able to deliver it

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in September 2026, Europe's right to an explanation of high-risk AI decisions had taken effect on August 2, though the duties it points at were deferred to December 2027. And on August 11, Colorado filed proposed rules for its replacement AI law.

Settled

What both sides accept

In law, an explanation means reasons and notice, never access to the model, and Europe's highest court has said so twice. Neither team disputed the trial: explanations helped when the model was right and rescued nobody when it was wrong. And both cases ended in the same place: an independent reviewer, the outcome frozen, and the power to reverse.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesAudit The Outcomes

In the only randomised trial, explanations didn't rescue clinicians from a biased model, and the maths shows after-the-fact explanations tell you nothing about complicated models. So a mandate to explain buys a document. Test the system instead: error rates by group, audited outcomes, and a reviewer who can reverse the decision.

▶ Hear Green at 5:04 on YouTube
Gold arguesGive Me The Reasons

Due process asks for two things: notice that you were decided about, and reasons you can argue with. Europe's highest court picked reasons, and said complexity is no excuse. An audit can tell you how often the system is wrong, but not that you're one of the times. A wrong reason can be contested, and a missing one can't.

▶ Hear Gold at 5:04 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

The law has already moved past explain-or-don't, toward a decision you can contest. Both teams want a referee who can reverse the outcome, and they disagree about what that referee needs in hand: your reasons, or the system's audit. The verdict didn't settle it and left that choice to you.

The verdict

The two cases, as the judge put them
The case for auditing rests on the only randomised evidence in the debate, and on a proof that the story a model tells about itself is often no story at all.
The case for reasons rests on due process, on a court that picked reasons over the maths, and on the one person an audit can't identify: the one it got wrong.

What our fact-check caught

Our drafts got these wrong before they aired. Every correction is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidThe right to an explanation was killed in America.

    The record saysCalifornia's version is in force, with companies due to comply by January 2027, and Colorado replaced its law and is writing new rules.

  2. Draft saidCalifornia's rules send you a special notice when a decision goes against you.

    The record saysNo such notice exists. You have to ask for the information yourself.

  3. Draft saidResearch shows faithful, understandable explanations are too hard to compute for most AI models.

    The record saysThe paper isn't about computing power. It shows these after-the-fact explanations often tell you nothing about complicated models, depending on the kind of model, and it sets out when they do.
