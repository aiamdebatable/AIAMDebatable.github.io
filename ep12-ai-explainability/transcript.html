The verdict

AndreThis one doesn't resolve, and I want to be precise about where it stops rather than call it a draw.

AndreBoth teams accepted the same settled ground. The law means reasons and notice, never access to the model. The court said so twice. Neither debater disputed it, and neither disputed the trial: explanations helped when the model was right and rescued nobody when it was wrong.

AndreSo the disagreement isn't about what an explanation is. It's about what protects you when the machine says no. Reasons you can argue with, or a system somebody has checked.

AndreBoth also conceded their weak flank. Green conceded that an audit is a statistic about a system, and you are one person. Gold conceded that the reasons she wants may not be faithful to the model, and that a rule demanding accuracy may be demanding something that doesn't exist.

AndreThe case for auditing rests on the only randomised evidence in the debate, and on a proof that the story a model tells about itself is often no story at all.

AndreThe case for reasons rests on due process, on a court that picked reasons over the maths, and on the one person an audit can't identify: the one it got wrong.

AndreAnd both cases ended in the same place. An independent reviewer, the outcome frozen, and the power to reverse. Neither side is asking for the model's logic. Both are asking for a referee. They disagree about what the referee needs in hand.

AndreSo the question we're handing you isn't whether AI should explain itself. Both teams just told you the law has moved past that. It's this: when the machine says no to you, do you want its reasons, or its audit? That one's yours.
