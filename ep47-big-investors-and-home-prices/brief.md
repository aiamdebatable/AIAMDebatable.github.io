# Research brief — Housing Market: Will Banning Big Investors Make Homes Cheaper?

_The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video._

**Episode type: DEBATE.** Two teams argue at full strength; the verdict names no winner and hands the
viewer one dial. The record leans (see "Where the record lands"), and the brief says where, but the lean
is about **what kind of effect** a ban has, not about which team is right: the honest finding is that a
purchase ban can change **who** gets a house more than **what it costs**, possibly at a cost to renters.
That is a trade a viewer has to price for themselves.

**Scope, said early on air:** the federal ban on **buying** by the largest single-family landlords,
whether it makes a home cheaper, and what it does to the people who rent. It is **not** a forecast of

debate about landlords in general, and not about apartment buildings: the law covers structures of two
units or fewer (A-B4).

## The pitch, in one line

**"Why should a company own a family home it will never live in?"** That is the question viewers ask
most (27 comments in the council's harvest of 1,824), and it is the premise the ban was written on
(its section is headed "Homes are for people…", T1-R1). **The plain answer:** a company buys a family
home to rent it to a family (the two biggest report 96–97% of their homes occupied, company-reported,
T4-13, T4-16), and from 7 January 2027 anyone who controls 350 or more single-family homes may not buy
another one. Nobody has to sell, and the group it covers holds
about 6 or 7 of every 1,000 single-family homes in the country, by private estimates (A-B1, A-B6, A-B9;
B-B1, B-B4, B-A1). **Will that make homes cheaper? On the US record, not measurably and not
nationally:** the big owners have been selling slightly more than they buy since about 2023, and in
the six metros GAO followed, roughly 4 to 6 in 10 of the biggest owners' sales went to people who moved
in (B-C1, B-B5, B-A12); the price effects found are local, in a few Sun Belt metros (C-A5, C-A9). No US
ban has been measured yet (T4-30, T4-31); the one place that has actually tried a purchase ban, the
Netherlands, saw prices not fall while first-time buyers took most of the homes investors stopped
buying (T2-T8–T2-T10). **So the real question is not "will homes get cheaper". It is: who should the
next starter home go to, the family who'll buy it or the family who'll rent it, and what is that worth
to you?**

## The viewer's moment

**Open on the gut question, in a viewer's words, then answer it plainly** (cold-open plan below):
"Why should a company own a family home it will never live in?" The answer is a trade, and these three
people are its sides. Each is invented and unnamed, and built only from figures in the rows cited.

- **The first-time buyer who lost the house.** She is about the age of the typical first-time buyer,
  now 40, in a year when first-time buyers were a record-low 21% of primary-home buyers (C-E1; NAR's
  annual survey covers buyers of a home they live in, so investors are outside it, C-E2). She offered
  with a mortgage. The seller took a cash offer **with no financing condition**. That is how investors
  win: on terms, not on price. Mortgaged buyers on average pay about 11% **more** than all-cash buyers
  for comparable homes (C-D2); in the one place that has tried a purchase ban, the Netherlands, the
  study's authors said investors won homes "without bidding higher prices" (C-D3; their 2023 memo, a
  line the 2025 version does not repeat, so attribute it to 2023). ⚠ Whether the cash buyer was a
  350-home landlord is exactly what nobody measured: most investors pay cash (65%, all investors,
  Redfin, C-D1), and small investors make about two-thirds of investor purchases (B-C12). NAR's own
  economist puts the all-cash pressure on equity-rich owner-buyers too (C-E3). **The ban takes one
  kind of bidder out of her way. Will she win the next one?**
- **The renter in Jacksonville.** Firms owning 5,000+ homes held about 22% of the single-family
  rentals in the Jacksonville metro in 2024, up from 7% in 2018 (B-B9). In Atlanta in 2022, owners of
  1,000+ homes held about a quarter (B-B8). Her landlord will not have to sell (A-B6), and nothing in
  her lease changes; what she gets is a federal complaint line her landlord must tell her about every
  year (T1-R1, T1-R4, T1-R5). What the ban changes is what happens to the **next** rental house:
  whether a family buys it, a small landlord rents it, or nobody builds it. Whether that costs her in
  rent is the open question. **Does her landlord name her rent?** The US record says it nudges it where
  big landlords cluster: on the first lease after a big firm buys a home, rent rises about 3.7 points
  more than an average 6% rise, and only a little faster after that (Philadelphia Fed, T4-21). The
  nudge shrinks where renters have other homes to choose from (T4-24), and the biggest firm cut rents on
  new leases three quarters running in 2025–26 (company-reported, T4-14). On what their buying did
  overall the past record points both ways: rents grew faster where they moved in (C-C1, C-B7), but one
  model finds their buying **lowered** rents on net, because they added rentals (C-C4, C-C5). What a
  ban on their **purchases** does to rent nobody has measured: no study simulates it (C-A1), and no US
  ban has run long enough (T4-30, T4-31); beside that, the one place that has tried a purchase ban
  reports no rent figure in its latest study, whose authors warn it may cut housing opportunities for
  lower-income renters (T2-T12). **The ban was written for the buyer. Does the renter pay for it?**
- **★ The hook nobody expects: the big landlords were already leaving.** The largest owners have been
  selling slightly more homes than they buy since about 2023, before the law existed (B-C1, B-B5). And
  where government data followed the homes the very biggest owners sold, in six metros from 2019 to
  2024, roughly 4 to 6 in every 10 went to people who moved in (B-A12, GAO). The ban arrives in a
  market the big buyers had partly left, and whose biggest seller of the moment is selling to pay its
  debts (B-C3).

**The backdrop the viewer lives in (D-D1–D-D7):** the 30-year mortgage rate was 7.32% on 2026-10-22,
up from 6.19% a year earlier; the national median existing-home price was $417,500 in September, up
1.3% on a year; supply was 4.8 months. Prices are still rising and rates went back up. A quarter of
full-time workers earn under $849 a week (T3-6), and the typical home costs about $3,400 a month to
carry with taxes and insurance (our arithmetic, see "Can a paycheck buy the house?"). **Is the ban
aimed at what is actually squeezing her?**

## The question

**Will banning big investors from buying family homes make homes cheaper?** Under it sits the question
the record forces: **if it changes who gets the house more than what the house costs, is that trade
worth what it may cost renters?**

The viewers bring three more, each answered from US data below:
- **Can a paycheck buy the house at all?** (wages, T3): no, not a typical single paycheck.
- **Do big landlords name the rent?** (T4): they nudge it where they cluster; they don't set it
  nationally.
- **Why not just build?** (T5): builders face measured costs and credit, and the law's own building
  pieces are slow and unfunded; nothing here ranks building against investors.

## Why now

_Peg hunt run 2026-10-26 (peg-hunt skill, Phases 1–4); every peg fact re-checked at the primary by hand
pass A. Re-run the hunt before scripting and on the day it posts._

**The hot peg (description): the Treasury's proposed rule, published 2026-10-23.** The Treasury
published a first-phase proposed rule implementing the investor ban (Federal Register document
2026-21675; 91 FR 66799; RIN 1505-AC96; 31 CFR Part 36; docket TREAS-DO-2026-0529) (A-D1). It
leaves reporting, compliance and enforcement procedures to later rules (A-D2). **Public comments close
2026-11-23** (A-A5; the Federal Register prints that date; regulations.gov lists 11-24 and accepts late
comments, so say "November 23"). Two things in it carry the argument:

- **Treasury says it cannot count who is covered:** "Reliable data are not currently available
  regarding how many institutional investors and other entities may be affected" (A-A6, B-B13). Its
  "100 to 300" is a paperwork estimate for **voluntary** recordkeeping, not a count of covered
  investors (A-A7). The rule quantifies no effect on prices or rents (A-D3).
- **The proposal widens two routes the "loophole" side will cite.** Homes owned at enactment, or bought
  in compliance, may pass from one covered investor to another, more than once (proposed 36.11, A-D7);
  and a large investor may buy a foreclosed home "directly or indirectly from" the servicer or lender
  (proposed 36.10(b)(2), A-A2). Both are **proposals**, not final.

It also proposes what build-to-rent means: homes never owner-occupied, bought within 18 months of the
certificate of occupancy, first occupied by renters (A-D4), and it rejected treating existing
build-to-rent stock as covered by that exception (A-D5).

**The structural peg (script): the law itself, and Michigan's.** Name the law, the line and the date:
**"the ROAD to Housing Act: from January 7, 2027, anyone who controls 350 or more single-family homes
can't buy another one."**

- **How it became law:** H.R. 6644 was presented on 2026-06-29 and **became law without the President's
  signature on 2026-07-11** as Public Law 119-101 (A-C1). ⛔ Never "signed". Senate 89-10 (2026-03-12)
  and 85-5 (2026-06-22); House 358-32 (2026-06-23) (A-C2–A-C4). Every House "no" was Republican; every
  House Democrat voted yes (A-C5). The ban was **added in the Senate**: the House's original "Housing for
  the 21st Century Act" had none (A-C6).
- **What section 1001 does:** covers any for-profit entity **in the business** of single-family homes
  with "investment control" of **350 or more** single-family homes nationwide, counted in the aggregate
  (A-B1, A-H1). Investment control reaches the owner, the general partner or managing member, the
  investment manager, or a non-passive stake above 25%, alone or in concert (A-B3). A single-family home
  is a structure of 2 or fewer units, **excluding manufactured homes**, so duplexes count (A-B4,
  A-H1). **Homes held before enactment count toward the 350**, so the big landlords are covered from day
  one (A-B2), but **nothing forces them to sell** (A-B6). The ban starts **2027-01-07**, 180 days after
  enactment (A-B9), and is repealed **15 years after the effective date: 2042-01-07** (A-A3, our
  arithmetic). Penalty: up to the greater of **$1 million per violation or 3 times the purchase price**;
  only Treasury, or the Attorney General at Treasury's request, can sue (A-B7). Penalties go to HUD's HOME
  program, but only as appropriations Acts provide (A-B8). No regulation may change the 350 line (A-B5).
- **About ten kinds of exception**, among them build-to-rent, renovate-to-rent (15% spent), two
  homeownership programs, homes taken in satisfaction of a debt or by a foreclosing servicer, and buying
  from another large investor; the eleventh letter, (K), only allows **combinations** of the others, and
  a separate clause exempts restructurings of homes held at enactment (A-A1, A-A2, A-D4, A-D7, A-D9,
  A-F4). ⛔ Never "eleven loopholes".
- **The executive order before it:** EO 14376 (2026-01-20) was a policy statement with no home-count
  threshold and no ban on private purchases; it told agencies to stop **facilitating** such purchases,
  with a build-to-rent carve-out (A-E1–A-E4). What the agencies actually issued under it is **not
  established** (A-E7): make no claim about it.
- **Michigan already has its own ban, in force since 2026-07-21:** Public Act 32 of 2026 (HB 6074),
  covering entities with investment control of **more than 100** Michigan single-family homes **and**
  $375 million or more under management or in net value, with a fine of up to $25,000 per home (A-F1–A-F3).
  It copies several federal exceptions but has **no** buy-from-another-large-investor exception and no
  sunset (A-F4). Whether the federal law overrides it is **untested**: section 1001 has no preemption or
  savings clause (A-F11). New York (2025) makes covered buyers wait until a home has been listed 90 days
  (A-F6). Arizona's 50-home cap died in committee (A-F8); Georgia's cap was stripped in a House committee
  (A-F7).
- **Courts:** no court challenge had been reported as of late October (A-G1; absence in a docket search,
  not proof).

**Why it fits the thesis.** The government is enforcing a ban on a buyer class it says it cannot yet
count, on a group that holds well under 1 in 100 homes and was already selling. The live question is
no longer "should they be banned" but "will it work, and for whom".

**Places to name on air (structural, frozen):** the law and its line; **Michigan** (its own ban since
July); **Atlanta, Jacksonville, Charlotte, Phoenix** as the metros where big landlords concentrated
(B-B8, B-B9); **the Netherlands** (and **Rotterdam**) as the one place that has actually tried a
purchase ban, named only beside a US number (T2-T8, T2-T9). Leave the
personalities out: the law passed both chambers by wide margins (A-C2–A-C5).

**Durability:** after 2026-11-23, re-peg the description to "comment period closed; final rule
pending; the ban starts January 7, 2027". After 2027-01-07 the script still reads correctly ("the ban
that began in January").

## Settled (not the debate)

**The law** (hand pass A)
- It is law; it bans future **purchases** only; no forced sale; pre-enactment homes count toward the
  350; starts 2027-01-07; repealed 2042-01-07 (A-C1, A-B2, A-B6, A-B9, A-A3).
- Penalty ceiling: the greater of $1M per violation or 3x the price; Treasury or the AG sues; no private
  right of action (A-B7). Several affiliates on one purchase = one violation for the maximum (A-D8).
- Treasury has no count of who is covered (A-A6).

**How big they are** (hand pass B)
- **Nationally, very small.** Three private estimates at the law's own 350 line agree: Parcl 0.59%
  (about 140 entities, about 530,000 homes, March 2026), AEI about 0.65% (June 2025), John Burns about
  0.7% of 92 million single-family homes (B-B1, B-B4, B-A2). On air: "about 6 or 7 in every 1,000, by
  private estimates; Treasury says it has no reliable count." ⚠ Vendor counts are not the statute's
  test (B-B2), and no single source is authoritative (B-B10).
- **Locally, large in rentals.** As a share of **single-family rentals**: Atlanta 25%, Jacksonville
  20.5%, Charlotte 18.3%, Tampa 15.3%, Phoenix 13.6% (June 2022, owners of 1,000+, GAO 2024, which says
  the shares "may be overstated") (B-B8). Firms with 5,000+ homes in 5+ metros: Jacksonville 22% in 2024
  (7% in 2018), Phoenix 13%, Dallas 9%; their shares fell in all six metros in 2024 (B-B9, GAO 2026). As
  a share of **all** single-family homes in those six metros: under 1% (Cincinnati, Seattle) to 3%
  (C-A23, B-A13). Parcl: Atlanta 71,625 homes, 4.16% of its single-family stock, at 350+ (B-B3).
- **They were net sellers before the ban.** Parcl: the 1,000+ cohort has been a net seller since early
  2023 (B-C1, vendor). AEI: over two years 350+ owners sold 185,000 and bought 178,000 (B-B5,
  advocacy). Invitation Homes, Q2 2026: 657 sold, 196 bought, and it gives private-market prices as the
  reason, without naming the Act (B-C6, B-A14). AMH guides **no** traditional purchases in 2026; its
  growth is homes it builds (B-D8). VineBrook's selling is distress: its own filing says it lacks the
  liquidity to meet its debts (B-C3).
- **Who bought what they sold, before the law:** in GAO's six metros, 2019–2024, the share of 5,000+
  owners' sales going to owner-occupants averaged 38% (Seattle) to 64% (Nashville) a year; 17–35% of
  their purchases came **from** owner-occupants (B-A12). After 2027-01-07: not measured.
- **Who is buying instead now:** smaller investors. Cotality: mega investors' share of purchases halved
  from 2.5% to 1.3% (Dec 2025 → Jan 2026) while 3–9-home investors rose (B-C11, vendor). Realtor.com:
  investors 10.7% of H1 2026 purchases, small investors 66.2% of investor purchases (B-C12, vendor).
- **Build-to-rent:** 68,000 single-family built-for-rent starts in 2025, down 19% (B-D1, NAHB from
  Census); 63,000 over the four quarters to Q2 2026 (B-D3). NAHB names **the bill itself** (the Senate's
  later-dropped forced-sale rule) among the causes, with financing costs (B-A9).

**Prices, rents, buyers** (hand pass C; US evidence first, the Dutch line beside it)
- **Prices: the US evidence finds local effects where big landlords concentrated, not national ones.**
  In Coven's model (Georgia), in their top-decile markets entry explains 20% of the price rise, about
  6.2%, or $18,600 on a $300,000 home; in most entry areas, "almost all" of the price association is
  not the investors' doing (C-A5, C-A9, C-A7, C-A8). The price studies mostly cover the crash and its
  aftermath (GAO, C-A19). **No US ban has been measured yet** (T4-30, T4-31). **Beside it, the one
  place that has actually tried a purchase ban** (the Netherlands, from 2022: cities could bar
  investors from buying cheaper homes to rent out): the paper's **latest version (2025-07-11)** finds
  prices **did not fall**: +1.3–1.4% in Rotterdam, not significant, with a bump of about 3% right after
  the ban that had faded by late 2022 (T2-T8, T2-T10, T2-T11). Its window ends 1 January 2023, about a
  year after the ban (T2 method).
- **Who gets the house: in the US, the biggest owners' sales already went roughly 4 to 6 in 10 to
  people who moved in** (GAO's six metros, 2019–2024, B-A12), and in Coven's model homeownership fell
  0.22 per home they bought (C-A10). **Beside it, the Dutch result:** in Rotterdam's banned
  neighbourhoods investor purchases fell **19 points (73%)**, offset by **+13 points of first-time
  buyers and +6 of repeat buyers** (T2-T9). The landlords shut out were mostly small private owners;
  large investors (500+ units) were not buying homes to let there (T2-T13, C-C10). The Dutch
  government's own evaluation (RIGO and Kadaster for the Interior Ministry, 2026-06-10, Tweede Kamer
  2026D51974) found the buyer shift **only where a city adopted the ban before Q2 2023**; for later
  adopters, "geen significant effect" (no significant effect); its price chart shows no causal price
  effect. Context, **not independent confirmation**: Lianne Hans (Kadaster) co-wrote both it and the
  paper (T2-T15).
- **Rents:** the US record is under "Do big landlords name the rent?" below. Beside it, the Dutch
  paper's latest version reports **no rent result**: the +4% rent rise, which the authors called
  "suggestive", exists only in the June 2023 version (T2-T4, T2-T5, T2-T12).
- **Cash wins on terms, not on price:** in the US, mortgaged buyers pay about 11% **more** than all-cash
  buyers for comparable homes, and ZIPs with more single-family REITs show a **lower** sale-to-list ratio
  (C-D2, C-B4); beside it, the Dutch authors' 2023 memo said investors won "without bidding higher
  prices" (C-D3, a line the 2025 version does not repeat).

**Other drivers and the market** (hand pass D)
- The studies that try to explain the 2020s jump point to **remote work** (over half of the 2019–2023
  real rise, a working paper) and **rates and lock-in**; **none of them tested investors** (D-B9,
  D-B2, D-B6, D-A1, D-A11).
- The 2026 baseline: 7.32% (10-22), $417,500 (September, +1.3%), 4.8 months (D-D1, D-D3, D-D5).

### What it changes for you (top-up T1, 2026-10-27, quote-checked 18/18; added after the council's affected seat)
- **Renter:** the ban stops big investors **buying**; section 1001 contains no provision on leases, renewals, rents or
  evictions (terms searched in T1). The one tenant provision is a HUD complaint line every 350+ landlord must tell
  tenants about at move-in and every year. ⛔ So never "the law says nothing about renters". Only homes bought under
  the rent-to-own or boost-homeownership exceptions (D, E) carry more: a rent cap in (D) in the statute, and in
  Treasury's **proposal** a first right to buy (30 days, then a 5-business-day match) and, after 5 years, the lesser
  of $5,000 or 2.5% toward buying (T1 rows; NPRM = proposed, not law).
- **Small landlord or seller:** covered means 350+ homes counted nationwide through ownership or control, so a
  mom-and-pop landlord is not covered. Selling **to** a big investor stays open only for two years after the ban
  starts (to 2029-01-07, our arithmetic), then only under an exception; whether a family selling the home it lives
  in counts in that window is unresolved (T1-S4). Buying **from** a big investor is never restricted.
- Line for the script (T1): "The ban stops big investors buying. It does not touch your lease. If your landlord
  owns 350 homes or more, you get a federal complaint line."

### Can a paycheck buy the house? (wages, top-up T3, 2026-10-27; the viewers' most-liked point, true and now with numbers)

The comment, about 2,700 likes: "$35–45k can't buy a $400k house." **True, and by a wide margin.**
- **The pay:** a quarter of full-time wage and salary workers earn under **$849 a week** (BLS, Q3 2026),
  about **$44,100 a year** (our arithmetic) (T3-6). The median full-time worker earns $1,232 a week, and
  over the past year that pay rose 1.5% while consumer prices rose 3.5% (T3-4, T3-5). The median
  household earned $87,460 in 2025, a record after inflation (T3-1).
- **The house:** the median existing home sold for $417,500 in September (D-D3, T3-7) at a 30-year rate
  of 7.32% (D-D1, T3-8). With 10% down, principal and interest alone come to about $2,580 a month; add
  the Atlanta Fed's national typical **$822** a month for property tax, insurance and mortgage insurance
  (T3-12) and it is **about $3,400 a month** (our arithmetic).
- **The gap:** at the standard line of 30% of gross income, a $40,000 earner has about $1,000 a month
  for housing, which carries a home of roughly $120,000–$160,000, not $400,000 (our arithmetic). The
  gap reaches well past the viral number: the Atlanta Fed puts the income needed to own the median home
  at **about $126,800 a year, against a median household income of about $86,700** (August 2026, T3-9,
  T3-11), and Harvard's Joint Center at $120,800 (T3-19). Just over 82% of US metros are unaffordable to
  their own median household on the Atlanta Fed's measure (T3-14).
- **How the gap opened:** the typical home cost about **3.2 years** of the median household's income on
  average in the 1990s, 4.1 in 2019, a record 5.0 in 2022 and **4.7 in 2025** (T3-17). From 2019 to 2025
  national house prices rose about **62%** and median household income about **27%** (both nominal; our
  arithmetic on FHFA's index and Census's table, T3-22, T3-3). The monthly payment on the median home
  nearly doubled in five years, $1,240 at the end of 2020 to $2,420 (T3-18). In March 2026 only 23% of
  listings were affordable on $75,000 or less, against 49% in March 2019 (T3-20).
- **Who it hits:** first-time buyers fell to a record-low **21%** of buyers, and their median age rose
  to a record **40** (NAR's annual survey, C-E1, T3-30).
- **Property tax and insurance (another viewers' point):** about a quarter of the typical monthly cost,
  $822 of $3,172 (T3-10, T3-12).
- **The caveats, carried.** NAR's own index says the typical **family** can qualify (105.4 in its
  September report, 103.3 in August; above 100 means qualifying, D-D6, T3-15), because it uses family
  income, 20% down, and principal and interest alone at 25% of income (T3-16). So ⛔ never "nobody can
  afford a home"; say "a typical single paycheck can't come close, and even the typical household falls
  well short on standard terms". **Rents did not outrun pay since 2019:** from September 2019 to
  September 2026 BLS's rent of primary residence rose about 35%, average hourly pay about 34% and
  rank-and-file pay about 38% (our arithmetic on T3-24–T3-26). So ⛔ never "rents outran wages". What is
  at a record is the **burden**: 22.7 million renter households, 49% of renters, paid more than 30% of
  income in 2024, up 2.3 million since 2019, and 26% paid more than half (T3-27).
- ⛔ **No "because" in this beat.** These rows show *that* the gap opened, not *why*; nothing here ties
  it to investors (see the ruling under "Both sides quietly agree").
- Line for the script (T3): "If you earn $40,000, the standard rule says you can spend about $1,000 a
  month on housing. The typical home at today's rates costs about three and a half times that."

### Do big landlords name the rent? (top-up T4, 2026-10-27; US evidence only)

**The answer, at the record's strength: they nudge rents up where they cluster, by single digits, and
the market still sets the ceiling.** That they set rents nationally, or that "that's where rents come
from", is not supported.
- **Where they cluster, a real local markup.** When a big firm first re-lets a home it bought, rent
  rises about 60% faster than average: in the strictest model about **3.7 points on an average 6% rise,
  on the first lease after purchase**, then only about 7% faster (Philadelphia Fed staff working paper,
  national listing and deed data, 2010–2021, T4-21); renovations don't explain it (T4-22). Where their
  share of a neighbourhood's rentals grows, rents rise a little faster for every landlord, about +0.3
  points per point of share, a correlation the authors flag (T4-23). When the 2015–2017 mergers that
  formed Invitation Homes and AMH put more homes under one firm, rents in the overlapping neighbourhoods
  rose about **0.5%** relative to others (Gurun et al., T4-25, C-F1). These sit with the studies already
  here: Gorback-Qian-Zhu (C-C1), Hanson's markups (C-B7) and Barbieri-Dobbels' concentration effect
  (C-C5).
- **The limit, in the same studies.** The markup shrinks where renters have other single-family homes
  to choose from (T4-24). In Miami the biggest buyers got only "marginally" higher rents (T4-26).
  Barbieri-Dobbels find their buying lowered rents on net in Atlanta (C-C4), and the later
  Lambie-Hanson paper finds no significant rent effect through 2014 (C-A16). GAO, in its own words: some
  studies find investors "may have contributed" to higher rents where they concentrated, while "other
  studies found more limited effects" (T4-27, T4-28, C-A20); its newer work on rents isn't finished
  (T4-29).
- **What their own books show (company-reported; label it).** Invitation Homes cut rents on new leases
  three quarters running in 2025–26 (−0.7%, −4.2%, −3.0%) before +1.1% this spring, while renewals
  stayed positive (T4-14). In Q2 2026 its rent per home rose 2.0% and AMH's 2.6%, while all US rents rose 2.8%
  (BLS) (T4-15, T4-17, T4-18): at or below the national pace on their own numbers, a rough comparison of
  different measures (T4-20). Invitation Homes says it prices with a tool on public data, benchmarked
  partly against the other big single-family landlords (T4-12). Tenants who stay pay more than new ones;
  that fits moving costs and proves nothing about market power (T4-14).
- **Nationally:** owners of 1,000+ homes hold about 3% of single-family rentals (GAO 2024, citing the
  Urban Institute, B-B6, B-B7). No study in this record attributes national rent levels or rent growth
  to them.
- **RealPage, "the rent algorithm", is a real case about apartment buildings, not these landlords.**
  The Justice Department and 8 states sued RealPage in 2024, alleging it pooled competing **apartment**
  landlords' private rent data (T4-1–T4-3); the six landlords added in 2025 are all apartment operators
  (T4-4). The government's own complaint puts single-family homes outside the market (T4-5), and no
  big single-family landlord is a defendant (T4-6). RealPage settled with the United States in November
  2025 on **conduct rules** (no rivals' private data, a three-year monitor), with no payment found in
  the filing and no liability found (T4-7, T4-8); whether the court has entered that judgment is
  unresolved (T4-9). The states' case continues after a 2026-09-30 ruling refusing to dismiss it
  (T4-10, T4-11). Nothing has been proven at trial.
- **Beside it, the one place that has tried a purchase ban:** its latest study reports no rent result
  (T2-T12), so no purchase ban anywhere has a measured rent effect.
- Line for the script (T4): "Where the big rental companies cluster, rents do rise a bit faster, mostly
  on the first lease after they buy. But they don't name the rent: over the past year the biggest one
  cut rents on new leases three quarters running."

### Why not just build? (top-up T5, 2026-10-27)

**Measured** (Census, BLS, Harvard's Joint Center), kept apart from the builders' trade group's
estimates below.
- **Builders are building smaller:** homes under 1,400 sq ft were 11% of single-family completions in
  2025, up from 7% every year 2016–2022 (15% in 1999), and homes under 1,800 sq ft about a third
  (T5-24, T5-25); 41% of homes built for sale sat on lots under 7,000 sq ft (T5-26).
- **But fewer:** single-family starts January–September 2026 were down 4% on a year and completions down
  11% (T5-17, T5-18); 476,000 new homes sit unsold, 8.4 months of supply (T5-20); 43% of builders cut
  prices in October (NAHB's survey, T5-28).
- **What goes into a house costs about half again what it did in January 2020:** BLS's index of inputs
  to new home building is up about **52%** since January 2020 and about 10% in the past year (our
  arithmetic on T5-8); much of the past year's jump is energy, not materials (NAHB's reading of BLS:
  materials +5.5%, energy +64%, T5-10). Gypsum rose 47% and steel mill products 84% through April 2026
  (Harvard's Joint Center, T5-11).
- **Builders borrow at 10–13%:** effective rates on land, development and construction loans of about
  10.4% to 12.6% in Q2 2026 (NAHB's survey of builders, T5-33), against a 7.3% mortgage (D-D1).
- **Few new homes are cheap:** about **one new home in six** sold for under $300,000 in 2025 (119,000 of
  678,000, Census; our arithmetic, T5-27). Manufactured homes, the cheapest kind, numbered 102,700 in
  2025, about a third of the 1970s–1990s pace (T5-29). Lot values rose 52% from 2020 to 2024 (an AEI
  indicator cited by Harvard's Joint Center, T5-34).

**The builders' trade group's estimates, labelled every time** ("NAHB, the builders' trade group,
estimates…"; they would gain if the rules were eased):
- Regulation is about a quarter (26.4%) of a new home's price, $131,734 on a $499,500 home; NAHB says
  it is not estimating how much of it is excessive (T5-5); its biggest single item is building-code
  changes (T5-7).
- For every $100 of a new home's price in NAHB's own member survey, about $64 is building the house,
  $14 the land and $11 the builder's profit: shares only, since NAHB says the sample is not
  representative (T5-1–T5-3).
- The skilled-labour shortage costs about 19,000 homes a year (the Home Builders Institute, NAHB's
  training arm, T5-32).

**What the same law does about building, with its dates** (T5-36–T5-38; D-E1–D-E6): zoning
**guidance** only, due from HUD by **2029-07-11** (our arithmetic), with no duty on any state or city;
environmental-review streamlining by HUD rulemaking with no deadline, for money appropriated afterwards;
the block-grant cut and bonus from **fiscal 2029** (from 2028-10-01, our arithmetic) through 2043;
chassis-free manufactured homes once states certify and HUD writes standards; and **no new money**
(sec. 1202). CBO scored the Act, but its figures could not be read, and no source estimates how many
homes these sections add (T5-39).
- ⛔ **This beat ranks nothing:** no source here says building matters more or less than investors, and
  how much zoning explains is itself contested (D-A2, D-C7, D-C8).
- Line for the script (T5): "Builders are already making smaller homes, but they're starting fewer of
  them this year, and what goes into a house costs about half again what it did in 2020. The same law
  has building pieces too, but they're slow: zoning guidance by 2029, grant rewards from late 2028, and
  no new money."

## Contested (the real fight)

The labels below are proposals; the episode's sides are set when it is built.

**Team Green: "Protect the starter home."** The big landlords raised prices where they concentrated,
and they win homes on terms a family can't match; a cap keeps the next starter home for a family.
Steelmanned from primaries:
- **Prices, measured:** Gorback, Qian and Zhu (2010–2022, instrumented): faster growth in rental
  companies' share brings faster house-price **and** rent growth (C-C1). Barbieri and Dobbels (metro
  Atlanta, house-level data, 2026): institutional buying raised sale prices about 3.2%, about $17,000
  (C-C3). Even Coven's anti-ban-leaning model concedes about 6%, $18,600 on a $300,000 home, in the
  top-decile markets (C-A9).
- **Rents through market power:** on the first lease after a big firm buys a home, rent rises about
  3.7 points more than an average 6% rise, not explained by renovations (Philadelphia Fed, T4-21,
  T4-22); where the 2015–2017 mergers concentrated ownership, rents rose about 0.5% (T4-25);
  concentration alone raised rents (removing it cuts rents 2.1%, C-C5); Hanson finds the rent gap
  after entry is **not** market timing, consistent with markups (C-B7); a Brookings piece that opposes
  a ban concedes concentrated investors can raise rents above a competitive level (C-F8).
- **Homeownership:** Coven: homeownership fell 0.22 per home they bought (C-A10). Lambie-Hanson et al.:
  more corporate buying cut county homeownership (C-A15) (⚠ their "institutional" means **any company**,
  C-A11). **Beside it, Green's best true point is the one place that has actually tried a purchase
  ban:** it did move homes to first-time buyers (in Rotterdam they took 13 of the 19 points investors
  lost, T2-T9), and prices did not fall (T2-T8). The government's evaluation agrees on buyers, but
  only for cities that adopted early (T2-T15).
- **Conduct:** the FTC's $48 million settlement with Invitation Homes over alleged fees that could top
  $1,700 a year (settlement of allegations, C-F2, C-F3); in 2015 Atlanta, large landlords **filed**
  evictions against about 1 in 5 of their homes (C-F4, C-F5).
- **Green's gap (say it):** "outbid with cash" is advocacy wording, unmeasured (C-H1, C-H2); the
  price effects are local and modest, mostly measured before 2015 or in a few metros (C-A19, C-C2); the
  rent markup is local, single-digit and shrinks where renters have choices (T4-21, T4-24); the
  rent-algorithm case is about apartments, not these landlords (T4-5, T4-6); no study shows the ban
  will lower prices, and no US ban has been measured (T4-31).

**Team Gold: "Wrong target, and renters pay."** The ban hits a small, shrinking group, leaves the bulk
of investor buying alone, and may cut the rental homes that families who can't buy rely on.
Steelmanned from primaries:
- **Scale:** about 6–7 in 1,000 homes nationally (B-B1, B-B4, B-A2); already net sellers (B-C1, B-B5);
  replaced as buyers by small investors (B-C11, B-C12). **Beside it, the one place that has actually
  tried a purchase ban didn't see prices fall:** the latest version says it "does not negatively
  impact prices" (no significant change, if anything a slight, short-lived rise; T2-T8, T2-T10). ⛔ Not
  "and rents rose": the latest version reports no rent result (T2-T12). Gold's Dutch renter point is the
  authors' warning that the ban may cut "housing opportunities for low-income renters" (T2-T12): a
  warning, not a measured rent effect.
- **Rents, measured:** Barbieri and Dobbels find institutional buying **lowered** rents on net, because
  added rental supply outweighed market power (C-C4); in Coven's simulation, removing the big landlords
  raises rents (0.7 points per point of rental stock they own) and 64% of their homes go to small
  landlords, not buyers (C-A3, C-A4). The later Lambie-Hanson paper finds **no** significant effect on
  rents or evictions through 2014 (C-A16). The Philadelphia Fed finds single-family REITs added only
  0.14% a year to price growth, with no effect on mortgage access (C-B1, C-B3). **They don't name the
  rent:** the markup shrinks where renters have choices (T4-24); GAO calls the record mixed (T4-27,
  T4-28); on their own numbers their rent per home rose at or below the national pace in Q2 2026, and
  the biggest one cut new-lease rents three quarters running (company-reported, T4-14, T4-20).
- **Who bought from them:** 38–64% of the biggest owners' sales went to owner-occupants in GAO's six
  metros (B-A12).
- **Gold's gap (say it):** every rent-backfire result is a model, not a measurement of this law:
  Coven's forced exit, which this law doesn't do (C-A1), or Barbieri-Dobbels' model of what the big
  landlords' buying did in Atlanta (C-C4). The one measured rent rise under a real ban was "suggestive",
  from a market of small landlords, and is gone from the paper's latest version (T2-T4, T2-T5, T2-T12,
  T2-T13); two other studies find rents grew **faster** where big landlords moved in (C-C1, C-B7), and
  the first lease after a big firm buys a home carries a real markup (T4-21); the Coven simulation that
  raises rents also **lowers prices** (C-A2); and build-to-rent "adds homes" is an assertion nobody has
  tested (B-D6).

### Where the record lands, said plainly

- **On "will homes get cheaper":** the record leans **no, not measurably, and not nationally**. In the
  US the covered group is tiny nationally (B-B1) and was already selling (B-C1, B-B5), the price
  effects found are local, modest and mostly historical (C-A5, C-A19), and no US ban has been measured
  yet (T4-30, T4-31). Beside that, the one place that has actually tried a purchase ban did not see
  prices fall (latest version: no significant change, if anything a slight, short-lived rise, T2-T8,
  T2-T10). This is the question in the title, and the episode answers it honestly.
- **On "who gets the house":** the record leans **yes, a ban can tilt homes toward buyers who live in
  them**, with caveats. In the US, the biggest owners' sales already went roughly 4 to 6 in 10 to
  owner-occupants (B-A12), their buying cut homeownership in Coven's model (C-A10), and what happens
  after 2027-01-07 is unmeasured. Beside it, in the one place that tried a ban first-time buyers took
  13 of Rotterdam's 19 points (T2-T9), with the caveats that the landlords shut out there were small
  private owners (T2-T13, C-C10) and that the government's evaluation found the effect only where
  cities adopted early, before Q2 2023 (T2-T15, not independent).
- **On rents: do big landlords name the rent? Locally they nudge it; nationally they don't set it.**
  Where they cluster, the first lease after they buy carries a markup of a few points (T4-21) and
  concentration lifts rents a little (T4-23, T4-25, C-C5); two studies find rents grew faster where they
  moved in (Gorback-Qian-Zhu, C-C1; Hanson, consistent with markups, C-B5, C-B7). But the markup shrinks
  where renters have choices (T4-24), Barbieri-Dobbels find their buying lowered rents on net in Atlanta
  (C-C4), the later Lambie-Hanson paper finds no significant rent effect through 2014 (C-A16), GAO calls
  the record mixed (T4-27), and their own rent growth sits at or below the national pace
  (company-reported, T4-20). What **this** ban does to rents nobody has measured: no paper simulates a
  future-purchase-only ban (C-A1), no US ban has run long enough (T4-31), and beside that, the one place
  that tried a purchase ban reports no rent result in its latest version (T2-T12); its authors' renter
  line is a warning, not a measurement (T2-T12). Say "untested", never "contested by the Dutch
  evidence".
- **On wages: the viewers are right.** A typical single paycheck can't carry the typical home, and the
  typical household falls well short on standard terms (T3-6, T3-11; our arithmetic); the gap opened
  over decades (T3-17). Not "nobody can afford a home" (T3-15, T3-16), not "rents outran wages"
  (T3-24–T3-26), and no cause is assigned.
- **On building:** builders face measured cost and credit headwinds and are building smaller but fewer
  (T5-8, T5-17, T5-24, T5-33); the Act's own building pieces are slow and carry no new money
  (T5-36–T5-38). Nothing ranks building against investors.
- ⛔ Do not write past those caveats: no paper in this record simulates a **future-purchase-only** ban
  (C-A1); the Dutch study is an unpublished working paper (no journal version found), with a window of
  about one year to 1 January 2023, and Rotterdam funded the authors' data access (T2 notes); its
  versions are now reconciled (T2).

## The reframe

**The shouting gets the question wrong.** "Will it make homes cheaper?" has a fairly clear answer on
the record: probably not by much, and not nationally. The real question is **who the next house goes
to**: a family who buys it, or a family who rents it from a landlord, big or small. The ban picks the
buyer. The fight is over what that costs the renter: big landlords nudge rents up where they cluster
but don't set them nationally (T4-21, T4-24, T4-20), the record splits on what their buying did to
rents overall (C-C1, C-B7 against C-C4), and there is **no measurement** of what a ban on their future
purchases does to rent, here or in the one place that tried one (C-A1, T4-31, T2-T12). The honest line
is "nobody has tested it", which is why it is the viewer's call.

A second, smaller reframe: **the law is wider than its one famous section.** The same Act carries
supply measures: HUD zoning guidelines (sec. 107, guidance only, due by 2029-07-11, our arithmetic),
NEPA streamlining for small infill (sec. 206, by rulemaking with no deadline), a 10% cut in
community-development block grants for cities with below-median housing growth (sec. 213, from fiscal
2029), manufactured homes without a permanent chassis (sec. 301), and it authorizes **no new money**
(sec. 1202) (D-E1–D-E6, T5-36–T5-38). A viewer who reads the Act should not think the episode hid half
of it.

## Both sides quietly agree

**Neither side's best evidence says this ban moves national prices.** The pro-ban studies find their
effects where big landlords concentrated (Atlanta and a handful of Sun Belt metros), and their own
strongest paper notes that only about 2,100 of its roughly 30,000 tracts ever saw the rental companies
reach a 1-point share (C-C2); the anti-ban model concedes a real effect in exactly those places (C-A9).
Both put the action in a few metros and a few ZIP codes. No US ban has been measured (T4-31), and the
one place that has actually tried a purchase ban did not see prices fall (T2-T8, T2-T10), which Green
concedes and Gold cites.

What they do **not** agree on is the renter. The two structural models in the record find a trade,
from opposite directions: the purchases that cost homebuyers helped renters on net (Barbieri-Dobbels:
prices up about 3.2%, rents down, C-C3, C-C4), and removing the big landlords lowers prices but raises
rents (Coven, C-A2, C-A3). But two reduced-form studies find big landlords pushed rents **up** where
they moved in (C-C1, C-B5, C-B7), which would mean no trade at all, and no study has measured what a
ban on future purchases does to rent (C-A1, T2-T12). So whether the trade exists is part of the
fight, not common ground.

⚠ Do **not** say "both agree supply and rates matter far more": no study ranks them against investors
(D-A1), and how much zoning explains is itself contested by top researchers (D-A2, D-C7, D-C8). The
same ruling runs the other way: ⛔ never "rising prices and rents all trace back to corporations buying
houses". No study in this record attributes national prices or rents to them (T4-20, T4-27, D-A1), and
none ranks investors against rates, the shortage, remote work or zoning.

## Verdict framing

No winner. The judge names the trade and hands the viewer **one dial: who should the next starter home
go to?**

- **"The buyer first":** keep the ban as written. Behind it, in the US: the biggest owners' buying cut
  homeownership in Coven's model (C-A10), and cash wins on terms a mortgaged family can't match (C-D2).
  Beside it, the one place that has tried a purchase ban: first-time buyers took most of the homes
  investors stopped buying (T2-T9). Its documented failure: prices did not fall there (T2-T8, T2-T10);
  the effect showed only in cities that adopted early (T2-T15); and the authors warn it may cut housing
  opportunities for lower-income renters (T2-T12). Its documented limits: the landlords it shut out
  were small private owners, not firms like the ones this law covers (T2-T13), and no US ban has been
  measured (T4-31).
- **"The buyer, but keep building rentals":** the ban with its build-to-rent door open, which is what
  the Treasury is proposing (A-D4). Behind it: the Act's own exceptions. Its documented risk:
  build-to-rent starts were already falling, partly because of the bill itself (B-D1, B-A9), and
  whether those homes add to the stock is untested (B-D6).
- **"The renter first":** no purchase ban; police conduct instead. Behind it: the FTC's settlement
  over alleged fees (C-F2, C-F3), research on concentrated and first-re-let rent markups (C-C5, C-B7,
  T4-21), and the conduct route the government already used on rent-setting software, in the apartment
  market (RealPage's settlement is conduct rules, T4-7). Its documented limit: the FTC matter was
  settled on allegations, not findings (C-F2), robust research on fees, maintenance and evictions is
  limited (C-A21), and it does nothing for the buyer who lost the house on terms (C-D2, C-D3).
The viewer sets it. **Three settings only** (Lucas's ruling 2026-10-27: "Make build instead a closing caveat,
not a dial position").

**Closing caveat, after the dial: "whichever you pick, it's still a fight over too few houses."** The
dial decides who gets the next starter home; it doesn't make more of them. Say it briefly, with the
measured facts: builders are already building smaller (T5-24, T5-25) but starting fewer (T5-17), what
goes into a house costs about half again what it did in 2020 (T5-8), builders borrow at 10–13% (T5-33),
and only about one new home in six sold under $300,000 (T5-27). The same Act has building pieces, but
they are slow and unfunded: guidance by 2029, a grant rule from fiscal 2029, no new money (T5-36, T5-38),
and nobody has estimated how many homes they add (T5-39). ⛔ Never "building would fix it", never rank
supply against investors, and if the regulation share airs, it is the builders' trade group's own
estimate (T5-5); how much zoning explains is contested (D-A2, D-C7, D-C8).

**A comment window is open until November 23** (A-A5): say it as process ("the rule
is open for public comment until November 23"), never as a call to argue one side.
