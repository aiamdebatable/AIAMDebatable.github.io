## The research · What the ROAD to Housing Act does, who it covers, and your lease
**Lena:** Start with what the law actually does. It's the ROAD to Housing Act, and it became law in July without the President's signature. From January seventh, anyone who controls three hundred and fifty or more single-family homes nationwide can't buy another one.
**Cole:** So do they have to sell the ones they already own?
**Lena:** No. Homes they owned before the law count toward the three hundred and fifty, so the biggest landlords are covered from day one, but nothing forces them to sell. The ban runs fifteen years, and breaking it can cost up to a million dollars per violation, or three times the price, whichever is more.
**Cole:** And the loopholes everyone talks about?
**Lena:** There are about ten kinds of exception, among them homes built to rent, homes fixed up to rent, foreclosures, and buying from another big investor. In October the Treasury proposed its rule, which would widen two of those routes. It's open for public comment until November twenty-third.
**Cole:** How many companies are we even talking about?
**Lena:** The Treasury's own words: reliable data are not currently available regarding how many institutional investors and other entities may be affected. Three private estimates put the owners at that line at about six or seven of every thousand single-family homes in the country.
**Cole:** Six in a thousand? Then why does it feel like they bought the whole street?
**Lena:** Because they bunched up. In metro Atlanta in twenty twenty-two, owners of a thousand or more homes held about a quarter of the rented single-family houses. In Jacksonville, the very biggest firms held about one in five in twenty twenty-four, up from seven percent in twenty eighteen.
**Cole:** Of the rented houses, or of all the houses?
**Lena:** Of the rented ones. Of all single-family homes, in the six metros the government's auditors studied, it was three percent at most.
**Cole:** Say I rent from one of these companies. What changes for me?
**Lena:** Your lease doesn't. The ban stops big investors buying; it has no rule on your rent, your renewal or evictions. What you do get, if your landlord owns three hundred and fifty homes or more, is a federal complaint line they must tell you about when you move in and every year.
**Cole:** Is this the first place to try it?
**Lena:** No. Michigan's own ban has been in force since July, for firms with more than a hundred homes in the state and three hundred and seventy-five million dollars or more behind them. Whether the federal law overrides it hasn't been tested.

**Lena:** Not the way people mean it. They nudge it where they cluster. On the first lease after a big firm buys a home, a Philadelphia Fed working paper finds rent rises a few points more than an average rise of about six percent, and only a little faster after that.

**Lena:** On that first lease, where they cluster, and the markup shrinks where renters have other homes to choose from. And the biggest firm cut rents on new leases three quarters running, through early twenty twenty-six, by its own reports, while rents for tenants who stayed kept rising.
**Cole:** So what did their buying do to rent overall?
**Lena:** The research points both ways. Two studies find rents grew faster where they moved in. A study of Atlanta finds their buying lowered rents on net, because they added rentals. The government's auditors say some studies find they may have contributed to higher rents, and others found more limited effects.
**Cole:** And the rent algorithm? Isn't that these same companies?
**Lena:** No. The rent-algorithm case is about apartment buildings. The Justice Department and several states sued RealPage, alleging it pooled competing apartment landlords' private data. **Cole:** Then what does a ban on their buying do to rent?
