ColeMaybe you're saving for a down payment, or dreading your next lease renewal. You've heard that Wall Street is buying up your street. You've also heard that this law aims at the wrong buyer.

ColeSo which is it: does keeping the biggest buyers out help you, and who carries the cost?

The research · What the ROAD to Housing Act does, who it covers, and your lease

LenaStart with what the law actually does. It's the ROAD to Housing Act, and it became law in July without the President's signature. From January seventh, anyone who controls three hundred and fifty or more single-family homes nationwide can't buy another one.

ColeSo do they have to sell the ones they already own?

LenaNo. Homes they owned before the law count toward the three hundred and fifty, so the biggest landlords are covered from day one, but nothing forces them to sell. The ban runs fifteen years, and breaking it can cost up to a million dollars per violation, or three times the price, whichever is more.

ColeAnd the loopholes everyone talks about?

LenaThere are about ten kinds of exception, among them homes built to rent, homes fixed up to rent, foreclosures, and buying from another big investor. In October the Treasury proposed its rule, which would widen two of those routes. It's open for public comment until November twenty-third.

ColeHow many companies are we even talking about?

LenaThe Treasury's own words: reliable data are not currently available regarding how many institutional investors and other entities may be affected. Three private estimates put the owners at that line at about six or seven of every thousand single-family homes in the country.

ColeSix in a thousand? Then why does it feel like they bought the whole street?

LenaBecause they bunched up. In metro Atlanta in twenty twenty-two, owners of a thousand or more homes held about a quarter of the rented single-family houses. In Jacksonville, the very biggest firms held about one in five in twenty twenty-four, up from seven percent in twenty eighteen.

ColeOf the rented houses, or of all the houses?

LenaOf the rented ones. Of all single-family homes, in the six metros the government's auditors studied, it was three percent at most.

ColeSay I rent from one of these companies. What changes for me?

LenaYour lease doesn't. The ban stops big investors buying; it has no rule on your rent, your renewal or evictions. What you do get, if your landlord owns three hundred and fifty homes or more, is a federal complaint line they must tell you about when you move in and every year.

ColeIs this the first place to try it?

LenaNo. Michigan's own ban has been in force since July, for firms with more than a hundred homes in the state and three hundred and seventy-five million dollars or more behind them. Whether the federal law overrides it hasn't been tested.

LenaOn the record so far, probably not measurably, and not nationally. And here's the part nobody expects: since about twenty twenty-three, the biggest landlords have been selling slightly more homes than they buy. That started before this law existed.

LenaPartly. In the spring quarter, Invitation Homes, the biggest, sold more than three homes for every one it bought, by its own report, and it pointed to market prices, not the law. AMH, another of the biggest, plans no ordinary purchases this year; it grows by building its own.

ColeAnd when they sell, who buys? Another landlord?

LenaOften a family. In the six metros the government's auditors followed, from twenty nineteen to twenty twenty-four, roughly four to six in every ten homes the very biggest owners sold went to people who moved in.

ColeSo who's buying instead of them now?

LenaSmaller investors. One data firm found the mega investors' share of purchases roughly halved around the turn of the year, while landlords with just a handful of homes bought more. By another firm's count, small investors made about two-thirds of investor purchases in the first half of this year.

ColeBut did the big ones ever push prices up?

LenaWhere they bunched up, a little. One model of Georgia finds that in the markets where they bought the most, their buying added about six percent, close to nineteen thousand dollars on a three-hundred-thousand-dollar home. A study of metro Atlanta finds about three percent, roughly seventeen thousand dollars.

ColeAnd everywhere else?

LenaIn most places they entered, that same model finds the price rise wasn't their doing, and most of these studies cover the years around the crash. And no ban in the US has been measured yet: Michigan's only began in July, and the federal one hasn't started.

ColeThen has anyone, anywhere, actually tried it?

LenaOne place: the Netherlands, from twenty twenty-two, where cities could bar investors from buying cheaper homes to rent out. In Rotterdam's banned neighborhoods, investor purchases dropped by about three quarters, and first-time buyers took most of those homes. Prices didn't fall; if anything, there was a slight, short-lived rise.

ColeSo the homes went to buyers, and prices held?

LenaWith limits. The landlords shut out there were mostly small private owners, not firms like the ones this law covers, and the Dutch government's own evaluation found the shift only in cities that adopted it early. Here, the closest number is that four to six in ten.

ColeAnd the cash offer that beat our buyer?

LenaThat's how investors tend to win: on terms, not on price. Buyers with a mortgage pay about eleven percent more than all-cash buyers for comparable homes. But most investors of every size pay cash, and owners with plenty of equity do too, so this ban removes only one kind of cash bidder.

The research · Do big landlords name the rent? The first-lease markup, RealPage, and what nobody has measured

LenaNot the way people mean it. They nudge it where they cluster. On the first lease after a big firm buys a home, a Philadelphia Fed working paper finds rent rises a few points more than an average rise of about six percent, and only a little faster after that.

ColeA few points, every time they buy?

LenaOn that first lease, where they cluster, and the markup shrinks where renters have other homes to choose from. And the biggest firm cut rents on new leases three quarters running, through early twenty twenty-six, by its own reports, while rents for tenants who stayed kept rising.

ColeSo what did their buying do to rent overall?

LenaThe research points both ways. Two studies find rents grew faster where they moved in. A study of Atlanta finds their buying lowered rents on net, because they added rentals. The government's auditors say some studies find they may have contributed to higher rents, and others found more limited effects.

ColeAnd the rent algorithm? Isn't that these same companies?

LenaNo. The rent-algorithm case is about apartment buildings. The Justice Department and several states sued RealPage, alleging it pooled competing apartment landlords' private data. No big single-family landlord is a defendant. RealPage settled with the Justice Department on conduct rules, and nothing has been proven at trial.

ColeThen what does a ban on their buying do to rent?

LenaNobody has measured it. No study simulates a ban on future purchases only, and no US ban has run long enough. One model of forcing them out finds prices fall but rents rise, and this law forces nobody out.

ColeAnd the one place that tried a purchase ban?

LenaThe Netherlands reports no rent figure in its latest study, and its authors warn the ban may cut housing opportunities for low-income renters. That's a warning, not a measurement. So on rent, the firmest number we have for the US is that few-point markup on a first lease.

ColeWhat about new homes built just to rent?

LenaThat door stays open: building homes to rent is one of the exceptions. But build-to-rent starts fell about a fifth last year, to under seventy thousand, and the builders' trade group names an earlier version of the bill among the causes, along with financing costs.

The verdict

WaltNo winner here. I'll give you what both teams accepted, what held up on each side, and then the choice that's left to you.

WaltWhat both teams accepted: from January, anyone controlling three hundred and fifty or more family homes can't buy another, and nobody has to sell. Nationally they hold about six or seven in a thousand, and they were already selling. No US ban has been measured yet.

WaltWhat held up for green. Where big landlords cluster, they nudged prices up, about six percent at most in one model, and the first lease after they buy carries a markup of a few points. And they win on terms a family with a mortgage can't match.

WaltWhat held up for gold. The group is small and already shrinking, small investors now do most investor buying, and the one place that tried a purchase ban didn't see prices fall. What a ban on future purchases does to rent, nobody has measured.

WaltWhat didn't hold up. That this ban will make homes cheaper nationally. That rising prices and rents all trace back to corporations: no study here shows that. That big landlords set the rent; they nudge it locally. That rents rose faster than pay since twenty nineteen. And that the rent-algorithm case is about these landlords.

WaltSo here's where the record leans. On cheaper homes: no, not measurably. On who gets the house: a ban can tilt homes toward people who live in them, with limits. On rent: untested.

WaltThe dial: who gets the next starter home? Buyer first keeps the ban as written; where a ban was tried, prices didn't fall. Buyer, but keep building rentals, is the Treasury's proposal; those starts were already falling. Renter first drops the ban and polices conduct, which does nothing for the buyer who lost on terms.

WaltAnd whichever you pick, it's still a fight over too few houses. Builders are building smaller homes but starting fewer, what goes into a house costs about half again what it did in twenty twenty, and only about one new home in six sold for under three hundred thousand dollars last year.
