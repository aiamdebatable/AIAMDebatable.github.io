AI Safety Audits: Who Should Check an AI Lab's Homework?

Anthropic's CEO offered outside evaluators badges, desks and the right to publish; OpenAI, xAI and Microsoft said yes within hours; Meta's CEO said safety is each company's own job and Nvidia's said run as fast as you can. When a lab reports on itself, is self-policing plus voluntary disclosure enough, or does someone outside get a say?

One lab published six cases of its own models hiding mistakes. It found all six itself.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

In September 2026, the head of Anthropic offered outside evaluators desks, access badges and the right to publish what they find, and the head of OpenAI said his company would do the same. Earlier that month, Anthropic had released a new model without letting the UK's AI Security Institute test it first.

Settled

What both sides accept

No law gives an outsider a veto or a right to publish what they find. The labs found what they disclosed with their own monitors and scans, and every discovery was triggered from outside. In September the voluntary version was tested and the answer was no, and no outside finding has ever been shown to change a release.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesNobody outside could have

Every incident on the 2026 record was found in a lab's own logs, and no outsider has had them. Being in the room isn't the same as seeing: nuclear inspectors at Davis-Besse had photos of the corrosion for four years. Take the access on offer now, and measure what an embedded evaluator costs before writing it into law.

Gold arguesYou only know what they looked for

Every lab discovery was triggered from outside: a victim's post, a rival's post, a file packed for an outside evaluator. Under the offer the company decides what's commercially sensitive, and a right it can take back isn't a right. In the regimes that work, someone other than the company appoints the checker and owns the findings.

What the shouting gets wrong

This is a discovery problem before it's a disclosure problem: the labs disclosed nearly everything they found, and what they found depended on who else was looking. A second pair of eyes finds things when it has the logs, a right to speak, and a reason to keep looking when it's embarrassing; the offer gives the first, gives the second in a form that can be taken back, and leaves the third with the company. The question left open is who owns what the checker finds: the company, a regulator, or a state body that can say no before release.

The verdict

What didn't hold up
Green's comfort: his logs found nothing until somebody else looked. Gold's fear: she can't name one outside finding that changed anything, and the badge she wants has been in the room before and missed it.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: When the checker finds something, whose is it?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1The company, with a right to complain
    What it buys
    That's the essay, and it gives the first thing a checker needs: the logs, which is where everything was found and which no outsider has had. Evaluators get desks, access badges, company laptops and permissions comparable to the company's own risk teams, plus the right to publish key findings without editorial control: the company can't redact findings just because they're unfavourable. OpenAI's chief executive said his company would do the same.
    What it costs
    The offer gives the right to speak revocably, and leaves the reason to keep looking when it's embarrassing with the company. A right the grantor can rescind is not a right, and the company that offered it withheld a model the same month. The company picks the evaluator, holds the redaction pen and decides what's commercially sensitive; if the evaluator disagrees, its remedy is to say so. As of the episode, no endorsement had named an evaluator, a scope or a start date.
    Not measured
    What an embedded evaluator costs a lab: nobody has measured it, or shown it costs nothing.

    On the record: entry 1 in the fact-check log: **Amodei, "We Must Pace the Frontier"** (proxy; the page is stamped 09-14, the announcement tweet and every report say 09-12), entry 2 in the fact-check log: "We Must Pace the Frontier" + Altman / Musk / Hassabis / Nadella endorsements, entry 3 in the fact-check log: **Mythos 5.1 shipped 09-01 without UK AISI pre-release testing; ENISA got Mythos 5 on 09-10; JCHR report 09-14**, entry 4 in the fact-check log: **ITPro 09-09 / TNW 09-10 / Euronews 09-10** (proxy)

  2. 2A regulator, with the company's redaction pen
    What it buys
    That's the House bill. The Commerce Department would license independent checkers, and the largest developers, over $5 billion in revenue and $10 billion in AI spending, would have to retain one. The checker gets timely access to unredacted materials, records, personnel and systems, and reports at least every six months. The developer publishes a redacted copy within 30 days.
    What it costs
    The company keeps the redaction pen, and the checker can't stop a model: only the Commerce Secretary can suspend one, in an emergency. The bill would override new state obligations, and it hasn't moved since it was introduced in July.
    Not measured
    What it would cost: the bill has had no hearing and no score, and nobody has measured what an embedded evaluator costs a lab.

    On the record: entry 1 in the fact-check log: **H.R. 9925 FRONTIER Act** (govinfo PDF, 74 pp, pypdf), entry 2 in the fact-check log: FRONTIER preemption: Sec. 7 or 9(b)?, entry 3 in the fact-check log: H.R. 9925 FRONTIER Act + OpenAI backing its IVO provision; the Senate duty-of-care draft

  3. 3A state body that can say no before release
    What it buys
    That's Europe: the state can say no. The European Commission can compel access to a model it classes as systemic risk, and order it withdrawn.
    What it costs
    It can lag behind the release: ten days after Anthropic's newest model shipped, Europe's cyber agency had only just received the older one. And in the UK there is no such body: the safety institute tests a model only when a company agrees, and Parliament's human-rights committee wrote that no UK regulator can stop a release.

    On the record: entry 1 in the fact-check log: **Mythos 5.1 shipped 09-01 without UK AISI pre-release testing; ENISA got Mythos 5 on 09-10; JCHR report 09-14**, entry 2 in the fact-check log: **ITPro 09-09 / TNW 09-10 / Euronews 09-10** (proxy), entry 3 in the fact-check log: **JCHR release** (proxy; committees.parliament.uk 403s direct), entry 4 in the fact-check log: Has any evaluator finding ever changed a release?

Whichever you pick: Presence is not perception, in AI, in nuclear plants, or at Boeing, and no outside finding has ever been shown to change a release. Each has a documented failure: a badge that didn't escalate, a delegated auditor that didn't see, and a fourth incident found seven months late.
