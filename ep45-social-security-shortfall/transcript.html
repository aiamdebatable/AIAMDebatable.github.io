ErinOn paper and in law, yes. At the end of last year the trust fund held about two and a half trillion dollars, all of it in special-issue Treasury bonds, sold only to the fund and backed by the full faith and credit of the United States.

WaltSo where did the actual cash go?

ErinIt was spent, the year it came in. The cash the fund doesn't need right away goes into the Treasury's general fund and pays for other spending, the way the law builds it. The bonds are the receipt: the general fund owes every dollar back, with interest.

WaltSo did Congress steal it, or not?

ErinThe money was spent, and it is owed, and both are true. It's being paid back right now: the fund cashes bonds every day to pay benefits, and it has shrunk every year since twenty twenty-one, because cost now tops all its income.

WaltThen what's the catch?

ErinTo cash those bonds, the rest of the government has to raise taxes, cut other spending, or borrow. That's the critics' strongest point, and the White House budget office itself made it. It's an economic point, not a legal one: the debt is still owed.

WaltPeople write in: it's my money, they can't touch it.

ErinMorally, plenty of people feel that way. Legally, the Supreme Court said in nineteen sixty that there's no property right to a benefit, so Congress can change the rules, though not in a patently arbitrary way.

WaltAnd the new baby accounts? People call them a back door to privatizing it.

ErinLast year's tax law created children's accounts: a traditional I R A with a single thousand-dollar Treasury deposit, for citizen children born from twenty twenty-five through twenty twenty-eight, if someone signs them up. It redirects no payroll tax and changes no Social Security benefit.

ErinThe back-door line came from the Treasury Secretary, and Treasury called the accounts additive the same day. The worry is about some future law, not this one.

WaltOkay. And the letter that came this month?

ErinBoth changes are automatic. Checks rise three point six percent in January, about seventy-five dollars a month for the average retiree, before any change in the Medicare premium, which isn't out yet. And the cap on taxed pay rises from about a hundred and eighty-five thousand dollars to about a hundred and ninety-two thousand.

WaltDoes either one help the gap?

ErinNo. The raise follows prices and the cap follows wages, on autopilot, and the tax rate stays six point two percent each for you and your employer, so neither change touches the shortfall.

WaltDidn't Congress just change the retirement age, too?

ErinNo. Congress has passed a bill that renames several terms Social Security uses: full retirement age, for one, would become the standard monthly benefit age. It changes no age and no benefit.

ErinThis June's Trustees Report puts it in late twenty thirty-two for the retirement fund, a little earlier than last year's report. After that, the taxes still coming in cover about seventy-eight percent of scheduled benefits, and less over time.

ErinEveryone's: all scheduled retirement benefits, today's retirees included, not just new ones. Count the disability fund in, and it's late twenty thirty-four at eighty-three percent, but the funds can't be merged without a new law.

ErinNobody knows, because the law doesn't say. The Congressional Research Service says both are possible: full checks paid late, or smaller checks on time. People would still be legally owed the full amount and could sue, and the fund can't borrow. The Chief Actuary calls a cut the default if nobody acts.

WaltHow big is the hole, in plain terms?

ErinHad Congress fixed it all this January with tax alone, the payroll rate would go from twelve point four percent, you and your employer together, to about sixteen and two-thirds: about a third more. With cuts alone, it's about a quarter off every check, today's and future ones.

WaltAnd if they wait?

ErinIt gets bigger. Start in twenty thirty-four, and it's nearly five points on the rate, or nearly twenty-nine percent off every check. Every year of delay raises the price of every lever.

WaltWhy did it get worse this year?

ErinMostly because the Trustees now assume fewer births, then lower immigration. About a quarter came from last year's tax law, which lowered the income tax on benefits that partly funds the program. That law changed no benefit and no payroll tax.

The verdict

RajThis one doesn't resolve into a winning lever, and neither team wins it. Three things from me: what's settled, what survived each side, and the dial that's yours.

RajThe record both teams accepted: the money was spent, and it is owed. From late twenty thirty-two, taxes coming in cover about seventy-eight cents on the dollar on every retirement check, and the law doesn't say how. Fixing it takes a third more payroll tax or a quarter off every check, and waiting raises both.

RajWhat survived from green. The cap eroded, and the Chief Actuary names that, not longer lives, as a main reason. The payroll rate is the only single lever that closes the whole gap. And a higher age does nothing for twenty thirty-two, and the longer lives it counts on went mostly to the top.

RajWhat survived from gold. People live longer at sixty-five than in nineteen eighty-three. A later age tied to longevity closes about a third with no new tax. And the cap alone closes fifty-eight percent at most, so money in has to mean the rate as well, and that lands on every worker's pay.

RajWhat fell. Social Security isn't going broke; it will run short, and it wasn't stolen without being owed. The law doesn't cut checks automatically. People don't just work longer, a later age doesn't fix twenty thirty-two, the cap alone doesn't fix it, neither lever touches only the rich, and Congress hasn't moved the retirement age.

RajSo here's what the record supports. Doing nothing is the harshest option, because the default reaches today's retirees too. Every serious source points to a mix, or one very large single move. And no package has been scored on this year's numbers.

RajA single dial is left: how much comes from money in, how much from money out, and on whom. All money in, like the rate alone, costs a third more tax or more. Both is what nineteen eighty-three did. Mostly money out means a later age and a slower formula, cutting future checks.

RajIf nobody turns it, every retirement check comes up about a fifth short from late twenty thirty-two. Your full retirement age is sixty-seven if you were born in nineteen sixty or later, and claiming at sixty-two costs thirty percent. How much in, how much out, and on whom is a dial only you can set.
