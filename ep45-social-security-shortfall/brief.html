Episode 45 · The research

Research brief — Social Security's Shortfall: Raise the Age, Lift the Cap, or Trim Benefits?

The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video.

Episode type: DEBATE, with a design-style verdict. Two teams argue at full strength; the verdict names what survives both and hands the viewer one dial. Why design-style, decided from the record: no single lever on the table closes the gap except an equally large single move (a payroll-tax rise of about 4.25 points, or about a quarter off every check), and the Chief Actuary herself frames the choice as a split, "raise program income by about one-third, lower scheduled benefits by about one-fourth, or adopt a combination" (log 29, 31, 36). The levers are a mix question, so a winner would be false. The judge may name the dial the research earned. The judge never sets it.

Scope, said early on air: this is about how to close Social Security's retirement-and-disability financing gap: raise the full retirement age, raise or remove the cap on taxed wages, trim the benefit formula or the yearly raise, or combine them. It is not about Medicare, SSI or whether the program should exist. Private accounts get one short factual beat only, because viewers asked: what the 2025 children's accounts are and are not (log 367–376). It is not a debate on privatization. ⚠ "Runs out" is the wrong picture: payroll taxes keep coming in, and the program runs short (log 51, 81). ⚠ "Raise the retirement age" is a benefit cut, not a rule about when people may stop working: claiming still opens at 62 (log 25, 106).

The pitch, in one line

Is the money there? It was spent, and it is owed: both are true. The trust fund holds about $2.56 trillion of Treasury bonds the rest of the government must pay back, and it is shrinking (log 320, 321, 331, 337, 340). Doing nothing is also a choice, and it is the one that cuts today's retirees' checks across the board, all at once. (A smaller yearly raise, chained CPI, also reaches everyone on the rolls, but slowly: log 259, 260.) If Congress does nothing, in late 2032 incoming taxes cover about 78 cents of every dollar of scheduled retirement benefits, all of them, not just new retirees', and the law does not even say whether the checks would come smaller or come late (log 50, 51, 75, 78, 80). Each lever on the table moves part of that gap onto someone in particular: a higher age onto future retirees, a higher cap onto the top earners of any year, a slower raise onto the oldest, a higher rate onto every worker (log 105, 200, 259, 29). So the real setting is not "which lever". It is: how much of the gap comes from more money in, how much from less money out, and on whom.

The viewer's moment

It starts with the question under every comment: is the money there? The trust fund holds about $2.56 trillion, every dollar in special-issue Treasury bonds backed by the full faith and credit of the United States (log 320, 323, 324). The cash Social Security lent was spent on other government spending when it came in; the bonds are what the government owes back, with interest, and it cashes them every day to pay benefits (log 328, 331, 332, 337, 338). Both are true: to pay them, the rest of the government has to tax, cut or borrow (log 334, 340). And once the bonds run out, the law doesn't say whether checks would come smaller or late (log 75, 78).

Then four people, and the viewer is one of them. (Invented and unnamed. Each is built only from figures in the log.)

  • The 60-year-old in a physical job. Born in 1966, so her full retirement age is already 67, and she reaches it in 2033, the first full year after the retirement fund runs short (log 24, 50; our arithmetic on the birth year). Claiming at 62 already costs her 30% (log 25). Among Americans aged 60–61, more than a quarter said health limited their ability to work in a 1998–2008 survey (log 146, 148). A higher full retirement age would not stop her claiming at 62; it would make that check smaller again (log 105, 106).
  • The 35-year-old reading a paycheck. 6.2% comes out, and the same again from the employer, on pay up to $184,500 this year and $192,300 next (log 9, 10, 12). The all-tax fix would take roughly $1,600 a year, by our own arithmetic: an average earner's own half of the 4.25-point rise that fixes it if it starts January 2026 (log 29, 285; on screen, "our estimate"). A cap change would cost that earner no more tax at all (log 231, 283).
  • The 80-year-old. The yearly raise is the whole defence against prices, and the people who rely on Social Security most are the oldest (log 263, a 2015 survey figure, used only as a direction). A slower yearly raise takes more the longer you live: about 4% less by 75, 6.5% by 85, 9% by 95 (log 259, 260).
  • ★ The hook nobody expects: the retiree who did nothing wrong. The cut everyone pictures for 2032 is not on future retirees alone: the 78% is of all scheduled benefits, today's retirees' included, and the Chief Actuary calls the cut "the default" (log 51, 80). And nothing in the law says how it would be done (log 75, 78).

The episode follows one worker: SSA's own "medium earner", who earns about the national average wage ($75,247 in 2026), turns 65 in 2035, and is scheduled to get $34,975 a year at 67, in today's dollars (log 283, 284). The desk puts each lever's price on her.

The question

Social Security's trust fund runs short: raise the retirement age, lift the tax cap, or trim benefits? Under it sits the question the record forces: the gap is about a third more payroll tax or about a quarter off every check; who should carry it, and how soon?

Why now

Peg hunt run 2026-10-21 (peg-hunt skill, all five phases), then every peg fact re-checked at the primary by hand pass A (log 1–26). Re-run the hunt before scripting and on the day it posts.

The hot peg (description): the 2027 numbers, announced 2026-10-14. The Social Security Administration announced a 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment for 2027: nearly 72 million Social Security beneficiaries get it from January 2027, about $75 a month for the average retiree, and about 7.3 million SSI recipients from 2026-12-31 (log 1–4; SSA press release, 2026-10-14, https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/press/releases/2026-10-14.html). ⚠ The same release's "76 million" counts SSI too: never mix it with "nearly 72 million" (log 5). The figure is set by formula, not by a vote: the third-quarter average of the CPI-W rose from 317.265 (2025) to 328.746 (2026) (log 6; SSA Office of the Chief Actuary, https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/latestCOLA.html). The average retired worker's check goes from about $2,095 to about $2,170 (log 7). ⚠ The 2027 Medicare Part B premium, which nets against the raise for many, is not out until late November (log 8). In the same automatic round, the cap on wages subject to the Social Security payroll tax rises from $184,500 (2026) to $192,300 (2027), because it follows the national average wage; the rate stays 6.2% each for worker and employer (log 9–12; https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/cbb.html).

Why it fits the thesis. Two things the episode argues about moved this month, both on autopilot: the yearly raise followed prices and the tax cap followed wages. Neither touches the gap. The 2026 Trustees Report (released 2026-06-09, log 59) still projects the retirement (OASI) fund pays in full only until the fourth quarter of 2032, one quarter earlier than last year, after which incoming revenue pays 78% of scheduled benefits; with the disability fund counted in, the third quarter of 2034 at 83% (log 50, 51, 54, 55; https://www.ssa.gov/oact/TRSUM/). The viewer searching "social security changes" gets the answer: this year's changes were automatic, and the choice between the levers has not been made.

The structural peg (script): the retirement age, renamed but not moved. The Claiming Age Clarity Act (H.R. 5284) passed the House by voice vote on 2025-12-01 and the Senate without amendment by unanimous consent on 2026-09-29; its latest action (2026-10-14) is a message on the Senate action sent to the House, and it is not among the public laws published through 2026-10-15, so it is passed, not yet signed as far as the record shows (log 13–16; https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5284/all-actions). ⚠ Corrected by hand pass A: it does not rename the ages themselves. It replaces terms in SSA's own rules, guidance and materials: "early eligibility age" becomes "minimum monthly benefit age", "full" or "normal retirement age" becomes "standard monthly benefit age", "delayed retirement credit" is dropped and the age-70 cap becomes the "maximum monthly benefit age". It does not amend the Social Security Act, names no age except 70, and changes no benefit (log 18–21, 23; https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr5284enr/html/BILLS-119hr5284enr.htm). It sits beside the last real move of the age: everyone born in 1960 or later already has a full retirement age of 67, and claiming at 62 cuts their benefit by 30% (log 24, 25). ⚠ Do not say the act is law until the public record shows it signed; re-check on the day the script locks and the day it posts (log 16).

Sourcing note. ssa.gov refuses direct fetching; SSA's pages were read through a text-reader proxy (SSA's own text), and the full Trustees Report from a web-archive copy of SSA's PDF. The release date, 2026-06-09, rests on CRFB's same-day analysis and the PDF's server timestamp (log 59).

Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

The size and the clock (2026 Trustees Report unless named)

  • The 75-year gap is 4.42% of taxable payroll, about 1.5% of GDP, up from 3.82% last year (log 27, 28). In the Trustees' own dollars, $29.3 trillion over 75 years in today's money (log 41).
  • Fixing it at once takes a 4.25-point payroll-tax rise (12.4% to 16.65%, worker and employer together) or a 25.2% cut to every current and future check (log 29, 31). About a third more tax, or about a quarter off (log 30, 36). Waiting makes both bigger: 4.9 points or 28.5% if it starts in 2034 (log 33, 34).
  • The retirement fund (OASI) pays in full until late 2032, then about 78%, falling to 62% by 2100. Counting the disability fund too, late 2034, 83%, falling to 65% (log 50–56). The two funds cannot be merged without a new law (log 57). The disability fund alone pays in full through 2100 (log 58).
  • The date is a poor gauge of the gap. The deficit has more than doubled since the 2010 report (1.92% to 4.42%) while the combined date has sat in 2033–2035 in every report since 2012 (log 44, 63).
  • CBO is gloomier on size, not on the date. It puts OASI exhaustion in fiscal 2032 (within a quarter of the Trustees), a 26% first-year cut, a 4.57% gap, and the combined funds in fiscal 2033 (log 65–69). Its figures are its February 2026 baseline (log 70).

What happens at depletion

  • The law does not say. CRS: full checks paid late or reduced checks on time are both possible; beneficiaries stay legally entitled to full benefits and could sue; the Antideficiency Act bars spending beyond available funds; it is unclear what SSA would do (log 75–78). The trust funds cannot borrow (log 81, 82). The Chief Actuary calls the roughly one-fifth cut "the default" if no one acts (log 80). ⛔ Never "the law automatically cuts checks to 78%" (log 74).

What moved this year's numbers

  • Mostly lower assumed birth rates (the largest single item, −0.33 of the −0.60), then lower immigration, then the 2025 tax law's cut to income tax on benefits (−0.16, about a quarter) (log 85–91). That law changed neither benefits nor payroll taxes (log 99).
  • Over the longer run, not a demographic surprise. The Chief Actuary: since 1983 the age mix has "generally followed expectations"; the outlook worsened for two main reasons, neither demographic: the share of earnings under the cap fell (about 90% in 1983 to about 82.5% by 2000) and the economy did worse than expected, especially after 2007–09 (log 101, 205).
  • Workers per beneficiary: about 2.6 in 2025, about 2.3 by 2035 (log 347, 348). The comparator: it held at 3.2–3.4 from 1974 to 2008 (log 347). It falls because of lower birth rates plus longer lives, and it drives cost, but so do benefit levels and how much of pay is taxed; it does not by itself explain this year's worsening (log 349, APPROXIMATE: the mechanics are the Trustees', the reading is ours).

What the trust fund holds (is the money there?)

  • About $2.56 trillion at the end of 2025, about 1.5 years of benefits, down about $160 billion in the year (log 320, 321). Shrinking since 2021, because cost has exceeded all income, interest included (log 330).
  • All of it in special-issue Treasury bonds, sold only to the trust funds, backed by the full faith and credit of the United States (log 323–325, 339). It earned about 2.5–2.6% in 2025 (log 326).
  • It pays benefits by cashing them: $1.75 trillion redeemed in 2025 (log 328). Social Security cannot borrow beyond what it is owed (log 329, 82).
  • Spent, and owed; both true. The cash went into the Treasury's general fund and paid for other spending, by design (log 331–333). The general fund owes it all back with interest (log 337, 338). The critics' strongest point, made by the White House budget office in 1999 and by GAO in 2001, is economic, not legal: to cash the bonds, the government has to tax, cut or borrow (log 334, 335, 340).
  • "It's my money": a moral claim the Supreme Court did not accept as a legal one in 1960: no accrued property right, and Congress can change the rules, though not arbitrarily (log 341–346).
  • About 91% of 2025 income was payroll tax; interest about 5%, tax on benefits about 4% (log 350, APPROXIMATE by rounding).

The levers, priced on one baseline (the Chief Actuary's 2026 table, a 4.42% gap)

  • Payroll rate to 17% from 2027: 101% of the 75-year gap, the only single lever that closes it (log 48). A gradual rise to 13.4% by 2037: 20% (log 49).
  • Eliminate the cap, no benefit credit: 58%; with credit: 45%; a $250,000 donut hole: 57% (55% credited); a $400,000 donut: 51–53%; tax 90% of earnings: 19–24% (log 217, 219, 225).
  • Full retirement age to 69 by 2038, then indexed: 32%; to 68: 13%; to 69 by 2034 with the credit cap at 72: 25%; index to longevity alone: 16% (log 114, 116–118). CBO's own scores: 24% for 67 → 69, about a third for 67 → 70 (log 119, 122).
  • Progressive price indexing (bottom 30% protected): 38%; straight price indexing: 69% (log 246, 247). Chained CPI for the yearly raise: 15%; for retirement benefits only: 13% (log 256, 257).
  • A 12.4% tax on high earners' investment income from 2028: 30% (log 267).
  • No package has been scored on the 2026 basis (log 282). Packages scored on older baselines exist, from revenue-only to mixed, and some close the gap on their own baseline (log 270–276).

The retirement-age lever, as it works

  • It is a benefit cut at every claiming age, about 6–8% of the monthly check per year of age, including at 70 (log 105, our arithmetic on SSA's schedule; log 103). Waiting to the new age keeps the monthly amount but pays it for fewer months (log 106, 107).
  • It does not move the 2032 or 2034 dates and saves little in the first decade (log 120, 121, 123). It is a long-run lever.
  • Claiming moved with past age rises; actual work moved much less (log 128–134). Some people moved onto disability benefits after the 1983 cuts (log 136, 137), though more applications did not always mean more awards (log 140).

The wage cap, as it works

  • About 6 in 100 workers earn above it in any year, but almost 1 in 5 do in at least one year of a career (log 200, 203). The share of all earnings above the cap rose from about 10% in 1983 to about 17% now (log 201), because top pay outran everyone else's (log 206).
  • A worker under the cap pays no more tax when it rises (log 231, 283); the actuaries assume pay shifts that trim everyone's scheduled benefit slightly (log 231).

1983, the last rescue

  • It was signed on 1983-04-20, with almost no time left: OASI had borrowed $17.5 billion to keep paying (log 177, 192). It raised the age from 65 to 67 in steps no one felt for 17 years (log 178, 185), and deepened the cut at 62 from 20% to 30% (log 179).
  • The age rise was not in the Commission's consensus package, but a majority of its members backed a gradual one for the remaining gap; the House passed it 228–202 (log 181, 182, 186).
  • Near-term, the money split about evenly between taxes and benefit trims; over 75 years, more of it came from the cost side (log 195, 196). Taxing benefits counts as either (log 197).

Contested (the real fight)

Sides (Lucas's ruling at the pitch, 2026-10-21): Green, "Close it mostly with money in" (lift the cap AND raise the payroll rate) against Gold, "Close it mostly with less money out" (raise the age, slow the formula). Both teams argue inside the record above; neither may fight the settled facts.

Side A (Green) — "Money in first." The cap has eroded: in 1983 it taxed about 90% of all earnings, now about 83%, because top pay outran everyone else's, and the Chief Actuary names that, not longer lives, as one of the two reasons the 1983 fix ran short (log 201, 205, 206, 101). Removing it closes more than half the gap on this year's numbers, and the worker under the cap pays no more tax (log 217, 231). It works on the near-term gap; an age rise doesn't touch 2032 (log 120). And raising the age is a cut that falls on people whose life expectancy barely moved (log 161, 166), many of whom say their health limits work (log 146), and the 1983 cuts pushed some onto disability (log 136, 137). And the payroll rate is the only single lever on the actuaries' 2026 table that closes the whole 75-year gap: 17% from 2027 closes 101% (log 48). Revenue-only packages have closed the gap on their own baseline (log 273). The 1983 Commission's own principle was balance (log 194), and the last rescue leaned on the benefit side over the long run (log 196).

  • Where it is weakest: alone it closes 58% at most, and less with a benefit credit (log 217); the "zero behaviour" revenue estimate is a 2006 simulation of total government revenue, and how much a higher cap really raises depends on assumptions (log 234–242); about 1 in 5 workers would pay it at some point, not just "the rich" (log 203); and removing the credit breaks the earned-benefit link (log 218). The rate's weak point airs with it: about a third more tax on every covered wage if it starts now (12.4% to 16.65%, log 29, 30), about 37% more for the 17%-from-2027 version (our arithmetic: 17 ÷ 12.4), for every worker and employer, and even 17% from 2027 closes only 71% of the gap in the 75th year (log 48).

Side B (Gold) — "Less money out." Americans live longer than in 1983: life expectancy at 65 is up about 3 years against a 2-year rise in the full retirement age (log 157, 158). Raising it to 69 and then tying it to longevity closes about a third of the gap without raising anyone's tax (log 114). Claiming at 62 is already much rarer, about 1 in 5 new awards against nearly half in 2009 (log 144). By CBO's lifetime measure, a higher age takes a smaller share from the lowest earners than from the top, because they rely more on disability benefits (log 125, 126). Progressive price indexing protects the bottom 30% entirely and closes 38% (log 246, 249). Other countries tie the age to longevity (log 289, 292, 295), and the 1983 age rise passed because a majority of the Commission backed it (log 181).

  • Where it is weakest: the gain in years went mostly to the top (log 159–166), and for the full retirement age NAS finds the largest proportional cut at the bottom within Social Security (log 175); people mostly claimed later rather than worked longer (log 128–134); it does nothing for 2032 (log 120); abroad, automatic links were softened, delayed or need a fresh vote (log 290, 293, 298, 299, 311, 316); and price indexing cuts the average earner too (log 250).

The reframe

The shouting is "people live too long" against "the rich don't pay their share". The record backs neither story cleanly.

  1. The long-run gap is not mainly about longer lives. The Chief Actuary's two reasons since 1983 are the eroded cap and a weaker economy (log 101, 205). This year's jump is mostly about fewer future births, lower immigration and the 2025 tax law (log 85–91). Life expectancy did rise about 3 years at 65, but mostly at the top (log 157–166).
  2. The cap did erode, but taxing every wage closes only about half to three-fifths (log 201, 217).
  3. So the question is not who to blame. It is who carries a gap the size of about a third more payroll tax: today's workers (the rate), the top earners of any year (the cap), future retirees (the age, the formula), the oldest (the yearly raise), or, by default, every check from late 2032, today's retirees' included (log 30, 51, 80).

Both sides quietly agree

Every serious source in the record agrees that doing nothing is the harshest option, and that the answer is a mix or one very large single move.

  • The Chief Actuary: "raise program income by about one-third, lower scheduled benefits by about one-fourth, or adopt a combination", and the cut is "the default" if no one acts (log 36, 80).
  • The Trustees: every year of delay makes each lever bigger (log 33, 34).
  • Each side's own lever closes only part: the age to 69 and indexed, 32%; the cap removed, 58% (log 114, 217).
  • 1983 did both: a Commission package of taxes and trims, and a Congress that added the age rise a majority of commissioners had backed (log 181, 186, 194).

The fight is narrower than it sounds (our reading of the record, not a finding): not whether to act or which lever is "right", but the split between money in and money out, and how much lead time people get.

Verdict framing

"What survives both teams, and the dial you still have to set." Each candidate is earned in the debate and dropped if it is not.

  • Survives: the gap is real, dated and priced (log 27, 50, 51). Doing nothing is a choice, and the default lands on today's retirees too (log 51, 80). A higher age is a benefit cut and a long-run lever (log 105, 120). The cap has eroded (log 201, 205). Delay makes every lever bigger (log 33, 34). The money was spent, and it is owed (log 331, 337, 340).
  • Fell: "Social Security is going broke" (log 51, 81). "Congress stole the trust fund", as theft without a debt (log 337). "The law automatically cuts checks to 78%" (log 74).   • The one dial the viewer sets, named and never set: how much of the gap comes from more money in, and how much from less money out; and on whom. The settings the record prices, each with something real behind it and its documented weak point: 1. Weak point: the rate is about a third more tax on every covered wage (more if it starts later: 17% is about 37% more, our arithmetic) and closes only 71% of the gap in the 75th year (log 30, 48); the package is on an older baseline; the cap alone closes 58% at most; revenue depends on behaviour (log 217, 234–242). 2. Mostly money in, with some benefits raised — a 2023 House bill moved the combined date from 2034 to 2066 but left a deficit (3.61 → 0.47) (log 274, 275). Weak point: it does not fully close. 3. Weak point: nothing is scored on the 2026 basis (log 282). 4. Mostly less money out (Gold's case) — the age to 69 and indexed (32%) and progressive price indexing (38%) are the biggest single benefit levers (log 114, 246); in one 2026 model, the formula-and-age mixes cut benefits 20–40% by 2100 and no mix fully restores balance (log 279, 281). Weak point: the longevity gap and the average earner's loss (log 159–166, 250). 5. Nobody turns it — about 22% short on all scheduled retirement benefits from late 2032, today's retirees' included, by a method the law doesn't specify (log 51, 75, 80). The research earned the price tag on each setting. It did not earn a right split.

Make it Matter — one worker, followed

The order (Lucas's ruling, 2026-10-21): open on "is the money there?", then the levers, then the dial.

  • The cold open (≤ ~100 words, premise before the object): the question first: "Is the money for your Social Security check actually there?" Then the turn, feeling over figures: "The trust fund holds about two and a half trillion dollars, all of it in Treasury bonds. The cash was spent, and it is owed. Both are true. But in about six years, the bonds for retirement run out, and taxes coming in cover about 78 cents on the dollar. The law doesn't even say whether checks would shrink or come late." Then: "So: raise the retirement age, lift the tax cap, or trim benefits? Two teams argue it, the desk checks both, and you set the dial." (log 320, 324, 331, 337, 50, 51, 75; "about six years" is our arithmetic from today to late 2032.)
  • The desk tells one story, one worker (SSA's medium earner: average pay, turns 65 in 2035, $34,975 a year scheduled at 67; log 283, 284): 0. Is the money there? (about 60 seconds, first). What the fund holds: about $2.56 trillion, all in special-issue Treasury bonds backed by the full faith and credit of the United States (log 320, 323, 324). "Spent": the cash went into the Treasury's general fund and paid for other things, as the law designs it (log 331, 332). "Owed": every dollar back, with interest (log 337, 338). "The money was spent, and it is owed. Both are true." The catch: to cash the bonds, the rest of the government has to tax, cut or borrow (log 334, 340), and once they run out, the law doesn't say how a shortfall would be applied, smaller checks or late ones (log 75, 78). Three short, honest beats on the comments' biggest asks (Lucas's ruling, 2026-10-21)

Two to four sentences each, cited, information never advice. Where they sit is the script's call; suggested: "Congress first" beside the price list, the child accounts beside the scope line, claiming at 62 beside "what the viewer can do".

  • "Cut Congress's perks first." Since 1984, every member of Congress has paid the same 6.2% Social Security tax you do, sitting members included, not only new ones (log 352, 355, 365). Their pensions come from a separate federal retirement fund, not from Social Security (log 357). And the size: all of Congress's salaries and pensions come to roughly $131 million a year, against a gap of roughly $467 billion a year in today's terms; cancel every bit of it and you close about three-hundredths of one percent, roughly, by our own arithmetic (log 358–363; on screen, "our estimate").
  • "The child accounts are a backdoor." The 2025 law created children's accounts: a traditional IRA with a one-time $1,000 Treasury deposit for US-citizen children born 2025 through 2028, if someone signs them up, and nothing goes in before July 4, 2026 (log 367–371). It redirects no payroll tax and changes no Social Security benefit (log 374). The "back door to privatizing" line came from the Treasury Secretary, and Treasury called the accounts "additive" the same day (log 376); the last real attempt at personal accounts, in 2005, never got a Senate floor vote (log 377, 380).
  • "Should I claim at 62 before the cut?" Claiming at 62 cuts the monthly check by 30% for anyone born in 1960 or later, and SSA says the cut is usually permanent (log 25, 382). The law doesn't say how a shortfall would be applied, and no official source says people who claimed early would be protected; SSA's own 2004 study of a no-action shortfall assumed the same cut on every check (log 75, 78, 390–392). SSA says there is no best age for everyone, names cash needs, health and family longevity as the factors, and dropped the break-even age in 2008 (log 384, 385, 387). For a married higher earner, an early claim can also lower the surviving spouse's check for life (log 388, 389).

Script traps

The clock and whose numbers (hand pass A)

  • Say whose projection. Default to the 2026 Trustees Report. If CBO appears, name it and use its own numbers; never one Trustees number and one CBO number in a sentence without naming both; CBO's are its February 2026 baseline (log 65–70).
  • Say which fund. "The retirement fund runs short in late 2032; after that, about 78%." "Counting the disability fund too, late 2034 and 83%", and that combined figure is hypothetical (log 50, 51, 54, 55, 57). ⛔ Never pair 2032 with 83% or 2034 with 78%.
  • "Runs short", not "runs out" or "goes broke" (log 51, 81).
  • ⛔ Never "the law automatically cuts benefits to 78%". Say "incoming taxes would cover about 78%; the law doesn't say whether checks would be smaller or late". "If Congress does nothing, checks would effectively be cut about a fifth" is fair; "automatic" is not (log 74–80). The legal source is a 2022 CRS report: cite it for the law, never for its 2022 percentages (log 79).
  • 4.42% is a share of taxable payroll over 75 years: not of GDP (about 1.5%), not of benefits, not the tax rise needed (4.25 points) (log 27, 29, 38).
  • The fix sizes are dated January 2026, already past: "about", or "if Congress had acted this year"; 4.25 points is the worker-plus-employer rate (log 29, 33, 34).
  • 22%, 25.2%, 17% are different numbers: the OASI shortfall at 2032; the immediate 75-year fix; the combined shortfall in 2034 (log 31, 39, 51, 55).
  • Dollars: if at all, the Trustees' $29.3 trillion, never CRFB's $31 trillion or CRR's $30.5 trillion (log 41–43).
  • "Moved up a quarter, into late 2032" (log 50, 53).
  • What moved it: fewer births, lower immigration, the 2025 tax law (about a quarter). ⛔ Never say that law cut benefits or raised payroll tax, or moved the combined date this year (log 85–91, 97, 99).
  • "The largest since at least 1982", not "since 1977" (log 46).
  • The peg: "nearly 72 million Social Security beneficiaries" (not 76 million); "about $75 a month, before any change in the Medicare premium, which isn't announced yet"; "$184,500 this year, $192,300 next, rising with average wages; the rate still 6.2% each" (log 2, 3, 5, 8–12).
  • ⛔ H.R. 5284: "Congress has passed a bill that renames the terms SSA uses: full retirement age becomes the 'standard monthly benefit age'. It changes no age and no benefit." Never "Congress renamed age 67", never "law" until it is signed (log 16, 23).

The retirement-age lever (hand pass B)

  • A benefit cut, not a work rule. "Each year of full retirement age cuts the monthly check by roughly 6 to 8 percent, at whatever age you claim": our arithmetic, never CRS's, SSA's or CBO's (log 105). Never "a 5% cut".
  • Waiting to 70 escapes nothing under today's credits (the credit stops at 70); some designs move the cap to 72: say which (log 103, 109, 117).
  • Shares on the 2026 basis only: 13%, 32%, 25%, 16%. CBO's 24% and about a third are on CBO's own baseline. ⛔ Never a 2025-basis 36% or 14% on screen (log 114–122).
  • It does not move 2032 or 2034 (log 120, 123).
  • Dollars: about $129 a month per year of age for CRS's medium earner at 67 in 2027, our arithmetic; never $167 (log 110).
  • Who loses most: name the measure (log 124–126, 175). ⛔ Never NAS's "only modest effects" about the full retirement age: it's about 62 and Medicare's 65 (log 174).
  • Response: "claiming moved with the age; leaving work moved much less". "About half" only as "one early study of people born 1938–40" (log 127–134). "Many took the cut" is an inference (log 134).
  • Disability: the 1983 benefit cuts, not the age alone; "nearly double in the long run" at most; 8.7% is GAO's; applications are not awards; nothing yet on the cohorts reaching 67 (log 136–140, 143).
  • Health and hard jobs: GAO's 27.7% is of everyone at 60–61, 1998–2008; 63.6% is of those working, "at least some of the time". Don't add them. CEPR and SCEPA are advocacy-leaning, different definitions (log 146–152).
  • Claiming at 62: about 22–24% of 2024 awards. ⛔ Never "most people claim at 62" (log 144).
  • Longevity: one source per sentence (NCHS or SSA; period or cohort) (log 154–158). Chetty's 14.6 and 10.1 years are the extreme 1%s, at 40, an association (log 159–162). "The bottom barely moved", not "gained nothing" (log 161). NAS's 12.7 is a projection at 50 (log 164). Post-pandemic: label education (Case-Deaton) and California-only (log 172, 173).
  • 1983: "Congress added the age rise, which a majority of commissioners had backed for the remaining gap; the House passed it 228–202." Never "the commission rejected it" (log 181, 182).
  • Other countries: a statute shows what was adopted, not that it stuck: "softened" (Netherlands, Sweden), "floored or partly rolled back" (Germany), "delayed" (France to 2028), "repealed" (Spain's factor), "used late" (Japan, first in 2015). Denmark's link needs a vote each time (log 290–316). Most foreign pension ages are eligibility ages; the US full retirement age is a formula age: don't compare one-for-one. OECD's "70 or more" are projections (log 317).

The revenue and formula levers (hand pass C)

  • One baseline on screen: the actuaries' 2026 table (58%, 45%, 57%, 38%, 15%, 101%). Never a 2025 letter's 65% or CRFB's 70% beside them. "The Social Security actuaries' own scoring, on this year's numbers" (log 217, 226, 246, 256, 48).
  • "No single lever closes it" needs its one exception said out loud: a payroll-tax rise alone does (log 48).
  • Cap shares: "about 6 in 100 workers earn above the cap in a given year", never "only 6% would ever pay"; "about 10% of all wages sat above the cap in 1983, about 17% now", never "17% of workers" (log 200, 201, 203).
  • "Pays no more tax", not "is untouched" (log 231).
  • Behaviour numbers are model numbers: the "20–25% below the static estimate" is total government revenue in a 2006 simulation; Powell's 0.08 is the only measured response at the cap, from a temporary cut. Name the scorer (log 234–240). Penn Wharton's GDP −1.7% is one model, a saving effect, not people working less (log 241, 242).
  • With or without benefit credit are different policies: say which (log 217, 218).
  • Progressive price indexing protects the bottom 30%, not the middle (log 249, 250).
  • Chained CPI: "a smaller raise each year that adds up". "More accurate" is its advocates' claim (log 259, 260, 265).
  • 1983: "the deal split the near-term money about evenly"; never "half and half" for the long run (log 195, 196).
  • Bills: no bill has an actuary's score on this year's numbers. ⛔ Never say a bill "fixes Social Security" without its baseline year (log 270–282). Hirono-Tokuda's "11 more years" is the actuaries' figure, on the 2025 basis (log 227).
  • Dollars for a typical worker are ours: label "our arithmetic on the actuaries' numbers"; SSA's $34,975 (2026 dollars) is the base to quote (log 284–286). ⚠ The $1,600-a-year and $86-a-month rows are APPROXIMATE (log 285, 286): rough sizes, never precise figures. Lucas ruled 2026-10-21: they air, with a spoken "roughly, by our own arithmetic" and an on-screen "our estimate" label. Say the $1,600's basis: an average earner's own half of the 4.25-point fix that starts January 2026 (log 29, 285). ⛔ Never put the $1,600 beside the 17%-from-2027 row (log 48): a different start year and a different size.
  • The rate is Green's (his ruling, 2026-10-21): its case (the only single lever that closes the 75-year gap, log 48) and its weak point (about a third more tax on every covered wage if it starts now, about 37% more at 17% from 2027 by our arithmetic; 71% of the gap in the 75th year, log 30, 48) air together.

The money, and the three short beats (top-ups D, G1, G2, G3)

  • "Spent, and owed. Both are true." ⛔ Never "stolen" or "raided" without the debt in the same breath, and never "the money is sitting in a vault" (log 331, 337, 340). The critics' best version is the White House budget office's and GAO's: say whose (log 334, 335).
  • ⛔ Never "the fund earns 4.5%": that is only the newest bonds; the fund earned about 2.5–2.6% in 2025 (log 326). Don't air the numbers on SSA's Trust Fund FAQ page: they are 2024's (log 327).
  • Tax on benefits: only the tax on up to half of benefits goes to Social Security; the rest goes to Medicare (log 350).
  • "My money": the Court found no accrued property right, but left a limit for arbitrary cuts. Don't overstate either way (log 341–346).
  • Workers per beneficiary: 2.6 now, never 2.7 (log 347). ⛔ Never "16 workers in 1950" as a decline story without saying the program was young (log 347).
  • Congress: ⛔ never "Congress is exempt", even as a set-up line, without the answer in the same breath (log 352, 365). Never imply cutting their pensions would refill Social Security: different fund (log 357). "Full pay for life" is false (log 356). Pension counts are "the latest CRS count", 2022–23 (log 360, 361). The $131 million, $467 billion and 0.03% are always "roughly, by our own arithmetic" (log 362, 363). Name no member.
  • Child accounts: ⛔ never "every baby gets $1,000" (log 370); never "the actuaries cleared the accounts": they never scored them (log 375). "The Treasury Secretary" by role (a remark, not an official act). 2005: air the shape (it made the shortfall worse at first, and never got a vote), not the $541 billion in 2004 dollars (log 379, 380). The fear (a future replacement) is a fear, not a fact about this law (log 374, 376).
  • Claiming at 62: ⛔ never "claim early to lock in your benefit" (log 78, 390–392). ⛔ Never advice: no "you should wait", no "it pays to file early"; air SSA's own line that there is no best age (log 384, 387). ⛔ No break-even age on screen: SSA dropped it in 2008 and GAO found it nudges people to claim earlier (log 385, 386); if an example is shown, it is SSA's $1,400 / $2,000 / $2,480, labelled "SSA's example" (log 383). The spouse is a fact of the rules, not a reason to wait (log 388, 389). Say "the bump for waiting past full retirement age, up to 70", which survives H.R. 5284 either way (log 20, 103).
