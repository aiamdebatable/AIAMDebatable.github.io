Social Security Changes: Raise the Age, Lift the Cap, or Trim Benefits?

Social Security's trust fund runs short: raise the retirement age, lift the tax cap, or trim benefits?

Is the money for your Social Security check actually there? It was spent, and it is owed. Then one worker's price list for every lever: two AI teams argue how much of the gap comes from money in and how much from less money out; you set the dial.

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

On October 14 Social Security announced that checks rise 3.6% in January 2027 and the cap on taxed pay rises to $192,300. Both changes are automatic, and neither touches the shortfall: from late 2032 the taxes coming in cover about 78 cents of every dollar of scheduled retirement benefits.

Settled

What both sides accept

Both teams accepted the same record. The money was spent, and it is owed. From late 2032 the taxes coming in cover about 78 cents on the dollar on every retirement check, and the law doesn't say how. Fixing it takes about a third more payroll tax or about a quarter off every check, and waiting raises both.

What the shouting gets wrong

It isn't "we live too long" against "the rich don't pay their share". The record backs neither story cleanly: the long-run gap is mostly an eroded cap and a weaker economy, and this year's jump is mostly fewer births. So the question isn't who to blame. It's who carries a gap the size of a third more payroll tax: today's workers, top earners, future retirees, the oldest, or, if nobody chooses, every check from late 2032, today's retirees' included.

The verdict

What the record supports
Doing nothing is the harshest option, because the default reaches today's retirees too. Every serious source points to a mix, or one very large single move. And no package has been scored on this year's numbers.
What didn't hold up
Social Security isn't going broke; it will run short, and it wasn't stolen without being owed. The law doesn't cut checks automatically. People don't just work longer, a later age doesn't fix twenty thirty-two, the cap alone doesn't fix it, neither lever touches only the rich, and Congress hasn't moved the retirement age.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: How much in, how much out, and on whom?

The judge left one dial: how much of the gap comes from money in, how much from money out, and on whom. These are the settings the judge named along it, from all in to mostly out, and nobody was declared the winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1All money in
    What it buys
    The payroll rate is the only single lever that closes the whole gap: a 17% rate from 2027 closes all of it, on the actuaries' scoring. It starts with the part that eroded: of every 100 dollars of covered pay, about 10 sat above the cap in 1983 and about 17 do now, and the Chief Actuary names that, not longer lives, as a main reason.
    What it costs
    A third more tax or more, and it lands on every worker's pay. Taxing every dollar of pay closes 58% at most, 45% if those earnings count toward bigger benefits, so money in has to mean the rate as well. A 17% rate is about 37% more tax on every covered wage, by our arithmetic, for every worker and every employer, and even then it covers only 71% of the gap in the 75th year. Almost one in five workers earn above the cap at some point in a career, not just the rich.
    Not measured
    How much a higher cap really raises: it depends on how pay shifts, and part of this case rests on models of that.

    On the record: row 48 of the fact-check log, row 394 of the fact-check log, row 201 of the fact-check log, row 217 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 203 of the fact-check log

  2. 2Both is what nineteen eighty-three did
    What it buys
    In 1983 Congress raised taxes and trimmed benefits, with almost no time left, and raised the full retirement age from 65 to 67 in steps nobody felt for seventeen years; a majority of the commission had backed a gradual rise. Every serious source points to a mix, or one very large single move: neither side's lever closes the gap alone.
    What it costs
    Some of each side's costs: more tax on pay, and smaller future checks, in whatever split is chosen.
    Not measured
    What any given mix would close, or cost you: no package has been scored on this year's numbers.

    On the record: row 186 of the fact-check log, row 181 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 194 of the fact-check log, row 36 of the fact-check log, row 282 of the fact-check log

  3. 3Mostly money out
    What it buys
    No new tax. People live about three years longer at 65 than in 1983, against a two-year rise in the full retirement age. A later age, 69 and then tied to longevity, closes about a third of the gap. Protecting the bottom 30% of earners completely and slowing the formula above them closes about 38%. Claiming at 62 is already much rarer: about one in five new retirement awards, against nearly half in 2009.
    What it costs
    A later age and a slower formula, cutting future checks. Each year of later age takes roughly 6 to 8% off the monthly check at every claiming age, even 70, by our arithmetic; claiming still opens at 62. A higher age does nothing for 2032, and the longer lives it counts on went mostly to the top. People mostly claimed later instead of working longer, and some moved onto disability. The slower formula cuts the average earner too: about 11% less by 2035 in Social Security's model of an earlier design. Neither lever closes the gap alone.
    Not measured
    What today's slower formula would cost an average earner in dollars: nobody has put a figure on it, and no package of these levers has been scored on this year's numbers.

    On the record: row 158 of the fact-check log, row 114 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 246 of the fact-check log, row 120 of the fact-check log, row 250 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pick: Waiting raises the price of every lever: start in 2034 and it's nearly 5 points on the payroll rate, or nearly 29% off every check. And if nobody turns the dial, every retirement check, today's retirees' included, comes up about a fifth short from late 2032, and the law doesn't say how.

What our fact-check caught

33 of the 394 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidWhen the retirement fund runs short in 2032, the law cuts benefits automatically to 78%.

    The record saysThe law doesn't say what happens. 78% is what the taxes still coming in could pay. Checks could come smaller on time, or in full but late, and people would still be owed the full amount.

  2. Draft saidCongress stole the Social Security trust fund.

    The record saysThe cash was spent, the way the law sets it up, but every dollar is owed back with interest, and the fund cashes those bonds every day to pay benefits. Both are true.

  3. Draft saidRaising the full retirement age to 69, then tying it to longevity, closes about 36% of the gap.

    The record saysThat was last year's score. On this year's numbers it closes 32%, about a third.

  4. Draft saidLifting the cap costs a typical worker nothing.

    The record saysA typical worker pays no more tax. But the actuaries expect some pay to shift out of wages, which trims everyone's scheduled benefit slightly.

  5. Draft saidLast year's tax law started privatizing Social Security.

    The record saysIts new children's accounts are a kind of IRA, with a one-time $1,000 deposit for citizen children born 2025 through 2028 if someone signs them up. No payroll tax goes to them and no Social Security benefit changes.
