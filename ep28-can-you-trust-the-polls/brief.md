# Research brief — Can You Trust the Polls This November?

_The full research behind the episode. Design type: the verdict is what survives both teams plus the one
dial the viewer sets. Researched 2026-10-03: peg hunt, then a deep-research fan-out in design mode, then
two hand passes that opened every relied-on primary (certified canvasses, the AAPOR task-force reports,
the papers themselves). Every number below is in the fact-check log with its source._

⛔ **Frame (ruled 2026-09-30):** the process and why the viewer should care, never who is ahead. No live
2026 horse-race number appears anywhere in this episode. Misses are shown in both directions. Candidates
are "the polled leader" and "the winner", never the subject.

## The question
**What should a newsroom, and you, do with an election poll?** Four designs are in use right now:
1. **A single poll is news** — the headline habit; debate stages that use a poll test.
2. **Only averages of transparent pollsters** — the aggregators' gates (NYT, FiftyPlusOne, RealClearPolling).
3. **A poll is a forecast with a real error band** — wider than the printed margin of error.
4. **Play polls down** — cover what candidates would do, not who is winning.

## Why now
In August 2026 a firm called **Median Strategies** posted at least three "polls" — a big-city mayoral
race and two governor's races — and on Monday 17 August admitted to the Los Angeles Times that they were
made up, "a short-term social experiment" on how unverified numbers spread. A campaign, one news outlet
and some poll-aggregator accounts on social media had passed them on. The New York Times, FiftyPlusOne
and RealClearPolling never put them in their averages ([AP via PBS, 08-19](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/what-to-know-about-the-fake-election-poll-numbers-shared-and-retracted-by-a-mysterious-company)).
AAPOR, the pollsters' professional body, said on 19 August: "In this case, the responses were simply made
up. No individuals responded to survey questions" ([AAPOR](https://aapor.org/announcements/aapor-responds-to-fabricated-polling-data/)).

The same month, real polls missed three primaries — two one way, one the other:
- **Michigan (4 Aug):** FiftyPlusOne's final average had the polled leader up 12.9 points; the certified
  margin was 0.90 points, 13,792 votes ([MI SOS, official](https://mvic.sos.state.mi.us/votehistory/Index?type=C&electionDate=8-4-2026)). The margin was off by about 12.
- **Wisconsin (11 Aug):** the last big public poll, fielded 3–6 Aug, had the polled leader up 20–22; the
  other candidate won by 3,673 votes, under half a point ([WEC canvass](https://elections.wi.gov/sites/default/files/documents/August%202026%20Partisan%20Primary_Summary%20Results.pdf)).
- **Minnesota (11 Aug), the other direction:** the final public poll had the eventual winner two points
  behind; she won by 19.6 ([MN SOS](https://www.sos.mn.gov/elections-voting/election-results/2026/2026-primary-election-results/); [SurveyUSA](https://kstp.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/ElectionAugust.pdf)).

And on 30 September Pew reported that AI **"synthetic respondents"** standing in for real panelists
differed from the real answers by 12 points on average across nearly 300 questions ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2026/09/30/can-ai-stand-in-for-human-survey-takers-not-really/)).
With the vote on 3 November, every viewer is about to meet dozens of polls. Michigan's Nexstar debates
(governor 6 Oct, Senate 8 Oct) use a poll test to decide who is on the stage.

## Settled (not the debate)
- **The real error is about twice what the printed margin of error implies.** 4,221 state polls, 608 races,
  1998–2014: real error ≈ 3.5 points RMSE, "about twice" what most margins of error imply; nominal 95%
  intervals covered the result only 73% (Senate), 74% (governor) and 88% (presidential) of the time. Polls
  of the same race tend to be wrong together, so averaging cannot remove that shared part. (Shirani-Mehr,
  Rothschild, Goel & Gelman, *JASA* 2018.)
- **Misses run both ways over time.** AAPOR: "Democrats and Republicans have each been underestimated
  about equally often since the dawn of modern polling in the 1930s." 2000 and 2012 overstated the
  Republican; 2016, 2020 and 2024 the Democrat; the 2018 midterm overstated Democrats by 0.1 and 2022
  overstated Republicans by 0.6. AAPOR also says plainly that the last three presidential years all
  missed the same way, and that the cause is unclear.
- **Polls got much better in 2024 and still leaned.** State presidential polls missed by 3.0 on the
  margin, the most accurate since 1944; across all offices the lean was 2.7 points; House polls still
  missed by about 7. 2020 was the largest national miss since 1980 (1980 was larger).
- **Errors that cancel are not accuracy.** Midterm signed errors were near zero, but individual races
  still missed by 4.6 (2018 Senate and governor) to 4.8 (2022 Senate) on average.
- **Primary polls are a different, noisier instrument.** AAPOR: state presidential primary polls missed
  the margin by 7.0–9.3 points per cycle, 2000–2016. For Senate and governor primaries the best estimate is
  one analyst database's ~13 points (FiftyPlusOne; APPROXIMATE, named on screen).
- **Fewer people answer.** Pew's own phone surveys: 36% responded in 1997, 6% in 2018; NYT/Siena
  reported 0.4% in 2022. Pew: "No one approach [to weighting] is clearly superior in all contexts."
- **The gates held in August.** The fakes spread through the single-poll channel, not the averages.
- **AI stand-ins are not polls.** AAPOR's AI task force (May 2026): "'Poll,' 'polling,' 'survey' … should not
  be used to describe data created through artificial intelligence."

## Contested (the real fight)
**Team Green — "Read the average, not the headline":** the August scandal is the proof. The averages'
disclosure gates kept every fake out; the fakes reached voters only as single numbers shared by a
campaign, an outlet and social accounts. Transparency correlates with accuracy (538: AAPOR Transparency
Initiative and Roper members about 2 points more accurate on its scale). A newsroom should publish
averages of pollsters who show their methods, and say the real band is about twice the printed one.

**Team Gold — "Stop treating any poll number as news":** the average missed Michigan by 12 points too,
because the error is shared. Showing a margin of error doesn't help readers: in a 4,151-person experiment
adding "±2%" "had no effect" on what people took away, and an 87% win probability was read as about 70%.
Coverage already crowds out substance — in 2016 policy was 10% of general-election coverage, "less than a
fourth the space given to the horserace" (Shorenstein). A poll decides things before anyone votes: who is
on the debate stage (Nexstar's 10% test), what gets covered (the poll leader's coverage ran positive, the
trailer's negative, in 2016).

## The reframe
The fight is argued as "how loudly should we report polls." The record says the problem is shared,
correlated error that no presentation choice removes. Averaging solved the fake-poll problem in August —
but not Michigan. A wider band is statistically right but, in the one direct test, changed nothing in
readers' heads. Playing polls down lowers exposure, with no study showing voters end up better informed.
And the primary misses are mostly a different instrument (late deciders, dropouts, a late endorsement in
Wisconsin) — judging November's polls by August's primaries is a category error.

## Both sides quietly agree
A single poll whose method is hidden, or whose true uncertainty is unstated, should not be treated as
news. Even AAPOR, defending pollsters in court, calls a poll a snapshot measurement, not a warranty — and
an Iowa federal judge agreed, dismissing a suit that called a pre-election poll "fake news" (Donnelly v.
Des Moines Register; appeal argued 22 Sept, undecided).

## Verdict framing (design: what survives both, plus your dial)
**The design that survives both teams:** (1) never a single undisclosed poll as a headline — who paid,
who answered, how many, when; (2) averages from pollsters who disclose, with the band stated as roughly
double the printed margin; (3) primaries labelled as the noisier instrument they are.

**The dial you set:** *how much weight does a poll get in your head before 3 November?*
① **Ignore single polls** (France bans publishing them from the Saturday before a vote; 2017 shows
foreign outlets fill the gap). ② **Averages only** (the aggregators' gates — they held in August, and
still missed Michigan). ③ **A forecast with a band about twice the printed margin** (JASA — right on the numbers, but readers
didn't change in the one test). ④ **News** (the habit that carried Median Strategies to voters).
Each setting has a real institution behind it and a documented failure. No winner declared.
