How Accurate Are Election Polls? A 13-Point Lead That Vanished

What should a newsroom, and you, do with an election poll?

Last changed 2026-11-07 · See every change

A 13-point lead vanished on primary night, and a firm's polls turned out to be made up. What should a poll be to you?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in October 2026, the November election was weeks away. That August, the final polling average in Michigan's Senate primary had the leader up almost 13 points and the count came in under one, and a polling firm admitted that polls it had posted were made up.

Settled

What both sides accept

The real error in polls is about twice the printed margin. Misses have run both ways over time, though the last three presidential elections leaned the same way, and primary polls are rougher readings. August's fake polls reached a campaign and a news outlet, but not the big averages.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesRead the average

The big averages asked who did each poll and how, and kept August's fake polls out, while single polls passed around as news spread them. In 2024, state presidential polls were the most accurate in 80 years. Never headline a single undisclosed poll, publish averages from pollsters who disclose, and say the real error is about twice the printed margin.

▶ Hear Green at 2:31 on YouTube
Gold arguesStop calling it news

In Michigan the real polls fooled the average, because when polls share a mistake the average shares it too. Printing the margin of error changed nothing about what readers took away, and a poll can help decide who gets on a debate stage. Report a poll as one reading of a race, inside the story, never as the headline.

▶ Hear Gold at 6:13 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

Nobody defends a made-up poll, and nobody defends treating one number as a result. The design that survives both teams: no single undisclosed poll as a headline, averages from pollsters who disclose with the real error said out loud, and primaries labelled as rougher readings. What's left is how much weight a poll gets in your head before you vote.

The verdict

What survives both teams
Never a single undisclosed poll as a headline: say who paid, who answered, how many and when. Averages from pollsters who disclose, with the real error said out loud. And primaries labelled as the rougher readings they are.
What didn't hold up
Green's comfort: an average of honest polls can still be wrong together, and it was. Gold's fear: nobody has shown that playing polls down leaves anyone better informed.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: How much weight does a poll get in your head before you vote?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Ignore single polls
    What it buys
    No single number gets read as a result. A printed margin didn't change what readers took away: in the one test, adding a plus-or-minus 2% changed nothing.
    What it costs
    Hiding polls doesn't hide them. France bans publishing polls from the Saturday before a vote until the polls close, and in 2017 outlets in Belgium and Switzerland published election-day exit polls anyway. And Gold's fear fell: nobody has shown that playing polls down leaves anyone better informed.
    Not measured
    Which misleads more, an average that's wrong together or a vacuum filled by spin: nobody has measured it.

    On the record: row 28 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 23 of the fact-check log

  2. 2Averages only
    What it buys
    The averages' disclosure gates caught every fake: The New York Times, FiftyPlusOne and RealClearPolling kept August's made-up polls out, and the Times said they lacked basic information about how they were done and who was behind them. Averaging cancels the noise in any one poll.
    What it costs
    Green's comfort fell: an average of honest polls can still be wrong together, and it was. The average missed Michigan by about 12, because averaging cancels noise, not a mistake the polls share: the final average had the leader up 12.9 points, and the certified margin was 0.90.

    On the record: row 4 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 1 of the fact-check log, row 3 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 5 of the fact-check log, row 13 of the fact-check log

  3. 3A forecast with a band about twice the printed margin
    What it buys
    It says the real error out loud. One study of 4,221 state polls from 1998 to 2014 found a poll's stated 95% range caught the actual result only 73% of the time for Senate polls. And a November reading has a record: in 2024, state presidential polls were the most accurate since 1944, about 3 points off, though House polls still missed by about 7.
    What it costs
    A printed margin didn't change what readers took away: in one test of 4,151 people, adding a plus-or-minus 2% changed nothing, and an 87% chance of winning was read as about 70.

    On the record: row 13 of the fact-check log, row 15 of the fact-check log, row 23 of the fact-check log

  4. 4News, the habit that carried August's fakes
    What it buys
    Every poll reaches you as it comes out, on TV, in your feed, in a fundraising text. The judge credits this setting with nothing. On whether that moves votes, a Dutch experiment with 23,421 people found plain poll numbers didn't move vote intentions, though stressing that a party was rising gave it a small boost; we found no comparable test in a real American election.
    What it costs
    It's how August's fakes travelled: a campaign shared one, a news outlet covered it, and some poll-aggregator accounts on social media passed them around. The design that survived both teams says never a single undisclosed poll as a headline. And single primary polls are rough: in Wisconsin, the last big public poll had the leader up about 20, and the other candidate won by under half a point. In 2016, policy got about a tenth of general-election coverage, less than a quarter of the space given to who was winning.

    On the record: row 1 of the fact-check log, row 6 of the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), row 24 of the fact-check log, row 25 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pick: The real error is about twice the printed margin. Misses have run both ways over time, though the last three presidential elections leaned the same way, and primaries are rougher readings. And nobody has found a study showing that any of these leaves voters better informed: not averages only, not wider bands, not less coverage.

What our fact-check caught

7 of the 30 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidWisconsin's last big poll had the leader ahead 48 to 14.

    The record saysThose were only the early voters. Across everyone polled, the leader was up about 20.

  2. Draft saidA poll result was one alternative route onto a TV debate stage.

    The record saysThat came from an old version of the rules. Under the current rules, the poll test is one of five a candidate must meet.

  3. Draft saidA judge threw out the Iowa poll lawsuit by granting the pollster immunity.

    The record saysThe suit was dismissed because it didn't state a claim: a poll of a future event isn't a false statement of fact. The court did not grant immunity.

  4. Draft saidThe pollsters' professional body spoke out about the fake polls on August 25.

    The record saysIt first published its statement on August 19. The 25th was a re-post.
