How Accurate Are Election Polls? A 13-Point Lead That Vanished

The question: What should a newsroom, and you, do with an election poll?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

A 13-point lead vanished on primary night, and a firm's polls turned out to be made up. What should a poll be to you?

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in October 2026, the November election was weeks away. That August, the final polling average in Michigan's Senate primary had the leader up almost 13 points and the count came in under one, and a polling firm admitted that polls it had posted were made up.

The fight

Green arguesRead the average

The big averages asked who did each poll and how, and kept August's fake polls out, while single polls passed around as news spread them. In 2024, state presidential polls were the most accurate in 80 years. Never headline a single undisclosed poll, publish averages from pollsters who disclose, and say the real error is about twice the printed margin.

Gold arguesStop calling it news

In Michigan the real polls fooled the average, because when polls share a mistake the average shares it too. Printing the margin of error changed nothing about what readers took away, and a poll can help decide who gets on a debate stage. Report a poll as one reading of a race, inside the story, never as the headline.

What both sides quietly agree on

The real error in polls is about twice the printed margin. Misses have run both ways over time, though the last three presidential elections leaned the same way, and primary polls are rougher readings. August's fake polls reached a campaign and a news outlet, but not the big averages.

What the shouting gets wrong

Nobody defends a made-up poll, and nobody defends treating one number as a result. The design that survives both teams: no single undisclosed poll as a headline, averages from pollsters who disclose with the real error said out loud, and primaries labelled as rougher readings. What's left is how much weight a poll gets in your head before you vote.

The verdict

Lena Both teams accepted the same record. The real error is about twice the printed margin. Misses have run both ways over time, though the last three presidential elections leaned the same way. Primaries are rougher readings. And August's fake polls reached a campaign and a news outlet, but not the big averages.

Lena What survived from green. The averages' disclosure gates caught every fake. Twenty twenty-four's state polls were the most accurate in eighty years. And hiding polls doesn't hide them. France's neighbours published anyway.

Lena What survived from gold. The average missed Michigan by about twelve, because averaging cancels noise, not a mistake the polls share. A printed margin didn't change what readers took away. And a poll already helps decide things before the vote.

Lena What fell. Green's comfort: an average of honest polls can still be wrong together, and it was. Gold's fear: nobody has shown that playing polls down leaves anyone better informed.

Lena So here's the design that survives both teams. Never a single undisclosed poll as a headline: say who paid, who answered, how many and when. Averages from pollsters who disclose, with the real error said out loud. And primaries labelled as the rougher readings they are.

Lena That leaves one dial neither team can set for you. How much weight does a poll get in your head? Ignore single polls, like France's blackout. Averages only, like the aggregators' gates. A forecast with a band about twice the printed margin. Or news, the habit that carried August's fakes.

Lena So the question we're handing you isn't trust polls or don't. It's this: how much weight does a poll get in your head before you vote? That one's yours.
