## The research · How wrong real polls usually are
**Raj:** Further than they say.
**Cole:** Give me the honest number.
**Raj:** One study looked at more than four thousand state polls, across sixteen years of elections. The real error was about twice what the printed margin of error implies. A poll's stated ninety-five percent range caught the actual result less often than it claims. For Senate polls, about three times in four.
**Cole:** Twice the margin. So the plus-or-minus three is a promise it can't keep.
**Raj:** Not on its own. And polls of the same race tend to be wrong together. So averaging them cancels the noise, but not the shared mistake.
**Cole:** Wrong in whose favour, though?
**Raj:** Both. The pollsters' own review says Democrats and Republicans have each been underestimated about equally often since the dawn of modern polling. In two thousand, and again in twenty twelve, the polls overstated the Republican. And the last three presidential elections all overstated the Democrat. The review says the cause is unclear.
**Cole:** And the midterms?
**Raj:** Twenty eighteen leaned a tenth of a point toward Democrats. Twenty twenty-two leaned about half a point toward Republicans. Tiny. But individual Senate and governor races still missed by four to five points on average.
**Cole:** So the errors cancel out, but each race can still be off.
**Raj:** Right. And twenty twenty-four was actually good. State presidential polls were the most accurate in eighty years, about three points off. House polls still missed by about seven.
**Cole:** Why is it so hard?
**Raj:** Fewer people answer. At Pew, about one in three answered a phone survey in the late nineties. By twenty eighteen, about one in sixteen. So pollsters have to weight their samples. Pew says no one approach is clearly best everywhere.
**Cole:** So the honest number is twice the printed one, in either direction. Hold that.

## The research · What a poll decides — and what you take from it
**Raj:** Three stops: the debate stage, the reader, and what's been tried.
**Cole:** The debate stage? It's just a number.
**Raj:** It can decide who's on the debate stage. Michigan's televised debates in October use Nexstar's rules. A candidate must clear all five tests, and one is a poll: at least ten percent in a general election. If there's no poll, the other tests decide.
**Cole:** Does that keep people off the stage?
**Raj:** We found no case of it this year. And it shapes coverage. In twenty sixteen, policy was about a tenth of general-election news. Less than a quarter of the space given to who was winning.
**Cole:** So print the margin of error and people will read it right.
**Raj:** That's been tested. More than four thousand people were shown the same race. Adding a plus-or-minus two percent changed nothing about what they took away. And when told one side had an eighty-seven percent chance of winning, the average person read it as about seventy.
**Cole:** Then do polls change how people vote?
**Raj:** In a Dutch experiment with more than twenty-three thousand people, plain poll numbers didn't move vote intentions. Stressing that a party was rising gave it a small boost. We found no comparable test in a real American election.
**Cole:** Has anyone just banned them?
**Raj:** France bans publishing polls from the start of the Saturday before a vote until the polls close. In twenty seventeen, outlets across the border in Belgium and Switzerland published election-day exit polls anyway.
**Cole:** And can you sue a pollster for being wrong?
**Raj:** Someone tried, over an Iowa poll before the twenty twenty-four election. A federal judge dismissed it. A poll of a future event isn't a false statement of fact. The appeal was argued in September. No decision yet.
**Cole:** So which design actually leaves voters better informed?
**Raj:** We couldn't find a study that shows any of them does. Not averages only, not wider bands, not less coverage.
**Cole:** So a poll already decides things, readers don't read the margin, and nobody has measured a fix. Hold that.

## The verdict
