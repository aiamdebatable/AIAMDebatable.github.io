Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in late October 2026, just before the November vote, the FBI held Fulton County, Georgia's 2020 ballots, seized that January. Posts were also claiming the Post Office wouldn't deliver ballots to states that refused to hand over their voter rolls.

Settled

What the record has settled

Almost every viral clip the episode opened, from every side, was explained: ordinary steps that look sinister without the rulebook, or footage that was staged. Real fraud does happen, but it was small and caught by records, and in the record checked, six races were thrown out or run again, none statewide. Fulton's double scans and repeated images are proven errors, and whether any of it was intentional is still open.

What people get wrong

The famous State Farm clip showed real ballots, already opened, stored under a table, and Georgia's hand count of about five million paper ballots matched the machines to within about a tenth of a percent. The better questions to ask of a clip are what should be on screen if it were normal, and who has already checked. The record leans hard but not all the way: fraud nobody caught can't be counted, so nobody should claim a number for it, either way.

What to watch: When the next one lands, how would you tell?

This episode was an explainer, so nobody argued sides and there's no dial to set. The verdict sorted the clips into four trays: explained, proven, open, and not checkable. For the next clip, it hands you two questions to ask instead of just "is this fake?"

  1. What should be on screen if this were normal. Two officials, a sealed container, a form: that's the process working. Georgia's 2020 rules called for drop boxes on video, collected by at least two people with a signed transfer form. The famous State Farm clip showed real ballots, already opened, sealed in carriers under a table. It didn't show fraud, and it didn't show nothing: the real problems in that room were too dull to film.
  2. Who has already checked. Find the first upload, search a still from it, and read sideways: search what your local election office says about the clip. Ask what paper trail would exist if it were true, and who has already followed it. Proven fraud, like North Carolina's in 2018 and Bridgeport's in 2023, was caught by records, not by a clip. If it checks out, it's real and should be followed up. Nobody has tested whether viewers can use this whole method on election videos; it's what the investigators in these cases did.
