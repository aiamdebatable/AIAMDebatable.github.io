Isle Royale Wolves and Moose: Who Is the Island For?

The Park Service put 19 wolves back on Isle Royale in 2018-19; the moose are down 75%, one food-conditioned wolf has been shot, and tent camping is banned at four campgrounds through October. Who is the island for — and when the wilderness stops working, who intervenes, on which animal?

The claims
  • Confirmed

    "Since 28 August 2026, tents, group camping and hammocks are banned at Three Mile, Daisy Farm, Moskey Basin and Caribou Island through the season's end on 31 October — 13 tent sites, 8 group sites, the surrounding off-trail zones; 32 shelters stay open."

  • Confirmed

    "Early July a wolf got inside a shelter, tore the screen and chewed a cooler; the park closed that campground twice and called the first round ineffective. From 9 July a wolf entered a tent at Three Mile and dragged packs; rangers hazed it with air horns and paintball guns."

  • Confirmed

    "In mid-August 2025 the park lethally removed one food-conditioned wolf after aversive conditioning, waste-management changes and food-storage rules failed; at least two wolves had shown the behaviour in June–July; it said a second remained."

  • Confirmed

    "Summer 2024: wolves started taking food and garbage around the harbour; the park issued its first food-storage guidance. Spring 2025: hard-sided animal-resistant containers became mandatory."

  • Confirmed

    "37 wolves in three packs, the most since the late 1970s; about 500 moose (524), down 75% since 2019; predation rate 24% against a ~10% long-run average; no calves for the first time in 68 years."

  • Confirmed

    "By 2017 the island's own wolves had inbred down to two, a father and his daughter."

  • Confirmed

    "In 2018 the Park Service decided to put wolves back: 19 released over a year from Minnesota, Ontario and Michigan's Upper Peninsula."

  • Confirmed

    "The 2018 decision called this a one-time act: no more wolves after five years; it rejected a version that would have kept restocking for twenty."

  • Confirmed

    "The federal wildlife agency signed off with: potential human/wolf conflict on Isle Royale is very low and/or non-existent given the wilderness character of the island."

  • Confirmed

    "Before the Park Service may remove a native animal, its own policy says it must first try to fix the conflict by changing what the visitors do; only if that can't work may it relocate or destroy the animal."

  • Confirmed

    "Feeding or disturbing wildlife is a federal offence in every park; on Isle Royale the ceiling is $5,000 and six months. A ranger said visitors who ignore the storage rules can be cited. No citation, fine or prosecution was found here."

  • Confirmed

    "Every park statement blames food left out and packs left unattended, not feeding. No park record of a visitor feeding a wolf was found. What exists is testimony in the island's own online groups."

  • Confirmed

    "Isle Royale is 45 miles long, in Lake Superior, closer to Canada than to Michigan; no roads; ferry or seaplane; under 30,000 visits a year (29,091 in 2025), the least visited national park in the lower 48."

  • Confirmed

    "It closes completely from November to April, the only national park that shuts for winter; almost the whole island is designated wilderness (1976), 'a visitor who does not remain'."

  • Confirmed

    "A closed system: no bears, no deer, no hunting, no roads. Moose swam out early last century; wolves crossed the ice from Canada in the late 1940s. Counted every winter since 1958 — the longest predator–prey study in the world."

  • Confirmed

    "The 1931 act says 'a public park for the benefit and enjoyment of the people' — nothing about science or wolves."

  • Confirmed

    "The park's foundation document calls the wolf–moose relationship the essence of the island's wilderness character."

  • Confirmed

    "The 2018 decision says nothing about removing a wolf. Public hunting is barred by law. A moose cull was considered and dismissed. Managing the moose at all would need a new plan."

  • Confirmed

    "The Wilderness Stewardship Plan's final study (2024) has never been signed. Its preferred version allows larger groups and lets most campsites be booked in advance; the version that cuts campsites, adds reservations and removes shelters is the one the agency did not prefer."

  • Confirmed

    "The closures came from the superintendent's ordinary closure power, a standing rule any park has."

  • Confirmed

    "In June the Interior Department opened a review of the Park Service's whole wilderness policy; comments closed in August; no outcome yet."

  • Confirmed

    "The wolves were federally endangered in Michigan the day one was shot; whether the park held a permit to kill one could not be found."

  • Confirmed

    "Yellowstone, twice: hazed with cracker shells, bean bags and rubber bullets, then killed. Around seventy hazing episodes in five years and two animals killed."

  • Confirmed

    "Banff: two wolves shot in one summer (2016) after aversive conditioning failed. In September 2025 the park charged campers whose garbage a wolf reached; a court fined them a few hundred dollars ($350); the wolf was watched, not shot."

  • Confirmed

    "A provincial park in Ontario: every wolf that bit a person was shot afterward."

  • Confirmed

    "Yosemite: over a thousand bear incidents a year in the late 1990s; with canisters, lockers, education and enforcement, incidents were down more than 90% by 2015."

  • Confirmed

    "The island on the Canadian side: wolves crossed the ice in 2014 and ate its caribou from several hundred to almost none; Ontario airlifted the last 15 out, then let the Park Service take the starving wolves; by 2022 it had no wolves and no caribou."

  • Confirmed

    "The Grand Portage Band's biologist: 'they eat all the food, they become overpopulated, and then they either kill each other or they die of starvation'; the Band did not want the wolves brought in."

  • Confirmed

    "The wolves crashed to fourteen in the 1980s; the moose crashed to about five hundred in the 1990s and doubled back by themselves."

  • Confirmed

    "The first time anyone put wolves on Isle Royale was 1952, four wolves from the Detroit Zoo; the experiment failed primarily because the wolves became habituated to humans; two or three were removed."

  • Confirmed

    "Mean annual survival 0.74; 27 wolves by April 2023; more than 80% modelled chance of persisting 20 years despite high inbreeding." (on screen only)
