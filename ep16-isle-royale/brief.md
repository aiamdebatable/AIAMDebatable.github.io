# Research brief — Who Is Isle Royale For?

_The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video._

## The question
The National Park Service put 19 wolves back on Isle Royale in 2018–19 after the native population
collapsed to two inbred animals. Seven years on, the wolves are at 37, the moose are down 75% and no
calf was seen in the 2026 winter survey. And the wolves are in the campgrounds: one was shot in
August 2025 for living on human food, and since 28 August 2026 tents are banned at four of the
island's busiest campgrounds through the end of the season. Who is the island for — and when a
restocked wilderness stops working, who intervenes, on which animal?

## Why now
**As of 2026-09-13.** On 28 August 2026 Isle Royale National Park prohibited tent and group camping
and hammock use at Three Mile, Daisy Farm, Moskey Basin and Caribou Island through the park's
31 October season close — 13 tent sites, 8 group sites and the surrounding off-trail zones, leaving
32 shelters open — because, in the park's words, wolves keep "pawing and digging at tents and entering
tents to drag packs and sleeping pads away." It is the second wolf closure of 2026: Duncan Narrows was
shut 6–8 July after a wolf got inside a shelter (and again 13–31 July after the first round of
closure-plus-hazing was declared ineffective), and Three Mile from 9 July, with staff hazing the
animal with paintball guns, air horns and motion-triggered noise. A year earlier, on 15 August 2025,
the park lethally removed a food-conditioned wolf after aversive conditioning, waste-management
changes and food-storage rules failed; it said a second remained. Five months ago the 2026 winter
study counted 37 wolves — the most since the late 1970s — and 524 moose, down 75% from 2019, with no
calves observed for the first time in 68 years and balsam fir growth at a record low.

The closure is the argument in public: the park's response to wolves in the tents was to remove the
tents, not the wolves. That is the design choice this episode is about.

## The island (why anyone should care about a rock in the middle of Lake Superior)
Set the scene before the science. Verified 2026-09-13 unless marked.
- **Where.** 45 miles long, 9 miles wide at its widest — the largest island in the largest freshwater
  lake on Earth — with more than 450 smaller islands around it. It is 56 miles from Michigan's Upper
  Peninsula and 15 miles from the Canadian shore: a Michigan park that is closer to Canada and
  Minnesota than to Michigan. (Britannica; NPS)
- **How you get there.** No roads, no cars, no bridge. A ferry from Houghton (the NPS's own Ranger
  III, six hours), from Copper Harbor, or from Grand Portage, Minnesota — or a seaplane. The seat
  supply is the visitor cap: 25–30k visits a year. 29,091 recreation visits in 2025 — the least
  visited national park in the lower 48; 2024–25 was its busiest two-year stretch since 1987–88.
  (NPS visitation release; secondary for the seat-cap framing)
- **The only park that closes.** Isle Royale shuts entirely from 1 November to 15 April; the
  headquarters moves off Mott Island to Houghton for the winter. (NPS hours page)
- **What it is by law.** Authorised in 1931 "as a public park for the benefit and enjoyment of the
  people"; 132,018 acres — 99% of the land — designated wilderness on 20 Oct 1976; an International
  Biosphere Reserve since 1980. (16 U.S.C. §408; NPS)
- **Why the science is here and nowhere else.** A closed system: no bears, no deer, no hunting, no
  roads, one predator and one large prey. Moose swam out in the early 1900s; wolves walked across an
  ice bridge from Ontario in the winter of 1948–49. Since 1958 the same island has been counted every
  winter from a small plane — the longest continuous predator–prey study in the world. The island is
  the petri dish; everything the mainland argues about (ep15) plays out here with the variables
  removed. (Wolf–Moose Project; FEIS 2018)
- **Lucas's own scene (background for the writer, never on air — pseudonymous channel):** a 2024
  end-to-end thru-hike; Three Mile and Daisy Farm are the first two campgrounds out of Rock Harbor,
  which is why they are where the wolves and the backpackers meet.

## The history the episode needs (Lucas's ask #1)
- **The study.** Wolves reached the island over an ice bridge in the late 1940s. Durward Allen began
  the wolf–moose study in 1958; it is the longest continuous predator–prey study anywhere. Wolves have
  ranged 12–50 and moose 385–2,422. (Wolf–Moose Project; 2018 FEIS.)
- **The first intervention failed for today's reason.** In 1952 four Detroit Zoo wolves were released;
  "the experiment was not successful, primarily due to human habituation by the wolves" (FEIS 2018,
  citing Mech 1966). Two or three were removed.
- **The 1980s crash.** Wolves peaked at 50 in 1980 and fell to 14 by 1982 — canine parvovirus, carried
  in by an illegally landed dog. Recovery took ~15 years.
- **The 1996 crash.** Moose peaked ~2,400 and collapsed to ~500 after a severe winter and starvation
  (the annual report's own cause; not primarily ticks). They quadrupled back to ~900 by 2001 unaided.
- **The inbreeding collapse.** One immigrant wolf in 1997 briefly refreshed the gene pool; by 2011
  nine wolves remained, and by 2017 two — M183 and his daughter F193, who also shared a mother —
  with bone deformities documented. M183 was later killed by the new wolves.
- **The decision.** Record of Decision, 7 June 2018: Alternative B, 20–30 wolves within three years,
  supplementation only to year 3 (to year 5 only for an unforeseen die-off), "no additional wolves
  will be brought to the island after 5 years." The NPS rejected Alternative C — restock as needed
  for 20 years — precisely because it meant repeated intervention. The USFWS concurrence said
  "potential human/wolf conflict on Isle Royale is very low and/or non-existent given the wilderness
  character of the island."
- **The wolves.** 19 released Sep 2018–Sep 2019: Grand Portage Reservation (MN, 2018), Jostle Lake
  and Michipicoten Island (ON, Feb–Mar 2019), Michigan's Upper Peninsula (Sep 2019). The Michipicoten
  wolves were the pack that had eaten that island's caribou to near zero after crossing ice in 2014;
  Ontario airlifted the last 15 caribou out in 2017–18, then let the NPS take the starving wolves. By
  2022 Michipicoten had neither. Those wolves are now Isle Royale's dominant lineage.
- **Since.** Mean annual survival 0.74; 27 wolves by April 2023; eight early deaths, most from other
  wolves; a modelled >80% chance of persisting 20 years "despite high levels of inbreeding."

## Settled (not the debate)
- The 2018 decision was a one-shot, minimum-intervention act: it caps adding wolves after five years
  and says nothing about removing one. — ROD 2018
- The conflict escalated over three seasons (Jul 2024 → Aug 2026) and the park used the human-side
  toolkit FIRST — guidance (Jul 2024), mandatory animal-resistant containers (Apr 2025), hazing,
  electric fencing, waste changes — before killing one wolf on 15 Aug 2025. That order is what NPS
  Management Policies §4.4.2.1 requires: modify visitor use first, remove the animal only if that
  cannot work. — NPS releases; Management Policies 2006
- One wolf has been killed. A second food-conditioned wolf was reported still in the park; 2026
  brought more closures, not more removals. — NPS releases
- Every NPS release attributes the conflict to improperly stored food and unattended gear — never to
  deliberate feeding. The research found no park record and no identifiable social-media post of a
  visitor feeding a wolf. — dossier F5 (⚠ Lucas has seen such posts; see Open questions)
- The 2026 closures ran on the superintendent's ordinary closure authority (36 CFR §1.5, via the
  Compendium updated 25 Jun 2026), not on any new plan. Feeding wildlife is barred by 36 CFR §2.2;
  the ceiling is $5,000 and six months. No citation at Isle Royale was found — a search null, not a
  checked incident log. — Compendium; Bridge Michigan (ranger Liz Valencia: citations "possible")
- The Wilderness Stewardship Plan has no Record of Decision two years after its Final EIS (Aug 2024).
  Its PREFERRED Alternative B expands access — group size to 12, advance permits, campgrounds booked to
  85%. The visitor-restricting menu — reservation system, fewer campsites, 20 shelters removed, no
  commercial use — is the non-preferred Alternative C. — WSP FEIS (opened by hand)
- The 2026 winter study: 37 wolves in three packs; 524 moose, −75% since 2019; predation 24% against
  a 10% long-run average; zero calves for the first time in 68 years; balsam fir growth 2.7 cm, a
  record low, even though browsing fell from >80% to 4%. — Michigan Tech annual report 2025–26
- Nobody in the record proposes managing the numbers. Public hunting is barred by law; a managed cull
  was dismissed in the FEIS; direct moose management is "outside the scope" and would need its own
  plan; no moose contraceptive is approved. — FEIS 2018
- Every completed wolf-habituation case found elsewhere (Yellowstone 2009 and 2011, Banff 2016,
  Algonquin 1963–98) ended with the animal killed after hazing failed. Yellowstone also logged 72
  hazing attempts in 2007–12 against two removals, so most hazing episodes do not end in a death. The
  one clean "manage the humans" success is Yosemite's BEARS: incidents down ~95% from 1998 to 2015
  under canisters, lockers, education AND enforcement. — dossier F9, F10
- Isle Royale's wolves were ESA-endangered on the day one was shot (the 2022 vacatur re-listed them;
  the park's own 2024 FEIS says so). Whether the removal carried a take authorisation was not found.

## Contested (the real fight)
**Green — Intervene on the animals:** the 2018 decision promised crash-avoidance and forest recovery
and delivered a 75% moose decline, a zero-calf year and record-low fir growth; the FEIS calibrated its
20–30 wolves to a historic ~30:1 moose-to-wolf ratio and 2026 sits near 14:1. The ROD's own logic —
intervene once to correct a human-caused collapse — applies again if the founders narrow to one
Michipicoten lineage. On the campground problem: the human-side toolkit ran for 13 months before the
kill and the conflict worsened after it; every escalated wolf case in North America ended lethally; a
pack predator is not a re-trainable bear; and the tent ban is itself the heaviest human-side tool
short of closing the park — it followed the kill, it did not replace it.

**Gold — Manage the humans and stand back:** the ROD anticipated exactly this and chose to let it run;
the 1980s and 1990s show both species recovering unaided; and every intervention on this island has
been sold as the last one (1952, 2018). On the campground problem: "manage the humans" was applied
late and incrementally — guidance in 2024, canisters not mandatory until April 2025, pack cages only
in 2026, penalties never located in use — so it was never tried at Yosemite intensity. Closures are
the correct, cheap, reversible tool; the island is wilderness by law, "where man is a visitor who
does not remain."

**The cross-cutting axis (Lucas's ask #2) — human or wolf, and who gets punished?** The record's
answer is that the ladder is fixed by policy: humans first, animal last. The live question is where
on the ladder the park should have stopped, and how much enforcement it never used. The wolf side has
one named, dated death; the human side has a $5,000 ceiling and no located citation. But nobody in the
record deliberately fed anything — the documented mechanism is unattended food and gear — so "punish
the human" aims at a compliance failure, not a feeding culture. Banff shows the other design: in
September 2025 Parks Canada charged campers whose unattended garbage a wolf reached, and a court
fined them $350; the wolf was watched, not shot.

## The reframe
The shouting treats Green and Gold as untried options. Both have been used, in a fixed order that
national policy dictates: humans first (2024–25), one animal (Aug 2025), then humans again, harder
(2026). The real choice is the rung, not the ladder. Second, the shouting fuses two stories that share
an island and nothing else in the primary record: the ecological story (37 wolves, 524 moose, no
calves) and the campground story (wolves in tents). No NPS release and no Michigan Tech scientist
connects prey scarcity to food-conditioning; Peterson declines to predict it. The one voice who does
connect them is the Grand Portage Band's biologist, Seth Moore — a population "not hunted or trapped"
with "no fear of humans whatsoever" as natural prey thins "absolutely" cues more conflict — and he is
the partner who did not want the translocation. Third, the two designs are not symmetric in legal
status: the individual-animal action has a policy pathway and a precedent; Gold's flagship tools sit
in a plan with no decision, whose preferred alternative expands access rather than restricting it.

## Both sides quietly agree
Nobody wants the numbers managed — not the NPS, not Michigan Tech, not the Band, not Wilderness Watch.
Culling wolves, feeding moose or restocking again is proposed by no one. Both accept the order of
the ladder; they differ on speed and enforcement. And both agree the real long-run risk the 2018
decision left open is genetic: one Michipicoten family dominating a population that is inbred again.

## Verdict framing
What survives both designs: the ladder (humans first, animal last, closures as the reversible step);
mandatory containers and cages; that the numbers stay unmanaged. The one dial the viewer sets: how
far up the ladder a wilderness should go before it touches the animal — and whether "who is the
island for" is answered by the 1931 act ("for the benefit and enjoyment of the people"), the 1976
wilderness designation ("where man is a visitor who does not remain"), or the 2016 Foundation
Document, which calls the wolf–moose dynamic "the essence of Isle Royale's wilderness character."
No winner declared. You're the jury.
