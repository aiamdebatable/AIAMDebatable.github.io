AI Data Centers: Should Your Town Be Able to Ban One?

When a community says no to a data center, is a moratorium the only tool that works — or the one that guarantees it gets nothing?

Seven in ten Americans say not near me. 500+ towns have paused or banned. A court just ordered one to let its voters decide. Ban it — or make them sign?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in September 2026, two governors had chosen a freeze on data centers that summer, and Pennsylvania had chosen rules with a veto for the town. On September 1, Ohio's supreme court ordered a city to let its voters decide on a ban, which went on the November 3 ballot.

Settled

What both sides accept

Both teams accepted that a county ban doesn't lower a power bill set across the region, and that the numbers a county is shown are inflated, promised or unmeasured. Both wanted no secrecy deals and a study before the vote. The design that survived both is a pause with a date, rules published before it ends, the developer signing for its full grid cost and a water cap, and the town voting last.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesRules, not bans

The power bill is set across a whole region, so a county ban doesn't lower it. Rules can bind the developer: it signs for 100% of the grid cost it causes before any permit, a water cap in gallons, no secrecy deals, and the town votes last. Meanwhile pauses have dragged on indefinitely and drawn lawsuits.

Gold arguesThe right to say no

The numbers a county is shown are inflated, promised or unmeasured, so not yet is the rational answer. Pause with three locks: a date of six to twelve months, rules published before it ends, and a limit of 25 megawatts. Rules answer how, not whether, and whether is the only question a county board gets asked.

What the shouting gets wrong

Ban versus rules turned out to be the wrong fight: pauses dragged on, rules-first counties still said no four to one, and the tool didn't predict the answer once. What matters is the date on the pause, the megawatt number on the rule, and the default. The verdict left the default to you: no until proven, or yes with the developer's signature.

The verdict

What survives both teams
A pause with a date and a deliverable, the rules published before it ends. The developer signs for a hundred percent of caused cost and a water cap in gallons before any permit. No secrecy agreements. A study before the vote. And the town votes last, and may say no.
What didn't hold up
Green's promise: the tariff stops the cost shift. Nobody has measured it, and she said so herself. Gold's promise: a pause becomes a rulebook. Sometimes, and he said so himself.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: When a building arrives that will draw a farm's worth of water and a contract's worth of power, is your county's default no, or yes with a signature?

The judge left two dials and declared no winner: the number, because it decides which buildings the rule even sees, and the default. You'd pick one of each. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

The number

  1. 1Twenty
    What it buys
    The rule sees the most buildings of the four. It's the number in New York's bill that failed, which would have caught the mid-size buildings New York's order at fifty exempts.
    What it costs
    On air, only that failed bill used it, and nobody named what a lower number costs.
  2. 2Twenty-five
    What it buys
    Gold's lock put the number here, not at fifty, so the building can't hide under it, and Green took Gold's three locks as amendments. It's Pennsylvania's number: above it, no state permit until the town has approved it.
    What it costs
    Nobody on air named a cost for it.

    On the record: “PA EO 2026-05, full text” in the fact-check log

  3. 3Fifty
    What it buys
    It's the number New York's order and Florida's law use: above it, a facility is paused or regulated.
    What it costs
    Gold's charge on air: New York's fifty exempts the mid-size buildings its own failed bill would have caught. Below the number, a facility is invisible to the rule.

    On the record: “NY EO 62” in the fact-check log

  4. 4A hundred megawatts
    What it buys
    It's Georgia's number, the highest of the four, so the rule reaches only the biggest buildings.
    What it costs
    Below the number, a facility is invisible to the rule, and this one leaves the most buildings below it.

The default

  1. 1No until proven
    What it buys
    The numbers the county is shown are inflated, promised and unmeasured, so not yet is a rational answer. Virginia's biggest utility holds 47 gigawatts of signed data-center contracts but forecasts 16.6 gigawatts of demand, and the research found no audited count of permanent jobs at any operating data center. The veto survived every rules regime, and counties kept using it. And a pause with a date and a deliverable is what Wixom and Kearney actually did.
    What it costs
    What fell from Gold: the promise that a pause turns into a set of rules. Sometimes, and he said so himself. A pause gets you a delay, and temporary has meant indefinite: New York's has no end date, and DeKalb County, Georgia, has extended its pause to March 2027 and is being sued. Hill County, Texas, was sued for $100 million and rescinded its pause.
    Not measured
    What the pauses that ended became: about 70 of more than 500 have ended, and nobody has coded what they ended into.

    On the record: “Dominion: 47 GW of executed contracts vs 16.6…” in the fact-check log, “No audited permanent-jobs figure” in the fact-check log, “Moratorium Nation 533 / 42 states; CC-BY CSV” in the fact-check log, “DeKalb County GA moratorium: adopted 8 Jul 2025…” in the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), “Hill County TX: moratorium rescinded within weeks of…” in the fact-check log

  2. 2Yes under conditions
    What it buys
    A county ban does not lower a regional bill: the grid's market monitor puts 46% of the capacity charges in its last four auctions, about $29 billion, on data centers, and that charge is spread across the whole region. Rules get you a signature on a number; a pause gets you a delay. Pennsylvania's rule makes the developer pay 100% of the grid cost it causes, and the town approves first. The town keeps its no: under rules, Palm Beach and Pinal counties both said no, four to one.
    What it costs
    What fell from Green: the tariff's promise for household bills. Nobody has measured it, and she said so herself: Virginia's starts in January, Ohio's is a year old with no review published, and the big companies' pledge is, in Pennsylvania's own word, non-binding. And a yes under rules still draws the lawyer: Prince William County's approval was voided over its public notices, and the county spent more defending it before the project was withdrawn.
    Not measured
    Whether any tariff works on household bills: the research looked for one measured billing cycle, anywhere, and found none.

    On the record: “PJM IMM 38% / $6.3bn of $16.4bn latest…” in the fact-check log, “PA EO 2026-05, full text” in the fact-check log, “No post-implementation measurement of any large-load tariff” in the fact-check log, “Palm Beach denied Project Tango 5–1” in the fact-check log (our draft, corrected), “Prince William: voided Aug 2025 on a "three-day…” in the fact-check log (our draft, corrected)

Whichever you set: Both teams built you a town that makes the developer sign and keeps the veto. Either way, the bill is set three states away, where neither design can reach it, and both designs get you a lawyer's bill.

What our fact-check caught

6 of the 27 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidDeKalb County, Georgia's pause runs through September 2026.

    The record saysIt was extended again in July 2026, to March 2027, and the county has been sued.

  2. Draft saidVirginia's tariff charges data centers at least 85% of wires costs and 60% of generation costs, with $1.5 million of collateral per megawatt.

    The record saysIt's a 14-year contract: at least 85% of wires costs whether the power is used or not, and collateral of up to 60% of the minimum charges. There's no generation minimum and no per-megawatt figure.

  3. Draft saidA judge voided Prince William County's approval over a notice that ran three days late.

    The record saysThe first required notice never ran, and later ones missed the six-day gap the law requires. An appeals court agreed, and the project was withdrawn in July 2026.

  4. Draft saidPalm Beach County turned down the project five to one.

    The record saysFour to one.

  5. Draft saidData centers pay about a third of Loudoun County's property tax, and its rate fell about 30% in a decade.

    The record saysThe county's own figures: $1.2 billion, 39% of its whole budget for the year, and that year it cut its rate by six cents.
