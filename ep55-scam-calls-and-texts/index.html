Gift Card Scams: Why Smart People Still Pay, and Who Could Have Stopped It

Whose job is it to stop a scam: yours, your family's, your bank's, the store's, or the law's?

One call, followed stop by stop: the family, the bank teller, the cashier. Who could have stopped it?

The verdict

Read the verdict as it aired

Nia So, whose job is it to stop it? First, what the record settles.

Nia The script is built to beat every person on that path. Warnings alone don't stop it. And the protections anyone has measured sit where the money leaves, at the counter and the register, not at the phone.

Nia Who should carry it is a judgement. So here's the dial. Five settings: our four stops, plus the law. Each has something real behind it, and a catch.

Nia You. Learn the signs, hang up, call back on a number you look up yourself. It's free, and the F.B.I. recommends it. But no study has measured what that advice prevents.

Nia Your family. Be the person she calls before she pays, and ask to be named as her trusted contact at the bank. People with someone to talk to lose money less often. But the script says tell no one, and families usually hear afterwards.

Nia The bank. Train the tellers, and pause a payment that looks wrong. Trained staff stopped about seven times as much in a trial. But only about half the states allow a hold, half the banks there have never used one, and customers push back.

Nia The store. Let the cashier refuse the sale. A refusal works better than a question. But no law we found requires it, and fewer than half the stores in one study set any limit.

Nia The law. Make banks refund scam victims, the way Britain now does, where most of the money claimed is being paid back. But America has no refund right for a payment you send yourself, and Britain's drop in scams is an estimate, not a trial. Your setting is your call.

The judge named no sides on this one, so there's no dial: we don't invent positions.
