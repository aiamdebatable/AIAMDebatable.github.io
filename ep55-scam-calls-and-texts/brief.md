## The reframe (the episode's spine)
**The phone is the front door; the money leaves at the payment.** Almost every protection people repeat is aimed
at the phone (don't answer, code words, filters), and none of them has outcome data. The protections with measured
effects sit at the moment of payment: an interrupting warning with a cancel button, a fast report that lets the FBI
freeze money still in a bank, and (abroad) a rule that makes banks refund scam victims. In the US, a payment you were
tricked into SENDING yourself carries no legal right to a refund; one a scammer made with your stolen login or code
does. That single distinction is the most useful thing the viewer can learn.

_Under the ruled shape the reframe still holds, now with people in it: the measured protections sit where the money
leaves, which is exactly where the teller and the cashier stand._

## The playbook: why warned, smart people still pay (top-up D)
- **The regulators wrote it down.** The FTC, in one sentence: scammers keep older adults on the phone, send them to the
  bank, a gift-card store or a Bitcoin ATM, coach what to say to bank staff and tell them not to talk to family (D1).
  For gift cards: stay on the line the whole time, and here is what to tell a cashier who asks (D2). The FTC's own
  signs include "they might tell you not to hang up so you can't check out their story" (D3). The FBI describes
  stacked impersonations (tech support, then "your bank", then "the government") and an order to keep the real reason
  secret (D4). FinCEN describes the method as trust, then pressure through emotion or fear (D6); a Senate chair
  described cutting the victim off "so that no one can warn him or her" (D8). Researchers called their transcripts
  "the con man's playbook" (D9, 2006).
- **It turns caution into the weapon.** Fake security alerts prey on older adults' vigilance about protecting their
  money (D7).
- **"Smart people fall for it" is half true, and the true half is the useful one.** For the mostly imposter and
  tech-support scams in a 2019 survey of 1,408 people, education made little or no difference (D12); the "more
  educated victims" finding is from investment fraud (D13, D14). What went with losing money was the moment: the caller
  seeming official, time pressure, feeling intimidated (D10), and having no one to talk to (D11). In the lab, emotion
  drove people's intention to buy even when they didn't find the pitch believable (D19), in older and younger adults
  alike (D18).
- **Warnings help briefly and often produce a doubtful yes.** A warning call cut acceptance of a sting offer from 75%
  to 37% (D21, 2003); in a field experiment, warnings cut confident yeses but raised "skeptical acceptance": saying yes
  while voicing doubt (D22). That is "I felt something wasn't right, and went ahead" in research form. The FTC's own
  review: even in-the-moment warnings often fail (D20).
- **Someone stepping in is the best-supported idea, not a proven one.** About half of people who reported a third
  party stepping in (a teller, wire staff, a checkout) kept their money (D23; self-reported, no comparison group). No
  study tests a relative, teller or cashier interrupting a live call with a control group (D27).
- **Sworn accounts, by role only:** the Utah grandmother (D28, D29; a wire); a retired nurse educator with "sharp
  financial instincts" who was coached to tell her bank she knew the payee, wired $20,000, then hid it from her sons
  and blamed herself (D31; a wire); a Maine woman kept on the phone for eight hours and sent to several stores, some of
  which tried to warn her (D30; news; gift cards).
- **Brain health:** low scam awareness has been linked to later dementia risk; say "a possible early sign", never "a
  test" (D16, CORRECTED).

## The bank stop: what a bank may and may not do (top-up E)
- **Federal law protects the report, not the hold.** The Senior Safe Act shields a bank for **reporting** suspected
  exploitation of someone 65 or older (E1, E2), covers supervisors and compliance staff rather than a teller acting
  alone (E3), protects the bank only if it trained its staff (E4), and does **not** cover holding or refusing a
  transaction (E5). Privacy law is no bar to reporting (E6). FinCEN asks banks to detect and report; it grants no hold
  power (E7). Its red flag for the counter: an older customer taking directions from someone on a cell phone and
  unwilling to hang up (D5).
- **Holds come from state law.** About half the states let a bank pause a payment when it suspects exploitation (E11,
  E17: APPROXIMATE, say "about half"). As of October 2024, 26 states plus DC had no such law, including California, New
  York, Illinois, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Georgia, North Carolina and Ohio (E18; re-check any state before naming
  it). Texas and Florida are examples of states with one (E19). No general federal right to refuse an adult's own
  withdrawal was found (E20, UNRESOLVED), and a bank that wrongly refuses can be liable (E20, E21); regulators have
  penalized banks for restricting account access (E12).
- **The teller's bind is real, in both halves.** In a state without a hold law, "the bank can't keep you from your own
  money" is roughly right; in a hold-law state it's wrong (E5, E11, E18).
- **Where the law allows it, most of the room goes unused.** Half the surveyed banks in hold-law states had ever used
  their law (E22). More than 45% of banks that used holds saw customers react badly, about 17% saw customers close
  accounts, and one banker put it as "damned if we do and damned if we don't" (E23); 40% were uncertain or worried
  about lawsuits (E24). Whether a hold works rests only on banks' own view (E25, APPROXIMATE, off air).
- **What is optional.** A bank may let you name a trusted contact but doesn't have to (E13); brokerages must ask for
  one (E16) and have a federal hold rule (E15); a trusted contact can't see the account or move money (AF28). Handing
  customers material is encouraged, never required (E14). Industry campaigns exist but are opt-in (E26; E27 is
  APPROXIMATE).
- **Trained staff stop more.** In AARP's randomized BankSafe trial, trained staff stopped $900,915 from leaving
  accounts against $54,384 for untrained staff: 16 times as much, or about 7 times without one $500,000 case; AARP runs
  and evaluated the program, and the savings are self-reported (E28, E29). In the CFPB's study of banks' elder-
  exploitation reports (2013–2017 data), only 15% describe an attempt with no money lost (E30). UK only: branch staff
  who call police under the Banking Protocol reported £58.8 million prevented in 2025, an industry count (E32).
- **The peg's bank half:** from November 25, 2026, a bank may send fraud alerts to a number a spouse or family member
  on the account supplied (E33); it is a reach rule, not a power to hold money (E34).

## The gift-card register (top-up F, rows GC)
- **No US law found caps how many gift cards a store may sell one person** (GC1; "no law we found": not every state's
  bills were read). Caps are each chain's own choice, pushed by two attorneys general in 2018 (GC2). Published: Best
  Buy, $500 per Best Buy card and "good faith" efforts to hold daily sales to $2,000 per person (GC4); Target, 5
  Visa/Mastercard/Amex cards per guest per day (GC5). Walmart and the grocery chains: we couldn't find a published
  limit, which is not the same as "there is none" (GC6, UNRESOLVED). "$200" is not confirmed as a cap anywhere (GC5).
- **What states require is mostly a sign.** Six states have merchant rules; only Maryland and New Jersey add staff
  training (GC12, GC8, GC9, GC10). New Jersey's own guidance lists a purchase cap only as a voluntary best practice
  (GC11). Most of 2025's new laws target card tampering, not victims at the till (GC13, APPROXIMATE). Pending:
  Pennsylvania SB1124 and the federal SWIPES Act, status unresolved (GC14, GC15). The FTC's role is voluntary best
  practices and a free retailer kit (GC16, GC17, D35); neither sets a dollar cap.
- **The scammer plans for the cashier:** victims are coached on what to tell a clerk who asks (GC3, D2, D23) and sent
  to several stores (GC22, D32).
- **Refusing beats asking.** In interviews with 27 gift-card victims, a cashier's questions worked less well than an
  outright refusal (D24); AARP says the store's policy must let a cashier refuse (GC19). Yet cashiers say they feel they
  can't refuse a customer who insists, and fewer than half reported any purchase limit (D25). A secret-shopper audit of
  23 stores: nearly 80% had warning signs, fewer than half capped the amount, and the stores' rules would fully stop
  about 9% of scam purchases (GC7). No study measures whether signs or cashier prompts cut losses (GC18).
- **Size:** gift and reload cards, $212 million reported lost in 2024, all ages (GC20); $139 million for people 60+ in
  2025 (GC21). For older adults, gift cards were the most frequent payment on tech-support, romance, family- and
  friend-impersonation and prize scams (D34, GC21).
- **After the sale:** a card company can freeze only money still on the card, and no recovery rate exists (GC23,
  AF19).
- **The ending:** the Utah grocery cashier who refused the sale (GC25; news; by role only).

## The family, and after (top-up G, rows AF)
- **The family hears it first, often alone.** In the government's victim survey, 77% of fraud victims told family or
  friends and 36% told only them; about 1 in 7 told police (AF1). For people 60+, 19% told police (AF2). Reports to
  the authorities run at roughly 2 to 7 in 100 by the FTC's estimates (AF3).
- **Shame is why the first conversation matters.** Nearly half of victims blame themselves and 61% say they were "too
  trusting" (AF5); "it wouldn't have made a difference" and embarrassment top the reasons not to report (AF4).
- **During the scam:** secrecy is part of the script (AF29, D1); scripts are built to beat the teller's and cashier's
  questions (AF24); people with no one to talk to were more likely to lose money (AF25, D11).
- **In this episode:** one line at the family stop. The FTC's advice to the helper: "The only person at fault here is
  the scammer" (AF14; advice, untested, AF18).
- **Carried by the planned follow-up episode, not ep55:** the second scam ("recovery" offers built on lists of past
  victims, fake FBI complaint-centre staff: AF11, AF12, AF13); where to call (Elder Fraud Hotline 833-372-8311, AF15;
  AARP's helpline and support groups, AF16, AF17); the first-hour steps (AF19, AF20); APS may not take a stranger-scam
  case (AF21); some scam losses are tax-deductible, not tax advice (AF22); the emotional toll (AF6, AF7, AF9).
  ⛔ Never air "1 in 5 older victims report family problems": the primary says 1 in 20 (AF8, CORRECTED).

## Settled (what the record shows)
- **Reported losses hit a record in 2025:** about 3 million fraud reports and $15.9 billion reported lost (FTC
  Consumer Sentinel, FTC testimony to the Joint Economic Committee, March 2026; the FTC rounds it to "about $16
  billion"), up from about $12.5 billion in 2024. These are REPORTED losses; the FTC's own modeled estimate of the
  true cost in 2025 runs from about $38 billion to $262 billion depending on assumptions.
- **Investment scams are the biggest by dollars** ($7.9 billion, about half); imposter scams are the most reported
  (over 1 million reports, over $3.5 billion), and bank impersonators caused the largest business-impersonation losses
  (FTC, June 2026).
- **Text is the most-reported contact method, but not where most money goes:** social media led by dollars ($2.1
  billion in 2025). In 2024 (the latest all-ages split): phone calls $948 million (median loss $1,500), texts $470
  million (median $1,000), social media about $1.9 billion (median $409) (FTC Consumer Sentinel Data Book 2024).
- **The money leaves mostly by bank transfer and crypto:** in 2024, bank transfers or payments $2.09 billion and
  cryptocurrency $1.42 billion; gift cards $212 million; credit cards are used most often but lose the least
  (Data Book 2024).
- **Older adults lose less often but more per loss:** people 60+ reported over $3 billion in 2025 (about $600
  million in 2020); their median loss was about $900, and $2,000 for people 80+. For people 60+, phone-call scams have
  the highest median loss ($3,000), and text-started losses rose 61% to $296 million (FTC report to Congress,
  October 2026). 6% of their loss reports, each over $100,000, carried 70% of their dollars.
- **The FBI's IC3 is a separate, overlapping dataset** (never add it to the FTC's): $20.9 billion reported in 2025;
  crypto investment fraud $7.2 billion; tech support $2.13 billion; government impersonation $798 million, nearly
  double; people 60+ $7.7 billion (IC3 2025 annual report).

What carriers and government do: caller-ID authentication (STIR/SHAKEN) proves which carrier signed a call, not that
the caller is honest, and the FCC itself names a gap on non-internet call paths with no final fix found; sanctions and
seizures (Treasury, DOJ) hit the offshore operations, with no measured drop in losses to Americans in the record.

## Myths the record doesn't support
- "The Do Not Call list blocks scam calls." The FTC says it does not.
- "Say 'yes' and they'll record it to charge you." No documented case; the 2017 complaints described no losses.
- "Answering 'hello' lets them clone your voice." No documented case.
- "Your bank has to give it back." Only if you didn't authorize the payment.
- "Older people are the most likely to lose money." They report losing money LESS often than younger adults, but lose
  more when they do.
- "AI voice clones are everywhere." Real and warned about, but no count exists.

## The dial (ruled 2026-11-19): whose job is it to stop it?
The viewer sets it; the evidence at each stop is laid out evenly in what it measures, not in airtime (NO FALSE BALANCE):
- **Yours.** The advice everyone gives ("just hang up") has no outcome study (C1, C2); warnings fade and often produce
  a doubtful yes (D21, D22); what predicts losing money is the moment, not intelligence (D10, D12).
- **Your family's.** They hear it first and often alone (AF1); having someone to talk to went with keeping your money
  (D11, AF25); the scammer's script orders "tell no one" (D1, AF29).
- **Your bank's.** Federal law protects reporting, not holding (E1, E5); about half the states allow a hold (E17), and
  half the banks there have never used one, for documented reasons (E22, E23, E24); trained staff stop more (E28, E29).
- **The store's.** No law found caps gift-card sales (GC1); chains choose their own limits (GC4–GC6); refusing beats
  asking (D24, GC19), yet cashiers feel they can't refuse (D25).
- Abroad, for contrast only: the UK made banks refund most scam victims (C6, C13) and runs a teller-to-police protocol
  (E32). The US puts a payment you sent yourself on you (C5, C28).

**Three things a family can set up this week** (each is advice the record supports, none is a measured outcome):
1. Ask the bank to add a trusted contact to the account (E13, AF28; a trusted contact can't see or move money).
2. Ask the bank whether your state lets it pause a suspicious payment (E11, E17, E18).
3. A "call me before you send money to anyone" agreement: having someone to talk to went with keeping the money (D11,
   AF25); no study tests the agreement itself (D27).
And the one rule: no real bank, agency or family member asks you to move money by gift card, crypto ATM, wire or cash.

## How to decide (the dial) — superseded 2026-11-19, kept as the record
Which options matter most depends on who you are, and the evidence points to the payment more than the phone:
- **An older parent:** the code word and call-back (free, no downside), plus the one rule that matters most: no
  real bank, agency or family member asks you to move money by gift card, crypto ATM, wire or cash.
- **Anyone who just paid:** the first hour: call your bank or app, then report to IC3; speed is the only measured
  recovery lever.
- **Everyone:** know which side of the line you're on: if a scammer moved the money with your code, you're
  protected; if you sent it, you're asking for goodwill.

## Script traps
- Always say REPORTED losses; never add FTC and IC3; modeled ranges stated as ranges, never "$262 billion was lost".
- The Treasury peg never mentions texts or calls; bridge with the FBI's description of how crypto investment scams start.
- The FCC rule does NOT block scam texts and does NOT make opting out easier; it widens real bank alerts and narrows opt-outs.
- Never attach $7.2B or $470M to toll or wrong-number texts specifically.
- "The FBI recommends", never "proven", for code words and call-backs.
- "70% fewer spam calls" is not "blocks 70% of scams".
- "Frozen", "seized" and "forfeiture" do not mean refunded.
- The 2025 FTC count is slightly low: no reports were taken Oct 1 to Nov 13, 2025 (the shutdown).
- Gift-card details from 2021 must be labelled "in 2021".
- UNODC's $88–114B covers East and South-East Asia, Australia and New Zealand, not losses to Americans.
- Cite sources by publisher, title and URL in any published file.
