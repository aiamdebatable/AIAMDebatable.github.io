## Script traps

_Merged from the three passes, duplicates removed._

**The numbers**
- Always say **REPORTED** losses for FTC and IC3 figures. Sentinel and IC3 are self-reported, and the FTC says they
  are a fraction of real losses.
- **FTC and IC3 are never added**, never placed in one sentence as if they were one total, and never set median
  against average. They overlap: Sentinel ingests part of IC3's data.
- The FTC's "true size" figures are **modeled ranges**. Say "the FTC estimates somewhere between $38 billion and
  $262 billion in 2025, depending on assumptions" (60+: "$13 billion to $120 billion"). Never "$262 billion was lost".
- **2025 is slightly undercounted**: the FTC took no reports Oct 1 to Nov 13, 2025 (the shutdown). The modeled ranges
  fill the gap with an average; the reported $15.9B does not.
- **Texts are the most reported contact method, not where most money goes**: text was 7% of 2025 loss reports. Social
  media led by dollars ($2.1B). For people 60+, phone-call scams have the highest median loss ($3,000), and for 80+
  the phone is the biggest dollar channel.
- **31/28/11/10/7** is a share of loss reports that name a contact method (website/app, social, phone, email, text).
  It is not a share of dollars.
- **Payment methods**: say "bank transfers and crypto carry the most money; credit cards are used most but lose the
  least". Use the 2024 all-ages Data Book dollars or the 2025 60+ figure, labelled by year and group. Do not use the
  secondary "$4B bank + crypto" figure.
- **Gift cards**: the vivid Target details are 2021 and nine months. Label them "in 2021". The current figure is
  $212M (2024, all ages) or $139M (2025, 60+).
- **Older adults**: "less likely to lose money, but they lose more when they do" (frequency vs severity). Only 46% of
  FTC fraud reports carry an age. 6% of older adults' loss reports carry 70% of their dollars, so an "average"
  misleads; use medians.
- **Crypto ATMs**: FTC figures (2023, H1 2024) and IC3 figures (2025) are different datasets and years. "Three times as
  likely" is a reporting rate. FinCEN's notice is guidance to banks, not a law. Iowa AG fees: "Bitcoin Depot takes
  23%, CoinFlip claims 21%" (the AG's wording, from a lawsuit).
- **Industry surveys** (Truecaller $25.4B, GASA $1.03T, McAfee's "1 in 4"): if used at all, say "industry survey
  estimates" and name the vendor. Never use one as a count.

**The scams and the peg**
- The Treasury peg is about investment and romance scams laundered through the Prince Group. It never mentions texts
  or calls. The bridge to the viewer's phone must be the IC3 line: crypto investment scams often start with a text.
- Do not say the FCC order "makes it easier to stop texts". It does the opposite at the margin. Also do not say it
  blocks scam texts: it governs legitimate callers that need consent. The useful line: more real bank fraud alerts
  may reach you after Nov 25, scammers already copy them, so call the number on your card.
- Never attach $7.2B (or $470M) to toll or wrong-number texts. $470M is all text-started scams in 2024; 89% of text
  reports had no loss.
- "$893M AI" is any complaint mentioning AI, not voice cloning. ">$5M distress" is the AI-tagged slice.
- "Restrained", "frozen", "seized" and "forfeiture complaint" do not mean refunded. Level Up's $562.7M is "estimated
  savings": money people did not send, not money returned.
- Caller ID authentication proves which carrier signed a call, not that the caller is honest. The non-IP gap is the
  FCC's own word, and a final fix was not confirmed.
- "Can you hear me" and "hello" cloning: say "there is no documented case", not "hoax".
- The SIM-farm bust was framed as a threat to officials, not consumer scams. SMS blasters: no US case in the record.
- UNODC's $88–114B covers East Asia, SE Asia, Australia and NZ; it is not losses to Americans. Don't set it beside the
  FTC's $15.9B.

**The options**
- Say "the FBI recommends", never "proven", for code words and call-backs. Say what IS measured: short scam lessons
  and warning calls reduce people's willingness to fall for a pitch in experiments, and the effect fades within weeks
  or months unless repeated. That supports a "refresh it with your family" line, honestly sourced.
- Don't sell "awareness" in general: a bank's mass warning message showed no effect (G3). The things with measured
  effects are specific, timely and interrupting: a warning call before the pitch, or a cancel button at the moment of
  payment.
- "Your bank has to refund you" is true only if you didn't make the payment. Teach the line: if a scammer got your
  login or one-time code and moved the money, that's unauthorized and you're covered under Reg E. If you sent it
  yourself, you have no legal right; you're asking for goodwill. Report fast either way: the 2-business-day / 60-day
  clocks are real.
- "70% fewer spam calls" is not "blocks 70% of scams". Don't present call filtering as protection against texts in
  iMessage/RCS/WhatsApp or social media.
- The Do Not Call Registry does nothing against scammers. Say so plainly; viewers believe it does.
- Credit freeze: free, and the right move for identity theft. It would not have stopped a single payment a scam
  victim sent. Don't list it as a phone-scam protection without that line.
- RAT's 58% is of attempted theft in 3,900 hand-picked, fast-reported incidents, not of all losses (IC3's 2025 total
  is about $20.9B). Say "the FBI sometimes freezes money still sitting in a bank, if you report within hours". The
  rate has FALLEN from 71% to 58%.
- UK reimbursement is a policy comparison, not something a US viewer can use. Frame it as "here's what happened when
  one country made banks pay". Credit the 21% to an evaluation's judgement, not an experiment, and mention that
  out-of-scope losses rose.
- FedNow and wires are final by design: "the bank can't just take it back" is accurate for wires once accepted,
  unless the receiving bank agrees.
- USPIS can intercept a mailed cash package only if it hasn't been delivered, it must be a domestic shipment, and it
  costs a fee. Read the number from the FTC page on air: 1-877-876-2455.

**The top-ups, 2026-11-09 (Parts D–G)**
- **Name no victim.** D28/D29, D30 and D31 are named in public records or news; use roles only (the Utah grandmother,
  a Maine woman, a retired nurse educator). The Utah cashier story (GC25) names neither the senior nor the cashier.
- The Senate Utah case (D28/D29) was a **wire at a credit union**, not gift cards. It may open the episode only as
  itself; never borrow its words or details for a gift-card telling.
- How often one victim goes bank → store was **not measured** anywhere in the record. The FTC names the bank, the
  gift-card store and the Bitcoin ATM as places scammers send older adults (D1); it gives no rate for the chain.
- Scam awareness and dementia: "a possible early sign", never "a test" or "twice the risk" (D16). Emotion raised
  intention in older AND younger adults; never "only older people" (D18).
- D23/AF23's 51% is self-reported with no comparison group: "about half of people who had someone step in kept their
  money", never "stepping in saves half". No controlled study tests a relative, teller or cashier interrupting a live
  call (D27): present it as the best-supported idea, not a proven one.
- D26 ("about 1 in 4 warned by a store employee") is APPROXIMATE: AARP's article about its own survey. Off air.
- **The Senior Safe Act covers REPORTING, never holding** (E1, E5). Holds come from STATE laws (E11): "about half the
  states" (E17 is APPROXIMATE: say "about half", never "24"). State lists are "as of October 2024" (E18); re-check any

- FinCEN's $27 billion includes attempted transactions; it is not losses (E8). Use the median, $33,499, not the

- BankSafe: "16 times more" carries one $500,000 case; without it about 7 times. AARP runs and evaluated the program

- **No US law caps gift-card sales** (GC1), but say "no law we found", since other states' bills were not all read.

- Signs are common and limits are not (GC7), but there is **no measured effect** of signs or cashier prompts on losses

## Fairness audit
- **Options explainer, no teams.** The options are weighted by their evidence, not given equal time: legal dispute
  rights on unauthorized transfers and the UK's mandatory refunds grade Strong; lessons, bank safeguards, the first-hour
  recall and the credit freeze grade Some; call filtering and the Do Not Call Registry grade Weak; code words,
  call-backs and not answering are Advice only, because nothing measures them. That lean follows the record; it is not
  trimmed or padded to look even.
- **No side taken on policy.** The Treasury action, the FCC order, the dropped CFPB suit and the UK rule are described
  with what each does and what is measured, never endorsed or opposed; the UK rule is framed as one country's result,
  not a recommendation.
- **Stakeholder figures are labelled at the source:** vendor surveys and claims (Truecaller, GASA/Feedzai, McAfee,
  Hiya, Google, the Google-commissioned Leviathan test), advocacy bodies (Amnesty, ITRC), and allegations or
  plaintiffs' estimates (the Iowa and D.C. attorneys general, the CFPB's $870M, Google's Lighthouse range).

## The peg, as it may be stated (hand pass B)

On November 5, 2026, the U.S. Treasury sanctioned four people and 41 companies that support the Prince Group scam
network, plus a Malaysian man whose Madagascar-based ring took more than $400,000 from Americans. Treasury said
these syndicates "continue to steal billions of dollars from American victims" through investment, romance and
other online fraud (Treasury release sb0683; rows B1–B6).

And on November 25, 2026, a new FCC rule takes effect that lets banks send fraud-alert calls and texts to more of
their customers' cell numbers. The same rule lets companies limit an opt-out to one kind of message and name the one
way you must use to opt out (FCC 26-67, Federal Register 2026-21821; rows B9–B13).
