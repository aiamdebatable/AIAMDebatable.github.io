Research brief — Who Pays for the Grid the AI Boom Needs?

The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video.

✅ EPISODE-GRADE. Peg hunt 2026-09-01; deep-research fan-out (108 agents, 26 sources, 130 claims); the two gaps that pass declared about itself closed by hand 2026-09-02 from enrolled bills and fiscal records. Every claim traces to factcheck-log.md.

⛔ The prior scouting sweep — an internal document, not part of this published bundle — was treated as a checklist of claims to verify independently, never source material — see the contamination rule in the new-episode skill. Its framing was also reworked at promotion: it proposed the axis, Lucas ruled it in.

The question

Should states pause new data centers until the rules on who pays for the grid exist — and what share of grid-upgrade and transmission costs should a large data-center customer bear versus ordinary ratepayers?

⚠ This is a cost-allocation question, not a carbon one. ep03-data-centers already asked whether the buildout is good for the climate and covers grid strain, water, the fossil bridge, Jevons and additionality. Taking this topic at face value would re-make that episode. The discipline here is utility rate design and regulatory economics.

Why now

Who pays stopped being a question and became an order — three times, in five weeks, in three states that each answered it differently.

dateevent
2026-06-04Both houses of the New York legislature pass the Responsible Data Center Development Act (S10642/A11560) — a one-year permitting moratorium on data centers with peak demand ≥ 20 MW.
2026-07-14Hochul does not sign it. She issues Executive Order 62 instead — a parallel moratorium on new hyperscale data centers ≥ 50 MW, for up to a year, while the state writes higher standards. Her stated reason is refusing to let utility costs be passed to New Yorkers.
2026-07-31⭐ The Virginia State Corporation Commission — regulator of the largest data-center market on earth — issues its final order in the Rider T-1 case (PUR-2026-00056, filed 1 May 2026). It rules in favour of a mandatory contribution in aid of construction (CIAC) for "direct connect" facilities and the substations and transmission lines serving them, directing Dominion Energy to "directly assign the cost of certain transmission infrastructure to the new large-load facilities which made that infrastructure necessary" and to file the amended policy in a new docket within 90 days — landing about 29 October 2026. The proceeding sought roughly $1.5 billion in transmission cost recovery.
2026-08-21⭐ The Kentucky Public Service Commission approves the retail electric service agreement in Case No. 2026-00115 between Big Rivers Electric, Kenergy and Justified DataPower LLC (a TeraWulf subsidiary) for a 482 MW data-center campus at the former Century Aluminum Hawesville smelter in Hancock County — with a 482 MW minimum take-or-pay, credit support equal to twice the estimated highest monthly bill, annual capacity cost prepaid or fully collateralised, and an express finding that the arrangement does not shift the project's costs or risks to other customers.
2026-08-24Sixty members of the New York Assembly and Senate sign a letter urging Hochul to sign the statutory bill, which reaches further than her order by covering facilities from 20 MW up.
2026-09-01The revised Rider T-1 takes effect. The bill still sits unsigned; Hochul has until 31 December 2026 before a pocket veto ends it.

⭐ Why this is the peg and not the New York moratorium alone

The scouting pass pegged this to New York. New York asks "should we pause?" — Virginia and Kentucky answer "who pays?", which is this episode's actual question. And the peg is three regulators reaching three different answers rather than one ruling, so no single signature can overtake it mid-production.

★ Kentucky answers the episode's own hero question on the record. The scouting brief proposed the number to put on screen as "what share of a $X grid upgrade should a 500 MW customer pay?" — and a commission has now priced it for a 482 MW customer, in an entered order, in public.

⛔ Found by the blind sweep, not by searching the brief's own words. "Virginia", "SCC", "Rider T-1", "contribution in aid of construction", "TeraWulf", "Big Rivers" and "482 MW" appear nowhere in the scouting brief. They surfaced from naming counterparties it never mentions — state commissions other than New York's. Rejected candidates, including a six-day-old presidential national-emergency declaration on the bulk-power system, are recorded in factcheck-log.md.

✅ Both figures that were barred are now RESOLVED against the order itself. The case is PUR-2026-00056, read from the Final Order; and $2.90 was Dominion's projection as filed while $0.94 is the post-order figure — so the SCC reduced it. ⚠ Say "reduced", never "overrode": the amended allocation came from Dominion's own rebuttal with Staff and Consumer Counsel support.

Settled (not the debate)

  1. ⭐ The instrument has already converged, and nobody voted on it. Virginia, Tennessee, Kentucky, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin all reached for the same two ideas: but-for causation and a contribution in aid of construction. Virginia's SCC ordered it; Tennessee wrote the "would not be incurred but for" test into statute; Kentucky priced it into a contract.
  2. ⭐⭐ And four of the five stopped at the same place. Sole-use facilities — the substation and the line to the door — are settled: the large customer pays. Shared network upgrades are unresolved in four of the five. Virginia expressly deferred higher-order transmission; Wisconsin ordered further revisions; Tennessee wrote an explicit exception for it. ⚠ CORRECTED 2026-09-03 — Pennsylvania DECIDED it. The Final Order in Docket M-2025-3054271 (entered 12 May 2026, <https://www.puc.pa.gov/pcdocs/1929842.pdf>, Disposition pp. 41–42, read in full) adopts the OCA's but-for test: "if a Network Improvement would not have been needed 'but for' the interconnection of the Large Load Customer, then the costs of the upgrade should be allocated to that customer irrespective of whether other customers would benefit from it", with CIAC covering "all distribution and transmission costs necessary to interconnect" and the "only exception" being upgrades already in a Commission-approved LTIIP. The Energy Association of Pennsylvania petitioned for reconsideration or clarification on 27 May 2026 (<https://www.puc.pa.gov/pcdocs/1932400.pdf>). This brief previously said Pennsylvania called the test "uncertain, difficult to quantify" — that phrase is on p. 28 of the same order and concerns the majority-beneficiary criterion for financial security (collateral), which the Commission set aside for rate proceedings. It is not about cost allocation. The mis-attribution came from the deep-research pass; the Final Order had not been opened (see factcheck-log.md).
  3. A tariff changes who pays, not what it costs. Rider T-1's revenue requirement — $1,538,974,037 — was uncontested and statutorily "deemed reasonable and prudent" under Va. Code § 56-585.1 A 4. The SCC could only re-allocate it between classes; the typical residential increase went from $2.90 to $0.94 a month. Not one dollar of the total moved.
  4. What a large-load contract actually contains, from Kentucky PSC 2026-00115: a 482 MW minimum take-or-pay, credit support at twice the highest estimated monthly bill, annual capacity cost prepaid or fully collateralised, and an express Commission finding of no cost or risk shift to other customers. Oklahoma's statute adds a ten-year minimum term and explicit stranded-cost recovery if the customer departs or materially reduces load.
  5. Data centers have already moved wholesale prices. Dallas Fed WP 2606: 2–6% on average nationwide, more in the corridors. ⚠ A dispatch model, not an observation.
  6. Siting responds to what states GIVE, not to what they CHARGE. State incentives measurably shift siting "without commensurate increases in local tech employment"; the count of data centers shows essentially no correlation with electricity prices.

Contested (the real fight)

GREEN — "Pause until the rules exist." A load this large arriving before the rule exists is a transfer, because a regulated monopoly's costs socialise by default. And the pause side is right about the part nobody has fixed: every state furthest along has deferred the shared-network question in its own words. New York's governor conceded the premise — she paused because she would not let utility costs pass to New Yorkers who already pay too much. A year is cheap next to a decade of contracts written under no rule at all.

GOLD — "The pause is the expensive option." Four bodies reached the cost-causation answer without banning anything — Virginia by order, Oklahoma and Tennessee by statute, Kentucky by contract. If the worry is cost-shifting, the tariff is the instrument, and it already exists and binds. A moratorium forfeits construction wages and the property-tax base while leaving the same rate-design problem unsolved. ⚠ But its sharpest claim is the one with no evidence: no documented case was found of a project leaving a state over a moratorium or a tariff.

The reframe

Everyone is arguing about whether to pause, and the pause and the tariff answer the same easy question. Both sides already agree the data center pays for the wires to its own door. The fight that matters is the one every state has deferred: who pays for the shared upgrade that the data center triggers but does not solely use — and the honest answer today is nobody has decided.

★ And the frame most viewers carry is wrong in a checkable way. The most-cited number in this debate, $329.17/MW-day, is an administrative price cap the auction bound against — not a market price that measures what data centers did.

Both sides quietly agree

That the large customer should pay for what it alone causes. Not one of the seven jurisdictions here disputes it — the pause states and the no-pause states wrote the same test. ★ The disagreement is not about principle at all; it is about a question none of them answered, and about whether you can keep connecting 500-megawatt customers while it stays unanswered.

Verdict framing

No winner. What the viewer is handed:

  • The easy half is settled and uncontroversial — sole-use facilities go to the customer.
  • The contested half is undecided in every state that has looked at it, including both exemplars the anti-pause case rests on.
  • A pause and a tariff change who pays, not what it costs: Virginia's $1.54 billion was never in play.
  • And nothing here has run long enough to know if it works — no large-load tariff has yet been tested by a customer that wanted out, so nobody knows whether the collateral holds, the exit fee is collectible, or the minimum take is enforceable.

⚠ The honest close: both sides are arguing about an instrument that has never been tested, over a question neither has answered.
