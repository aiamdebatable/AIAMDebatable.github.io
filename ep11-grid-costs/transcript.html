Transcript — "Who pays for the grid the AI boom needs?"

Verbatim spoken track, in broadcast order. Speakers: Lucas (host), Erin (research lead), Walt (the doubts), Lena (Team Green · SEND THEM THE BILL), Cole (Team Gold · NO RULE, NO HOOKUP), Raj (the judge), and the Narrator who reads the cold open.

Cold open

NarratorAsk the internet who should pay for the power lines the AI boom needs, and you'll get a moratorium on one side and a jobs number on the other.

The research · Three regulators, three answers, five weeks

ErinFor two years the fight over data centers has been about whether to build them. This summer it changed into a fight about who pays for the wires. Three regulators answered inside five weeks.

WaltThree answers to the same question?

ErinThree different ones. Start with Virginia, because it's the largest data-center market on earth. In late July its utility regulator ruled on a transmission charge worth about a billion and a half dollars.

WaltRuled how?

ErinIt ordered the utility to directly assign the cost of new transmission to the large customers who made it necessary. A mandatory contribution in aid of construction, for the substations and lines that serve them. Ninety days to file the new policy.

WaltSo Virginia sent the data centers the bill.

ErinFor the wires to their door, yes. Three weeks later Kentucky did something different. Its commission approved a contract with one customer: a data-center campus of nearly five hundred megawatts, at a shut-down aluminium smelter.

WaltA contract. Not a rule.

ErinA contract with teeth. The customer commits to buy the whole load whether it uses it or not. It posts collateral worth twice its highest expected monthly bill. And the commission wrote down, in the order, that none of the cost or risk shifts to other customers.

WaltAnd New York?

ErinNew York paused. The legislature passed a one-year moratorium on new data centers above twenty megawatts. The governor didn't sign it. She issued her own order instead, at fifty megawatts, while the state writes standards. In late August dozens of legislators wrote to her asking her to sign the bill anyway.

WaltSo one state assigns the cost, one prices it into a contract, and one stops and thinks.

ErinAnd the bill in New York sits unsigned until the end of December. Virginia's utility files its new policy at the end of October. So nothing here is finished.

WaltThree regulators, three different answers, and none of them done. That's not a settled debate, that's a live one.

The research · A tariff changes who pays, not what it costs

ErinIt is. But part of it is settled, and the settled part is the arithmetic. Start with Virginia's number.

ErinThat transmission charge was a little over one and a half billion dollars. Nobody in the case contested it. Under Virginia law, that kind of cost is deemed reasonable and prudent by statute.

WaltMeaning what, in practice?

ErinMeaning the regulator couldn't cut it. It could only decide who pays it. Before the order, a typical household was going to pay about two dollars ninety a month more. After the order, under a dollar. Not one dollar of the total moved.

WaltSo the bill didn't shrink. It got passed around.

ErinRe-allocated. That's the whole lesson, and it's why I'd tattoo it on this episode: a tariff changes who pays. It doesn't change what the wires cost. Neither does a pause.

WaltAlright. What does one of these large-customer deals actually contain? Not the headline, the terms.

ErinKentucky's, read from the order: a minimum purchase of the full load, take it or pay for it. Collateral at twice the highest estimated monthly bill. The year's capacity cost paid up front or fully secured.

WaltAnd elsewhere?

ErinOklahoma wrote it into statute this spring, for anything over seventy-five megawatts. A minimum ten-year term. Separate tariffs. And the tariff has to cover what's left stranded if the customer leaves or shrinks.

WaltSo the protections people are asking for exist. In writing. In more than one state.

ErinFor the part everyone agrees on, yes. The substation, the line to the door, the risk that the customer walks. That part is written down. Hold on to that word: part.

The research · Five states, one tool — and the same missing half

WaltYou keep saying part. What's the other part?

ErinThe finding that reshaped this whole file. We looked at five jurisdictions that have actually written rules. Virginia, Tennessee, Kentucky, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin. They all reached for the same tool.

WaltWhich is?

ErinA but-for test. If the cost wouldn't be incurred but for this customer, this customer pays it. Virginia ordered it. Tennessee wrote those exact words into law. Kentucky priced it into the contract.

WaltFive states, one answer. That sounds like the argument's over.

ErinIt sounds that way. Then you read where each of them stopped. Every single one settled the sole-use facilities, the substation and the line to the door. And four of the five left the shared network upgrade undecided.

WaltShared network upgrade meaning —

ErinThe line three counties away that has to be rebuilt because a five-hundred-megawatt customer arrived. It serves everyone. It was triggered by one customer. Who pays for that?

WaltAnd nobody's said?

ErinPennsylvania is the one that actually answered. In May its commission ruled that if a shared upgrade wouldn't be needed but for the data center, the data center pays for it, whether or not anyone else benefits. Two weeks later the utilities asked it to reconsider. Virginia expressly deferred it. Wisconsin sent it back for more work. And Tennessee, the state everyone cites for making data centers pay the full cost, wrote an explicit exception for it.

WaltSo the state that supposedly solved this carved out the hard case.

ErinCarved it out in its own words. The utility may share those costs with everyone if the upgrade goes beyond what the data center needs. That's the exact question Virginia and Wisconsin deferred. Tennessee didn't answer it. It wrote around it.

WaltSo both sides of this fight are standing on the same settled half, and neither's touched the other.

The research · The numbers people shout, and what they measure

ErinLast of the settled ground, and it's about the numbers people actually shout.

WaltWhich one first?

ErinThe one you've probably heard: a capacity price of about three hundred and thirty dollars a megawatt-day, blamed on data centers. That number is real. It's also an administrative price cap. The auction hit the ceiling.

WaltSo it measures the ceiling, not the demand.

ErinExactly. A capped price can't tell you what data centers did. What can is a Dallas Fed working paper that models the grid hour by hour with real data-center sites. It finds existing data centers have already raised wholesale prices two to six percent on average, and more in the corridors.

WaltAnd that's an observation?

ErinNo, and I'll say it plainly: it's a dispatch model, not a meter reading. But it's the strongest basis anyone has, and it's the number I'd trust over the price cap.

WaltSecond question. The threat is always the same: charge them and they'll build in the next state. Does that happen?

ErinWe went looking for a documented case of a data-center project cancelled and relocated because of a moratorium or a cost-allocation tariff. We didn't find one. Absence isn't proof it never happens, so the honest count is: zero found.

WaltBut they do move for something.

ErinTax incentives. The economics literature the Fed paper cites finds state incentives measurably shift where data centers are sited, without a matching rise in local tech jobs. So mobility is real for what states give. It's unproven for what states charge.

WaltWhich is exactly the thing this episode is about. Charging.

Quick check

WaltBefore we hand this over to the debaters, one quick gut-check for you at home.

ErinVirginia's regulator ruled on a billion-and-a-half-dollar transmission charge. What did the ruling change?

ErinA — it cut the total that customers pay. Or B — it changed who pays it, because the total was never in dispute.

ErinWhat's genuinely up for debate starts now.

The reframe

LucasI went in expecting to referee pause versus build. That's not the argument I found.

LucasEveryone in this fight already agrees on the easy half. The data center pays for the substation and the line to its own door. Five states wrote that down and not one of them disputes it.

LucasThe part they're actually fighting about is the part four of those five states deferred. Who pays for the shared upgrade a data center triggers but doesn't own. One state has answered it on paper, and its utilities are already asking for a rethink. Everywhere else, nobody has decided.

LucasThe thing that stopped me was Virginia's arithmetic. A billion and a half dollars, uncontested, deemed prudent by law. The regulator couldn't shrink it. It could only move it. A pause or a tariff changes who pays, not what the wires cost.

LucasAnd nothing here has run long enough to know if it works. No large-customer contract has yet been tested by a customer that wanted out. So both sides are arguing about an instrument nobody has stress-tested.

LucasGreen argues send them the bill. Gold argues no rule, no hookup. Green goes first.

The debate · Team Green · SEND THEM THE BILL

LenaI want to argue from what's already written down, because my opponent's whole case is about a gap, and I want to show you how much is on the other side of it.

LenaFour bodies reached the cost-causation answer without banning anything. Virginia by order. Oklahoma and Tennessee by statute. Kentucky by contract. If the fear is that households subsidise a data center, the tool that stops it already exists and already binds.

LenaLook at what Kentucky actually signed. Nearly five hundred megawatts, take or pay. Collateral at twice the highest monthly bill. A year's capacity cost secured up front. And the regulator's own finding that no cost or risk shifts to anyone else.

LenaOklahoma went further. A ten-year minimum term. Stranded-cost recovery if the customer leaves. That's not a promise, that's a statute.

LenaNow the pause. New York's order stops permits at fifty megawatts while the state writes standards. It says the cost of upgrades for large loads shouldn't fall on everyday New Yorkers, and it sends the agencies off to explore a fund. It states the principle. It doesn't write the rule.

LenaSo a year passes. Construction wages don't get paid. The property tax base doesn't grow. And the rule for the shared upgrade still has to be written by the same agencies, the same way, with the customers waiting instead of connected.

LenaAnd I'll say the weakest thing on my side myself. The threat that a tariff drives projects to the next state? We found no documented case of it. Not one. I won't use it.

LenaI don't need it. My case isn't that they'll leave. It's that the instrument that protects households is already in force in four states, and a moratorium adds nothing to it.

LenaMy opponent will say the shared upgrade is the whole fight and nobody's priced it. He's right that nobody has.

LenaBut a pause doesn't price it either. It just moves the arrival date of the same unanswered question.

LenaWrite the tariff, connect the customer, and make it pay for what it causes. That's the answer that exists. The other one is a calendar.

The debate · Team Gold responds

ColeI'll take her strongest point first, because it's genuinely strong. The sole-use rule exists, it binds, and it's in four states. I'm not disputing a word of it.

ColeBut notice what she read out. The substation. The line to the door. The collateral if the customer walks. Every one of those is the easy half.

ColeNow read where each of those same bodies stopped. Virginia: shared transmission expressly deferred. Wisconsin: sent back. Tennessee: an explicit exception. Pennsylvania answered it, and its own utilities filed to reconsider within two weeks.

ColeFour states with a rule, and four states that wrote their way around the same question. That's not a tariff that already works. That's a tariff with a hole in it the size of the argument.

The debate · Team Gold · NO RULE, NO HOOKUP

ColeLet me grant what's true. A pause doesn't write the rule. My opponent is right about that, and I'll build on it rather than dodge it.

ColeA regulated utility's costs socialise by default. That is how the system works: a shared line gets built, and everyone pays for it through their rates unless a rule says otherwise.

ColeSo when a five-hundred-megawatt customer arrives before the rule exists, a transfer happens. Nobody has to cheat. The default does it for them.

ColeAnd the transfer is already measurable. The Dallas Fed model puts existing data centers at two to six percent on wholesale prices nationwide, and more in the corridors. I'll say it the way the desk did: a model, not a meter. But it's the best estimate there is, and it's not zero.

ColeSecond: look at the terms she's proud of. Oklahoma's minimum contract is ten years. So every deal signed this year under no shared-upgrade rule is a decade of contracts written before anyone decided who pays for the shared upgrade.

ColeYou can't retrofit a rule onto a contract that's already signed. That's the case for the pause. Not that data centers are bad. That the order matters.

ColeThird, and this is where I'd sit. Nothing in this record has run long enough to test. No customer has tried to walk out of one of these contracts. Nobody knows whether the collateral holds or the exit fee is collectible.

ColeAnd I'll take her count, including the part that hurts me. Zero documented cases of a project leaving over a tariff. Fine. Then the cost of pausing to write the rule is lower than her side keeps saying, because they don't leave.

ColeThe thing everyone missed about New York is what the order actually says. It's the policy of the state that the cost of upgrades for large loads should not be paid for by everyday New Yorkers. That's the cost-allocation argument, written into the pause itself.

ColeI'm not asking for a ban. I'm asking that you write the rule for the hard half before you sign ten-year contracts that assume it.

ColeFour states have proved the easy half can be written in a year. Fine. Take the year, and write the other half.

The debate · Team Green responds

LenaThe ten-year point is his best card. A decade of contracts under an unwritten rule is a real cost, and I won't pretend it isn't.

LenaBut look at what a pause does to it. New York's order states the principle, that everyday New Yorkers shouldn't pay for large-load upgrades, and sends the agencies to explore a fund. Good. That's a rate case with the permits switched off. The rule gets written either way. The only variable is whether the customers are connected while it happens.

LenaThe rule gets written in a rate case. Virginia went from filing to order in three months. A regulator can write the hard half while customers connect under the easy half, because the easy half already protects the household from the thing everyone's actually afraid of.

LenaHe's right that the default socialises costs. That's why the sole-use rule exists. It's the fix for the default.

LenaBut I'll concede his strongest line. If a shared line is triggered by one customer and everyone pays for it, that is a transfer, and no contract at the door fixes it. On the shared upgrade, he has the better argument. It's just not an argument for stopping.

The verdict

RajThis one doesn't resolve, and I want to be precise about where it stops rather than call it a draw.

RajBoth teams accepted the same settled ground. The customer pays for the substation and the line to its own door. Five states wrote that down. Neither debater disputed it, and neither disputed that four of those five deferred the shared upgrade, with the fifth's answer already under challenge.

RajSo the disagreement isn't about what the rule says. It's about what you do while half of it is unwritten. Keep connecting under the half that exists, or stop until the other half does.

RajBoth also conceded their weak flank. Green conceded that a shared line triggered by one customer and paid for by everyone is a transfer that no contract at the door fixes. Gold conceded that a pause doesn't write the rule either.

RajThe case for connecting rests on what's already in force: an order, a statute, a contract, and a regulator that wrote the easy half in three months.

RajThe case for pausing rests on ten-year contracts being signed before anyone decides who pays for the wire three counties away, and on a default that socialises the cost unless somebody stops it.

RajAnd both cases are downstream of one piece of arithmetic. A billion and a half dollars in Virginia that nobody contested, that the regulator could only move, not shrink. A pause and a tariff both change who pays. Neither changes what the wires cost.

RajSo the question we're handing you isn't whether data centers should pay. Everyone here says yes. It's whether you'd sign a ten-year contract under a rule that's half written, or make the customer wait while somebody finishes it. That one's yours.
