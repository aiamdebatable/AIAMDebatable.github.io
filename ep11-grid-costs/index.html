Data Center Electricity: Who Pays for the Grid the AI Boom Needs?

Should states pause new data centers until the rules on who pays for the grid exist?

Last changed 2026-11-08 · See every change

Not whether data centers are good — what share of the wires a 500 MW customer should pay for

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in September 2026, three regulators had given three answers in five weeks. Virginia's regulator sent the cost of new transmission to the large customers who caused it, Kentucky approved a contract with a data-center campus of nearly 500 megawatts, and New York paused new data centers above 50 megawatts while it wrote standards.

Settled

What both sides accept

The data center pays for the substation and the line to its own door, and five states wrote that down. Four of those five left the shared upgrade undecided, and the fifth's answer was already being challenged. Green conceded that a shared line paid for by everyone is a transfer no contract fixes, and Gold conceded that a pause doesn't write the rule.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesSend Them The Bill

The protection households need already exists and binds. Virginia by order, Oklahoma and Tennessee by law, and Kentucky by contract make the data center pay for what it causes, with take-or-pay terms and collateral. A pause doesn't write the rule for shared upgrades either. Write the tariff, connect the customer, and make it pay.

Gold arguesNo Rule, No Hookup

Those rules cover the easy half: the substation and the line to the door. Who pays for a shared upgrade one customer triggers was left undecided in four of five states, and by default everyone pays. Contracts run ten years, and you can't retrofit a rule onto a signed one. So take the year and write the hard half first.

What the shouting gets wrong

Everyone in this fight agreed data centers should pay. A pause and a tariff both change who pays, and neither changes what the wires cost: Virginia's regulator could move its billion and a half dollars but couldn't shrink it. The open question is who pays for the shared line one customer triggers, and whether to keep connecting while that rule is half written.

The verdict

The two cases, as the judge put them
The case for connecting rests on what's already in force: an order, a statute, a contract, and a regulator that wrote the easy half in three months.
The case for pausing rests on ten-year contracts being signed before anyone decides who pays for the wire three counties away, and on a default that socialises the cost unless somebody stops it.

Your dial: Would you sign a ten-year contract under a rule that's half written, or make the customer wait while somebody finishes it?

The judge named these positions and declared no winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Keep connecting under the half that exists
    What it buys
    Customers connect now, under what's already in force: an order, a statute, a contract. The customer pays for the substation and the line to its own door, and a regulator wrote that easy half in three months.
    What it costs
    A shared line triggered by one customer and paid for by everyone is a transfer that no contract at the door fixes. Ten-year contracts get signed before anyone decides who pays for the wire three counties away.
    Not measured
    Whether these contracts hold: no customer has tried to walk out of one yet, so nobody knows whether the collateral holds or the exit fee is collectible.

What our fact-check caught

Our drafts got these wrong before they aired. Every correction is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidA capacity price of about $330 a megawatt-day showed data centers driving prices up.

    The record saysThat number is a price cap the auction hit. It measures the cap, not what data centers did.

  2. Draft saidOklahoma makes data centers of 75 megawatts or more pay the full cost of service.

    The record saysIts law says they pay their fair share of the costs they cause, which is not the same as the full cost.

  3. Draft saidNew York's pause order wrote no rule on shared upgrades and just promised standards.

    The record saysThe order states that everyday New Yorkers shouldn't pay for large-load upgrades and sends agencies to explore a fund. It doesn't itself decide who pays.
