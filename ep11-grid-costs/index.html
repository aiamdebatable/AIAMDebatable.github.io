Data Center Electricity: Who Pays for the Grid the AI Boom Needs?

The question: Should states pause new data centers until the rules on who pays for the grid exist?

▶ Watch on YouTubeThe full record ↓

Not whether data centers are good — what share of the wires a 500 MW customer should pay for

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in September 2026, three regulators had given three answers in five weeks. Virginia's regulator sent the cost of new transmission to the large customers who caused it, Kentucky approved a contract with a data-center campus of nearly 500 megawatts, and New York paused new data centers above 50 megawatts while it wrote standards.

The fight

Green arguesSend Them The Bill

The protection households need already exists and binds. Virginia by order, Oklahoma and Tennessee by law, and Kentucky by contract make the data center pay for what it causes, with take-or-pay terms and collateral. A pause doesn't write the rule for shared upgrades either. Write the tariff, connect the customer, and make it pay.

Gold arguesNo Rule, No Hookup

Those rules cover the easy half: the substation and the line to the door. Who pays for a shared upgrade one customer triggers was left undecided in four of five states, and by default everyone pays. Contracts run ten years, and you can't retrofit a rule onto a signed one. So take the year and write the hard half first.

What both sides quietly agree on

The data center pays for the substation and the line to its own door, and five states wrote that down. Four of those five left the shared upgrade undecided, and the fifth's answer was already being challenged. Green conceded that a shared line paid for by everyone is a transfer no contract fixes, and Gold conceded that a pause doesn't write the rule.

What the shouting gets wrong

Everyone in this fight agreed data centers should pay. A pause and a tariff both change who pays, and neither changes what the wires cost: Virginia's regulator could move its billion and a half dollars but couldn't shrink it. The open question is who pays for the shared line one customer triggers, and whether to keep connecting while that rule is half written.

The verdict

Watched it already? Read the verdict as it aired

Raj This one doesn't resolve, and I want to be precise about where it stops rather than call it a draw.

Raj Both teams accepted the same settled ground. The customer pays for the substation and the line to its own door. Five states wrote that down. Neither debater disputed it, and neither disputed that four of those five deferred the shared upgrade, with the fifth's answer already under challenge.

Raj So the disagreement isn't about what the rule says. It's about what you do while half of it is unwritten. Keep connecting under the half that exists, or stop until the other half does.

Raj Both also conceded their weak flank. Green conceded that a shared line triggered by one customer and paid for by everyone is a transfer that no contract at the door fixes. Gold conceded that a pause doesn't write the rule either.

Raj The case for connecting rests on what's already in force: an order, a statute, a contract, and a regulator that wrote the easy half in three months.

Raj The case for pausing rests on ten-year contracts being signed before anyone decides who pays for the wire three counties away, and on a default that socialises the cost unless somebody stops it.

Raj And both cases are downstream of one piece of arithmetic. A billion and a half dollars in Virginia that nobody contested, that the regulator could only move, not shrink. A pause and a tariff both change who pays. Neither changes what the wires cost.

Raj So the question we're handing you isn't whether data centers should pay. Everyone here says yes. It's whether you'd sign a ten-year contract under a rule that's half written, or make the customer wait while somebody finishes it. That one's yours.
