## The research · Three regulators, three answers, five weeks
**Erin:** For two years the fight over data centers has been about whether to build them. This summer it changed into a fight about who pays for the wires. Three regulators answered inside five weeks.
**Walt:** Three answers to the same question?
**Erin:** Three different ones. Start with Virginia, because it's the largest data-center market on earth. In late July its utility regulator ruled on a transmission charge worth about a billion and a half dollars.
**Walt:** Ruled how?
**Erin:** It ordered the utility to directly assign the cost of new transmission to the large customers who made it necessary. A mandatory contribution in aid of construction, for the substations and lines that serve them. Ninety days to file the new policy.
**Walt:** So Virginia sent the data centers the bill.
**Erin:** For the wires to their door, yes. Three weeks later Kentucky did something different. Its commission approved a contract with one customer: a data-center campus of nearly five hundred megawatts, at a shut-down aluminium smelter.
**Walt:** A contract. Not a rule.
**Erin:** A contract with teeth. The customer commits to buy the whole load whether it uses it or not. It posts collateral worth twice its highest expected monthly bill. And the commission wrote down, in the order, that none of the cost or risk shifts to other customers.
**Walt:** And New York?
**Erin:** New York paused. The legislature passed a one-year moratorium on new data centers above twenty megawatts. The governor didn't sign it. She issued her own order instead, at fifty megawatts, while the state writes standards. In late August dozens of legislators wrote to her asking her to sign the bill anyway.
**Walt:** So one state assigns the cost, one prices it into a contract, and one stops and thinks.
**Erin:** And the bill in New York sits unsigned until the end of December. Virginia's utility files its new policy at the end of October. So nothing here is finished.
**Walt:** Three regulators, three different answers, and none of them done. That's not a settled debate, that's a live one.

## The research · A tariff changes who pays, not what it costs
**Erin:** It is. But part of it is settled, and the settled part is the arithmetic. Start with Virginia's number.
**Erin:** That transmission charge was a little over one and a half billion dollars. Nobody in the case contested it. Under Virginia law, that kind of cost is deemed reasonable and prudent by statute.
**Walt:** Meaning what, in practice?
**Erin:** Meaning the regulator couldn't cut it. It could only decide who pays it. Before the order, a typical household was going to pay about two dollars ninety a month more. After the order, under a dollar. Not one dollar of the total moved.
**Walt:** So the bill didn't shrink. It got passed around.
**Erin:** Re-allocated. That's the whole lesson, and it's why I'd tattoo it on this episode: a tariff changes who pays. It doesn't change what the wires cost. Neither does a pause.
**Walt:** Alright. What does one of these large-customer deals actually contain? Not the headline, the terms.
**Erin:** Kentucky's, read from the order: a minimum purchase of the full load, take it or pay for it. Collateral at twice the highest estimated monthly bill. The year's capacity cost paid up front or fully secured.
**Walt:** And elsewhere?
**Erin:** Oklahoma wrote it into statute this spring, for anything over seventy-five megawatts. A minimum ten-year term. Separate tariffs. And the tariff has to cover what's left stranded if the customer leaves or shrinks.
**Walt:** So the protections people are asking for exist. In writing. In more than one state.
**Erin:** For the part everyone agrees on, yes. The substation, the line to the door, the risk that the customer walks. That part is written down. Hold on to that word: part.

## The research · Five states, one tool — and the same missing half
**Walt:** You keep saying part. What's the other part?
**Erin:** The finding that reshaped this whole file. We looked at five jurisdictions that have actually written rules. Virginia, Tennessee, Kentucky, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin. They all reached for the same tool.
**Walt:** Which is?
**Erin:** A but-for test. If the cost wouldn't be incurred but for this customer, this customer pays it. Virginia ordered it. Tennessee wrote those exact words into law. Kentucky priced it into the contract.
**Walt:** Five states, one answer. That sounds like the argument's over.
**Erin:** It sounds that way. Then you read where each of them stopped. Every single one settled the sole-use facilities, the substation and the line to the door. And four of the five left the shared network upgrade undecided.
**Walt:** Shared network upgrade meaning —
**Erin:** The line three counties away that has to be rebuilt because a five-hundred-megawatt customer arrived. It serves everyone. It was triggered by one customer. Who pays for that?
**Walt:** And nobody's said?
**Erin:** Pennsylvania is the one that actually answered. In May its commission ruled that if a shared upgrade wouldn't be needed but for the data center, the data center pays for it, whether or not anyone else benefits. Two weeks later the utilities asked it to reconsider. Virginia expressly deferred it. Wisconsin sent it back for more work. And Tennessee, the state everyone cites for making data centers pay the full cost, wrote an explicit exception for it.
**Walt:** So the state that supposedly solved this carved out the hard case.
**Erin:** Carved it out in its own words. The utility may share those costs with everyone if the upgrade goes beyond what the data center needs. That's the exact question Virginia and Wisconsin deferred. Tennessee didn't answer it. It wrote around it.
**Walt:** So both sides of this fight are standing on the same settled half, and neither's touched the other.

## The research · The numbers people shout, and what they measure
**Erin:** Last of the settled ground, and it's about the numbers people actually shout.
**Walt:** Which one first?
**Erin:** The one you've probably heard: a capacity price of about three hundred and thirty dollars a megawatt-day, blamed on data centers. That number is real. It's also an administrative price cap. The auction hit the ceiling.
**Walt:** So it measures the ceiling, not the demand.
**Erin:** Exactly. A capped price can't tell you what data centers did. What can is a Dallas Fed working paper that models the grid hour by hour with real data-center sites. It finds existing data centers have already raised wholesale prices two to six percent on average, and more in the corridors.
**Walt:** And that's an observation?
**Erin:** No, and I'll say it plainly: it's a dispatch model, not a meter reading. But it's the strongest basis anyone has, and it's the number I'd trust over the price cap.
**Walt:** Second question. The threat is always the same: charge them and they'll build in the next state. Does that happen?
**Erin:** We went looking for a documented case of a data-center project cancelled and relocated because of a moratorium or a cost-allocation tariff. We didn't find one. Absence isn't proof it never happens, so the honest count is: zero found.
**Walt:** But they do move for something.
**Erin:** Tax incentives. The economics literature the Fed paper cites finds state incentives measurably shift where data centers are sited, without a matching rise in local tech jobs. So mobility is real for what states give. It's unproven for what states charge.
**Walt:** Which is exactly the thing this episode is about. Charging.
