LenaYour envelope goes to your city or township clerk. The first thing they check is your signature.

LenaThey compare it with the signature they have on file for you.

Step 2 · The machine test

RajSo my ballot's accepted. Does it go straight into a machine?

LenaNot yet. Before any real ballot is counted, every counting machine has to pass a test. In public.

LenaOfficials run a stack of test ballots through it. And every candidate on the test gets a different number of votes.

RajWhy different?

LenaSo that if a machine mixed two candidates up, the totals would show it. The count has to come out perfect.

LenaIf it doesn't, they find the error and fix it before the machine is used. Then it's sealed.

RajAnd I can actually go to this?

LenaYou can. The time and place have to be published at least forty-eight hours ahead.

LenaCities and townships are posting their tests now.

LenaThey have to be done by October nineteenth for early voting machines, and October twenty-ninth for Election Day ones.

RajDoor number two. Open.

Step 3 · Early isn't early results

RajOkay. When does my ballot actually get counted?

LenaMaybe before Election Day. Towns of five thousand people or more can choose to start up to eight days early.

LenaThis year, that's from October twenty-sixth. They open the envelopes, and run the ballots through the machines.

RajWait. So they know who's winning a week early?

LenaNo. And this is the part people get wrong. The ballots go through the machines, but no totals can be produced until eight at night on Election Day.

LenaEvery day, the ballots are sealed, the number of ballots is posted, and inspectors from both parties sign.

RajSo why bother doing it early?

LenaSpeed. In twenty twenty-four, Sterling Heights used early processing. By about five in the afternoon on Election Day, it had more than twenty thousand ballots through.

LenaWarren, right next door and nearly the same size, skipped it. At ten at night, it still had a few thousand waiting.

LenaTowns that do it have to post where and when by October sixteenth.

RajAnd I can watch that?

LenaFrom a public viewing area, if there's room. Party challengers get closer.

Step 4 · The counting room

RajOkay. Election Day. Where's my ballot now?

LenaOn a counting board. And the law says it needs at least one inspector from each major party.

LenaParty and group challengers can stand where they see each ballot counted. They just can't touch it.

RajAnd regular people, like me?

LenaYou can watch from a public viewing area, if there's space.

LenaAuthorized reporters can film wide shots, with no ballot faces. Nobody else records.

RajIs the machine on the internet while it counts?

LenaNo. Michigan's rules say counting machines can't be connected while they're tabulating.

LenaAfter the count is done and printed, they can connect, to send in unofficial results. And the paper ballots are kept.

RajWhat if my ballot's torn? Or the machine can't read it?

LenaThen it's copied, not guessed. Two inspectors from different parties fill in a fresh ballot, marked duplicate.

LenaIt gets a serial number that ties it back to your original. And your original is kept.

RajSo a duplicate ballot isn't a fake ballot.

LenaIt's your vote, copied by two people from different parties, with a trail back to the one you marked.

Step 5 · Official takes weeks

RajPolls close at eight. When do we actually know?

LenaMost of it, fast. In twenty twenty-four, ninety-four percent of Election Day returns were in by nine the next morning.

LenaBut those are unofficial. The official result takes weeks.

LenaChecking. In every county, a board of canvassers, from both parties, makes sure the numbers add up.

LenaThey have until November seventeenth. And the job is arithmetic, not opinion. The law calls it ministerial.

LenaMeaning they have to certify based on the returns. Not on what they think of the result.
