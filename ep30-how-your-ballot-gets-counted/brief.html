Research brief — How Your Ballot Gets Counted: Every Step, and Where You Can Watch

The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video. It is an EXPLAINER: one Michigan ballot followed from the moment the voter lets go of it to certification, with every checkpoint and every place the public may watch. Every figure here is in the fact-check log.

The question

What actually happens to your ballot after you let go of it, and how much of it can you watch? Distrust of the count is now close to even between the parties: in July 2026, 55% of Republicans and 58% of Democrats said they were confident the midterms will be run fairly and accurately (Pew). So the ballot's journey is a question for every viewer, whoever they vote for.

Why now

Ballots for the 2026-11-03 midterms are already in Michigan voters' hands, and one batch has already gone wrong and been caught. On 2026-09-28 Wayne County told Belleville, Sumpter Township and Van Buren Township that the absentee ballots they had mailed listed a library board candidate in the wrong race. At least 8,500 voters are being sent new ones, and officials say any faulty ballot that comes back will be copied by hand onto a correct one, under the state's standing guidance for these errors (Van Buren Township notice, 2026-09-29; Belleville notice, 2026-09-29; Votebeat, 2026-10-02; county statement as quoted by FOX 2 Detroit). Michigan's general rule for a damaged or defective absentee ballot is that election inspectors from different parties copy it onto a duplicate, serial-numbered back to the original (MCL 168.765c); which provision officials are applying to this misprint has not been stated.

The "where can I watch" half is live too. Cities and townships are posting the public tests of their counting machines this month: Eagle Township on 10-05, Farmington Hills on 10-12, Cass County's early-voting equipment on 10-13 (notice), Dexter Township on 10-19. The state rule is titled "Public accuracy test", and the time and place must be published at least 48 hours ahead (MCL 168.798).

Settled: the ballot's journey (Michigan, 2026)

Each step as the record supports saying it.

  1. The envelope arrives. Your city or township clerk compares your signature with the ones on file, and can reject it only if it differs "in significant and obvious respects". If it is rejected or unsigned, the clerk has to contact you, by phone, email or text if they have them, and you have until 5 p.m. on Friday, November 6 to fix it. (The deadline to fix a signature on your ballot application is earlier: 4 p.m., Friday, October 30.)
  2. Before any ballot is counted, the machines are tested in public. Every tabulator is run on a test deck that gives each candidate a different number of votes; it must come out perfect and be certified. The test is announced at least 48 hours ahead, and must be done by October 19 for early-voting machines and October 29 for Election Day machines.
  3. Early processing is not early results. Towns of 5,000 or more can choose to start opening and tabulating absentee ballots up to eight days early, from October 26. No totals can be produced until 8 p.m. on Election Day, and each day's ballots are sealed. Such towns must post where and when they will count by October 16.
  4. Counting. Wherever absentee ballots are counted, at least one inspector from each major party has to be in the room the whole time.
  5. A ballot the machine can't read is copied, not guessed. Two inspectors from different parties copy it onto a fresh ballot marked "duplicate", with a serial number that ties it to the original, which is kept.
  6. The county canvass. After the polls close, each county's bipartisan board of canvassers checks that the numbers add up, and must finish by November 17. It is arithmetic and paperwork, not a recount; the law calls the job "ministerial". Ballots cured after Election Day are tabulated at a county canvassers' meeting.
  7. State certification. The four-member Board of State Canvassers, two from each major party, must certify by November 23, in a meeting open to the public and streamed online. (In 2024 it certified on day 17; in 2022, on day 20.)
  8. Audits. After certification, precincts are picked at random for audits, including a hand count, and the state constitution says audits are "conducted in public". In 2024 the hand count covered nearly 120,000 ballots, and the largest change for any candidate was 11 votes.

The reframe: does seeing the process restore trust?

Who wins moves trust several times more than anything an election office can show you. After the 2024 result, the winning side's confidence in the mail count rose 53 points and the losing side's fell 17 (Pew). The swing runs both ways over time: in 2004 the party holding the White House was the confident one, 87 to 59; in 2024, with the other party in the White House, it was 84 to 28 (Gallup).

Seeing the process is a smaller lever, but it is the one that works the same for both sides. In survey experiments, one short official video raised trust in the count by roughly 2 to 13 points, measured soon after viewing. The team's own real-world test a month later, on skeptical conservatives, moved one of five measures, by 1.3 points. The one study that measured both on the same scale found a short audit video, watched four weeks earlier, protected about as much trust as losing took away. It shielded trust; it did not close the gap between winners and losers.

The honest lineseeing how the count works is most useful before the result, as a shield, not

after it, as a cure.

Both sides quietly agree

Winning moves trust more than any official can, and people who actually see the work trust it more. The open question is scale: 772,433 people worked the polls nationally in 2024, against 158 million ballots.

Verdict framing: the dial you set

No winner is declared, because this is an explainer. The viewer gets one dial: what would you need to see to trust the count? A livestream, a public machine test, an audit result, a seat in the viewing area, or your own shift as a poll worker. Each setting has a real place you can go in Michigan this fall, and each has a documented limit.

Script traps

  • Never say Michigan law requires the Wayne County hand-copying. Say officials will copy the choices by hand; state the two-party rule only as Michigan's general rule.
  • Say "at least 8,500" voters, never 10,300. "Nearly 10,000" only as the three communities' permanent mail-vote list (9,965, CBS).
  • Cass County's 10-13 test covers its early-voting equipment only. Present the public tests as "cities and townships are posting their tests now".
  • "Processed early" never means "counted early" in the sense of results. Ballots are tabulated; results are withheld until 8 p.m.
  • The 10 to 12.5-point trust figure is trust in another state's elections, from a university release (the paper could not be opened). Air it only as "a university release reports", or not at all.
  • The Maine poll-worker study has no published figure; describe it without one.
  • Don't air the 64%/68% and 30%-vs-7% "both agree" survey shares or the 5.5 to 6-point audit-results effect until the papers are read.
  • 2024 turnout: about 78% of active registered voters. The Bureau's own "74.6% of active registered voters" is a wording error (74.6% is turnout of the citizen voting-age population).
  • Arizona's counting-room camera law depends on the legislature funding it, and streams only during tabulation.
  • No candidate is named, the Detroit counts are dropped, and the federal-versus-state disputes of 2026 get at most one neutral line.
