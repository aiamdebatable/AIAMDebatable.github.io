Sources — "Rent control: tenant lifeline or supply poison?"

Grouped by claim. Every on-screen number and named case traces here. Verdicts in factcheck-log.md.

The canonical study — both halves

  • SF's 1994 expansion: rent control "limits renters' mobility by 20 percent and lowers displacement"; "a 15% decline in the number of renters living in these buildings" (⚠️ treated landlords' own stock, not citywide); channels are sale to owner-occupants, redevelopment, condo conversion. 44,502 treated vs 1,861 controls; authors assert no external validity to other cities — Diamond, McQuade & Qian, AER 2019, 109(9):3365–94: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20181289
  • ⚠️ The "5.1% city-wide rent increase" and "$2.9 billion in tenant benefits" appear only in the working paper, which also reports an offsetting GE welfare loss the authors say "essentially completely undo" the gains — NBER w24181: https://www.nber.org/papers/w24181
  • ⚠️ SF's law has vacancy decontrol (no price restriction between tenants) and does exempt new construction (pre-1980 coverage; Costa-Hawkins bars control on 1994+ stock).

Second-generation stabilization — the pro-control plank that survived

  • 161 New Jersey municipalities over 10,000 (74 controlled, 87 not), 2010 Census, OLS across seven DVs; new construction 2000–2010: "Rent control is not significant in any of the models" (⚠️ cross-sectional, not causal) — Ambrosius et al., "Forty Years of Rent Control," Cities 49 (2015):121–133: https://ecommons.udayton.edu/pol_fac_pub/40/

Price caps tested elsewhere

  • Berlin Mietendeckel: advertised rents −7–11%; weekly advertised units halved from 600+ to under 300; unregulated Potsdam-area rents +5%/+9%/+12% across the first three quarters of 2020 — Hahn, Kholodilin & Waltl, Management Science 2024, 70(3):1901–1923: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2023.4775
  • Britain: 1915 Act restricted rents to August 1914 levels; private rented sector 90% of stock (1915) → under 10% (1991); 1988 Housing Act deregulated new lettings from Jan 1989. ⚠️ The report dates the sector's recovery to "since 2000," not to the 1989 deregulation, and was commissioned by the Residential Landlords Association — Whitehead & Williams, LSE 2018: https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/106114/

St. Paul — the case both sides quote badly

  • Permits 701 → 224 units, Nov 2021–Jan 2022 = −68% over three months (⚠️ the circulating claim of "−84% over six months, Minneapolis tripled" is false on all three counts; Minneapolis was "up") — MinnPost on HUD/Census data, 10 Mar 2022: https://www.minnpost.com/cityscape/2022/03/in-first-months-since-passage-of-st-pauls-rent-control-ordinance-housing-construction-is-way-down/
  • Full permit series: 2,000+ units in 2020 and 2021 (most since 1970), 1,169 in 2022, 1,204 in 2023, 404 in 2024, 357 in 2025 — the sustained decline post-dates the Jan 2023 new-construction exemption. St. Paul rents −7%+ (2021–2026), with declines also in Minneapolis, Bloomington, Eagan, Eden Prairie and Woodbury, none rent-controlled — Minneapolis Fed: https://www.minneapolisfed.org/article/2026/housing-policies-in-saint-paul-yield-mixed-results-data-and-developers-say

Repeal — what decontrol actually delivered

  • Cambridge MA 1994: decontrol added ~$1.8B to housing value 1994–2004, "nearly a quarter of total Cambridge residential price appreciation," with spillovers to never-controlled properties accounting for more than half. Question 9 passed 51–49 statewide; Cambridge voted 58–42 against — Autor, Palmer & Pathak, JPE 122(3):661–717 (2014) / NBER w18125: https://www.nber.org/papers/w18125

New York after HSTPA — four claims, four different fates

  • MCI applications: 1,342 (SFY2018-19) → 834 → 311 → 309 → 337 → 319, a post-HSTPA average of ~322/yr (~75% down). Median approved MCI value rose $112,700 → $174,459 on fewer, larger projects — NY HCR Office of Rent Administration, statutorily mandated reports: https://hcr.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/12/ora_2025-annual-report_final.pdf
  • Stabilized units vacant-but-unavailable: 42,860 (2021) → 26,310 (2023), −38.6%; available vacancy rate 0.98% (2023); fewer than 2,000 low-rent units vacant for unfunded repairs. ⚠️ HCR explicitly disclaims the "warehousing" reading — the count "does not indicate the cause of vacancy" — NYC Comptroller on NYCHVS data: https://comptroller.nyc.gov/newsroom/rent-stabilized-vacancies-plummeted-over-last-2-years-including-for-units-in-need-of-repairs-new-nyc-comptroller-report-finds
  • Per-unit values in buildings containing stabilized units, citywide: $398,181 (2019) → $289,478 (2025) = −27.3% nominal, −41.5% real — NYC Dept. of Finance via RGB 2026 Mortgage Survey, Tables 9–10: https://rentguidelinesboard.cityofnewyork.us/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/2026-MSR.pdf
  • Poor physical and financial conditions "affect only a small portion of the rent stabilized stock," concentrated in pre-1974 Bronx and Upper Manhattan buildings — NYC IBO, May 2026: https://www.ibo.nyc.gov/assets/ibo/downloads/pdf/press-releases/2026/2026-may-rent-stabilization-series.pdf

The money on both sides

  • NAR lobbying 2024: $86,385,941, top of any US organization (⚠️ not $63.5M) — OpenSecrets: https://www.opensecrets.org/orgs/national-assn-of-realtors/summary?id=d000000062
  • Open Philanthropy ~$27M to land-use reform since 2015 plus a $120M Abundance & Growth Fund (2025); California YIMBY received ~$5.5M (⚠️ funder self-reported)
  • ⚠️ Prop 33 (2024): ~$47M for (AIDS Healthcare Foundation), ~$100M against (California Apartment Association + California Association of Realtors) — secondary reporting only; Cal-Access filings not retrieved, so not used on air.
  • Construction inputs +42.2% Feb 2020 → Jan 2026, a competing explanation for permit declines that any rent-control effect must be separated from — BLS PPI via AGC: https://www.agc.org/sites/default/files/users/user21902/PPI%20Tables%202026_1.pdf

Checked and NOT used (see factcheck-log)

  • "$2.9 billion in tenant benefits" / "5.1% city-wide rent increase" — working-paper only; the first inverts the authors' conclusion when quoted alone.
  • "SF's law had no vacancy decontrol and no new-build exemption" — false on both counts.
  • "St. Paul permits −84% in six months while Minneapolis tripled" — false on all three counts.
  • "Manhattan stabilized values $290K → $122K" — Upper Manhattan, 2018–2024, from a tenant-side nonprofit; contradicted by both Ariel's and the city's own data.
  • "~40,000 warehoused units" — 2021 vacant-but-unavailable count; regulator disclaims the inference.
  • "Values down up to 80%" — undisclosed brokerage sample, self-described maximum.
