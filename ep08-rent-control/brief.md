# Research brief — Rent Control: Tenant Lifeline or Supply Poison?

_The full research behind the episode. This is the "full research" we link below the video._

> **Topic-shape verdict (guardrail, logged before either side was written):** ✅ **Genuinely
> two-sided.** Short-run protection for sitting tenants versus long-run effects on the rental stock is
> a real empirical split with credible peer-reviewed work on both sides. No manufactured balance is
> needed here — but the fight turns on a distinction almost nobody makes out loud: **first-generation
> hard caps versus second-generation stabilization.**

## Why now

**New York has just frozen the rent on roughly 928,000 apartments.** On 25 June 2026 the city's Rent
Guidelines Board adopted **Apartment & Loft Order #58**: a **0% adjustment on one-year leases and 0%
on two-year leases** commencing between 1 October 2026 and 30 September 2027 — the first two-year
freeze in the board's history. The stabilized stock it covers had a vacancy rate of **0.98%**, against
1.41% citywide.

⚠ **This is not background colour, it is the episode's own distinction happening in public.** The
freeze applies to **rent-stabilized** units — second-generation regulation, with vacancy decontrol
and a new-construction exemption. Almost every famous number in the rent-control fight describes a
**first-generation** hard cap: Berlin's freeze, Britain's eighty years, 1970s Cambridge. The argument
this episode makes — that the two are being judged with each other's evidence — is exactly the
argument now playing out over a real policy affecting close to a million homes.

## The question

**Does rent control protect tenants, or shrink the housing they need?**

Companion to [ep07](../../ep07-housing-supply/research/brief.md), which asks the same fight from the
supply end. Live because St. Paul enacted a 3% cap in 2021, California voted on Prop 33 in 2024, and
Berlin's Mietendeckel was struck down after briefly working exactly as both sides predicted.

## Settled (not the debate)

- **Rent control protects the people living in the units.** San Francisco's 1994 expansion limited
  covered renters' mobility by 20% and lowered displacement. — Diamond, McQuade & Qian, *AER* 2019
- **And it shrinks the regulated stock.** The same paper, same authors: a 15% decline in the number of
  renters living in treated buildings, via condo conversion, redevelopment, and sale to owner-occupants.
- **Price caps do cut advertised rents, and do cut listings.** Berlin's Mietendeckel cut advertised
  rents 7–11% while weekly advertised units halved from 600+ to under 300, with rent increases
  spilling into unregulated neighbouring areas. — Hahn, Kholodilin & Waltl, *Management Science* 2024
- **Repeal enriches owners without producing affordability.** Cambridge's 1994 decontrol added ~$1.8B
  in property value 1994–2004 — nearly a quarter of all local appreciation — with **more than half**
  flowing to properties that were never controlled. — Autor, Palmer & Pathak, *JPE* 2014
- **Regulation does suppress renovation investment.** New York MCI applications fell from 1,342
  (SFY2018-19) to ~322/yr after HSTPA — roughly 75% down, per the state regulator's own mandated
  reporting. — NY HCR
- **Over a long enough clock, hard control can dissolve a tenure.** Britain's private rented sector
  fell from ~90% of stock in 1915 to under 10% by 1991 under rent restriction. — LSE (⚠️ commissioned
  by the Residential Landlords Association; and the report dates the sector's recovery to "since 2000,"
  **not** to the 1989 deregulation)

## Contested (the real fight)

**Side A — "Lifeline":** The consensus against rent control was built on 1970s hard caps and is
routinely deployed against modern laws that share almost none of their design features. Forty years
of New Jersey's moderate ordinances show **no statistically significant effect on new construction**
across 161 municipalities. The canonical indictment — the San Francisco study — describes a law with
vacancy decontrol and a new-construction exemption, i.e. it never restricted new building at all. And
the alternative on offer has a track record: Cambridge's repeal made owners $1.8B richer and made
nobody's rent affordable. A 20% reduction in forced moves is a real, measured good that accrues to
people, not to buildings.

**Side B — "Supply poison":** Every mechanism runs the same direction, across every country and
decade tested. Landlords exit: San Francisco −15% of treated stock. Listings collapse: Berlin halved
in weeks. Investment stops: New York's MCI filings down ~75%. Tenure dissolves: Britain, 90% to 10%
over three generations, invisible in any single decade. And the binding damage is upstream of any
pro forma — a construction lender declines the market outright, so the project dies at the term sheet
months before there is a rent roll to cap. The tenant in the unit wins; the tenant who needed the
next unit never finds out what they lost.

## The reframe

**Both sides are arguing about different generations of a policy that shares only a name.** The
argument is almost never "does a 3% cap with vacancy decontrol and a new-build exemption reduce
construction" — it is "rent control failed in 1970s Boston, therefore." The New Jersey evidence and
the Berlin evidence point opposite ways because they *are* opposite policies.

**And the second reframe — the one verification produced by itself:** the most-repeated statistic in
this entire debate is wrong, and its error is a chosen window. "St. Paul permits fell 84% in six
months while Minneapolis tripled" is false on all three counts — the real figure is **−68% over three
months**, and Minneapolis was merely "up." More importantly, the permit series continues: **1,169 in
2022 and 1,204 in 2023** before falling to 404 in 2024. The rebound years are routinely dropped, and
the actual sustained collapse **post-dates the January 2023 new-construction exemption** — the
opposite of what the story claims. Meanwhile St. Paul rents fell over 7% since 2021, and so did rents
in Minneapolis, Bloomington, Eagan, Eden Prairie and Woodbury, none rent-controlled.

## Both sides quietly agree

**The single most cited paper in this fight says both things at once, and each camp quotes half of one
sentence.** Diamond, McQuade & Qian found a 20% mobility reduction *and* a 15% supply reduction —
tenant win and market loss are the same result, in the same table, by the same authors. Neither
finding is disputed by anyone. The disagreement is entirely about which half matters more, which is a
values question wearing an evidence question's clothes.

⚠️ **Do not quote the "$2.9 billion in tenant benefits" figure alone.** It is working-paper only, and
that same paper reports an offsetting general-equilibrium welfare loss the authors say "essentially
completely undo" the gains. Quoting the benefit without the offset inverts the finding.

## Verdict framing

No winner. The evidence genuinely supports both core claims, and the episode says so directly rather
than splitting the difference. What is handed to the viewer is the question that actually decides the
policy: **which generation of rent control is on the ballot in front of you** — a hard cap, or a
stabilization law with vacancy decontrol and a new-build exemption — because the evidence for one
says almost nothing about the other.

The viewer's tool, shared with ep07: **ask what window a number is measured over.** In ep07 it was
Austin's peak-versus-average. Here it is St. Paul's missing rebound years. Same move, opposite
political direction — which is the point.

## Reader's caution — what this brief will not say, and why

Checked and **failed**:

- "St. Paul permits fell 84% in six months while Minneapolis tripled" — **false on all three counts**,
  and it omits the 2022–23 rebound.
- "SF's law had no vacancy decontrol and no new-build exemption" — **false on both counts**; this
  was a load-bearing plank of the second-generation argument and it does not survive.
- "$2.9 billion in tenant benefits" and "a 5.1% city-wide rent increase" — **working-paper only**,
  absent from the peer-reviewed article; the first inverts the authors' conclusion when quoted alone.
- "Manhattan stabilized values fell $290K to $122K" — fails as stated (it is *Upper* Manhattan,
  2018–2024, from a tenant-side nonprofit, and contradicted by both sides' own data).
- "~40,000 warehoused units" — that is the 2021 count of units vacant-but-unavailable *for any
  reason*; the regulator explicitly disclaims the warehousing inference.
- "Values down up to 80%" — an undisclosed brokerage sample, self-described as a maximum. Independent
  NYC Department of Finance data gives −27% nominal / −41% real.
