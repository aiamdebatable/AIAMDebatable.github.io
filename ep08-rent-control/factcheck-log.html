Episode 8 · Fact-check log

Fact-check log — Rent Control: Tenant Lifeline or Supply Poison?

Independent verification pass, run 2026-08-19 (web-sourced, not trusting the draft). Each claim gets a verdict: CONFIRMED, CORRECTED, UNRESOLVED or APPROXIMATE. This is a REQUIRED gate in this show's production process. The show's differentiator is that this file is real.

  • CONFIRMED — the source says what the episode says.
  • CORRECTED — the source says otherwise; the script was changed, and the change is listed under "Corrections applied to source" below. These stay in the file: they are the point of it.
  • UNRESOLVED — could not be established from a source worth trusting, so it is not used on air.
  • APPROXIMATE — checked, roughly right, and deliberately not cited on air.

Verification statusadversarial-pass

Method: five independent research lenses produced the claim set; seven verification agents then checked every numeric and named claim against its primary source. The New York cluster was given an explicit presumption against on sourcing grounds, and received a second dedicated pass. Shared with ep07, which draws on the same pass.

1. "San Francisco's 1994 rent-control expansion limited renters' mobility by 20% and lowered displacement."

CONFIRMED — verbatim from the peer-reviewed abstract. Diamond, McQuade & Qian, American Economic Review 2019, 109(9):3365–94. The NBER phrasing gives the mechanism: covered renters' probability of staying at their 1993 address rose by nearly 20%, tracked 1994–2016.

  • https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20181289

2. "The same study found landlords cut rental supply by 15%."

CONFIRMED, with a scope correction. The paper reports "a 15% decline in the number of renters living in these buildings" — i.e. the treated landlords' own stock, not citywide. Channels: sale to owner-occupants, redevelopment, condo conversion. The episode states the scope.

3. "Rent control caused a 5.1% city-wide rent increase."

CORRECTED — not in the peer-reviewed article. This figure appears only in NBER working paper w24181, derived from a modelled ~6% citywide supply decline. The published AER version states the effect qualitatively only: it "likely drove up market rents in the long run." The episode uses the qualitative claim and does not state the number.

4. "The law generated $2.9 billion in benefits to tenants."

CORRECTED — working-paper only, and it inverts the finding when quoted alone. Also NBER-only ("$214 million annually and $2.9 billion present discounted value"). The same source reports an offsetting general-equilibrium welfare loss to renters that the authors conclude "essentially completely undo" the gains. The published AER article defers welfare analysis to a companion paper. Not used on air in either direction.

5. "San Francisco's law had no vacancy decontrol and no new-build exemption."

CORRECTED — false on both counts. The law "regulated price increases within the duration of a tenancy, but no price restrictions between tenants" — that is vacancy decontrol. New construction is exempt: coverage is pre-1980 buildings, and Costa-Hawkins "precludes any California city from rent controlling any housing stock built 1994 or later." This was a load-bearing plank of the second-generation argument and it does not survive; the argument is rebuilt on New Jersey instead.

6. "The San Francisco study generalises to rent control as a policy."

CORRECTED. The design rests on 44,502 treated renters against 1,861 controls (892 parcels) — a thin control arm — and the authors assert no external validity to other cities. The episode attributes findings to San Francisco's 1994 expansion specifically, never to "rent control" in general.

7. "Forty years of New Jersey's moderate rent ordinances show no significant effect on new construction."

CONFIRMED. Ambrosius et al., "Forty Years of Rent Control," Cities 49 (2015):121–133, Elsevier, peer-reviewed. 161 New Jersey municipalities over 10,000 population (74 controlled, 87 not), 2010 Census, OLS across seven dependent variables; new construction 2000–2010 is DV4. Text: "No significant differences exist between the two groups of cities in new construction"; "Rent control is not significant in any of the models." Design caveat carried on air: cross-sectional OLS, not a causal design — suggestive, not dispositive.

  • https://ecommons.udayton.edu/pol_fac_pub/40/

8. "Berlin's rent cap cut advertised rents 7–11% while collapsing supply."

CONFIRMED. Hahn, Kholodilin & Waltl, Management Science 2024, 70(3):1901–1923. Advertised rents down 7–11%; weekly advertised units "halved from more than 600 per week before the announcement to less than 300"; rents in the unregulated Potsdam area rose roughly 5%, 9% and 12% across the first three quarters of 2020.

  • https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2023.4775

9. "St. Paul's rent cap cut permits 84% in six months while Minneapolis's tripled."

CORRECTED — false on all three counts. Origin is MinnPost (10 Mar 2022) on HUD/Census permit data: three months (Nov 2021–Jan 2022), 701 → 224 units = −68%, and Minneapolis merely "up," not tripled. Corrected in full.

  • https://www.minnpost.com/cityscape/2022/03/in-first-months-since-passage-of-st-pauls-rent-control-ordinance-housing-construction-is-way-down/

10. "St. Paul permits collapsed from 2,000+ to 404."

CORRECTED — the series omits its own rebound. Minneapolis Fed data: over 2,000 units permitted in 2020 and 2021 (most since 1970), 1,169 in 2022 and 1,204 in 2023, then 404 in 2024 and 357 in

  1. The rebound years are routinely dropped from the talking point, and **the sustained decline

post-dates the January 2023 new-construction exemption** — the opposite of what the story implies. The episode states the whole series.

  • https://www.minneapolisfed.org/article/2026/housing-policies-in-saint-paul-yield-mixed-results-data-and-developers-say

11. "St. Paul rents fell after the cap — and so did rents in cities without one."

CONFIRMED. The Minneapolis Fed dashboard shows St. Paul median rents down more than 7% from 2021 to 2026, with declines also in Minneapolis, Bloomington, Eagan, Eden Prairie and Woodbury, none rent-controlled. Rents fell despite the ordinance as readily as because of it — neither side can claim the trend.

12. "Cambridge's 1994 decontrol added $1.8 billion in property value, over half of it to never-controlled units."

CONFIRMED. Autor, Palmer & Pathak, Journal of Political Economy 122(3):661–717 (2014) / NBER w18125, verbatim: "$1.8 billion… between 1994 and 2004, equal to nearly a quarter of total Cambridge residential price appreciation… spillovers to never-controlled properties account for more than half." Question 9 passed 51–49 statewide; Cambridge itself voted 58–42 against.

  • https://www.nber.org/papers/w18125

13. "Britain's private rented sector fell from 90% of stock in 1915 to under 10% by 1991, then doubled after the 1988 deregulation."

CORRECTED on the causal half. The series is confirmed inside the source: the 1915 Act restricted rents to their August 1914 level; the sector fell "from 90% of the total housing stock in 1915 to under 10% by 1991"; the Housing Act 1988 deregulated new lettings from January 1989. But the report dates the sector's recovery to "since 2000," not to the 1989 deregulation — the post-1989 causal chain was the lens's invention, and the sector stayed near its trough through the 1990s. ⚠️ Sourcing note carried in the brief: Whitehead & Williams (2018) was commissioned by the Residential Landlords Association.

  • https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/106114/

14. "New York MCI applications fell from about 1,000 a year to about 300 after HSTPA."

CONFIRMED — by the regulator, not the industry. NY HCR's own statutorily mandated reports (HSTPA Part L requires them): applications filed were 1,342 in SFY2018-19, then 834, 311, 309, 337 and 319 — a post-HSTPA average of ~322/yr, roughly 75% down. The "~1,000" baseline is if anything conservative. This claim was flagged with a presumption against as landlord-sourced and that presumption was wrong: the primary source is a state agency.

  • https://hcr.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/12/ora_2025-annual-report_final.pdf

15. "Manhattan median rent-stabilized unit values fell from $290,000 to $122,000."

CORRECTED — fails as stated. The figure traces to the University Neighborhood Housing Program, a Bronx tenant-side nonprofit (not a landlord group), and describes Upper Manhattan over 2018–2024 — not Manhattan, and not the HSTPA window. The landlord source contradicts it: Ariel's own 2025 table gives Manhattan $454K → $249K. Independent NYC Department of Finance data via the RGB Mortgage Survey gives Manhattan per-unit averages of $556,067 (2019) → $351,579 (2024) → $619,560 (2025). Not used.

16. "About 40,000 rent-stabilized units are being warehoused."

CORRECTED. The number is the 2021 NYCHVS count of stabilized units "vacant but unavailable for rent" (42,860) — a category covering renovation, legal proceedings, owner use and pending sale. The 2023 figure is 26,310, down 38.6%, with the available vacancy rate at 0.98%. Fewer than 2,000 low-rent units were vacant for unfunded repairs. HCR explicitly disclaims the warehousing inference, noting the count "does not indicate the cause of vacancy, condition of units or whether the units are transitioning between tenancies."

  • https://comptroller.nyc.gov/newsroom/rent-stabilized-vacancies-plummeted-over-last-2-years-including-for-units-in-need-of-repairs-new-nyc-comptroller-report-finds

17. "Rent-stabilized building values are down up to 80% since HSTPA."

UNRESOLVED. Origin is Ariel Property Advisors (June 2025): "A sample survey… shows that values have dropped by as much as 80%." No sample size is disclosed, and "as much as" is the extreme of a selected repeat-sale set — Ariel's own other publications say 30–50% and citywide −40%. Independent NYC Department of Finance data via the RGB 2026 Mortgage Survey gives citywide per-unit values of $398,181 (2019) → $289,478 (2025) = −27.3% nominal, −41.5% real. NYC IBO (May 2026) finds poor physical and financial conditions "affect only a small portion of the rent stabilized stock." The 80% figure is not used; the DOF series is used instead.

  • https://rentguidelinesboard.cityofnewyork.us/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/2026-MSR.pdf

18. "Construction lenders decline rent-controlled markets outright, killing projects before any rent roll exists."

UNRESOLVED. A practitioner mechanism claim, not a measured one. No study was found that isolates lender withdrawal from the confounding collapse in project feasibility documented in claim 19. Stated on air as a named industry perspective, not as an established fact.

19. "Construction costs are up about 40% since 2019."

CONFIRMED. BLS PPI via AGC, Feb 2020 → Jan 2026: inputs to new construction +42.2%. Relevant here because it is a competing explanation for permit declines that any rent-control effect must be separated from — including in St. Paul.

  • https://www.agc.org/sites/default/files/users/user21902/PPI%20Tables%202026_1.pdf

20. "California's Prop 33 saw $42 million spent for and $106 million against."

CORRECTED, and still not primary-verified. Approximately $47M of ~$50M for (AIDS Healthcare Foundation) and ~$100M of ~$125M against (California Apartment Association and the California Association of Realtors). These come from CalMatters and Ballotpedia; Cal-Access filings could not be retrieved. Marked UNRESOLVED for on-air use pending committee filings.

21. "The National Association of Realtors spent $63.5 million lobbying in 2024, the most of any organization."

CORRECTED. NAR was the top spender, but at $86,385,941 (OpenSecrets), up roughly $35M from ~$50M in 2023.

22. "Open Philanthropy granted about $7 million to land-use reform; California YIMBY took $500,000 from them."

CORRECTED — understated roughly fourfold. Open Philanthropy reports ~$27M to land-use reform since 2015, plus a $120M Abundance & Growth Fund (2025). California YIMBY received ~$5.5M from Open Philanthropy, not $500K. ⚠️ Funder self-reported; used only to establish that both movements are financed, never as a precise ledger.

Corrections applied to source

  • SF citywide rent increase (5.1%) and tenant benefit ($2.9B) → both removed as working-paper-only; the $2.9B additionally inverts the authors' conclusion when quoted without its offsetting welfare loss.
  • "No vacancy decontrol, no new-build exemption" → removed; the second-generation argument rebuilt on Ambrosius/New Jersey, which survived verification.
  • 15% supply effect → rescoped to the treated landlords' own stock.
  • SF findings → attributed to San Francisco's 1994 expansion specifically, never to rent control generally.
  • St. Paul → "−84% over six months, Minneapolis tripled" corrected to "−68% over three months, Minneapolis up"; the 2022–23 rebound years restored to the series; the post-exemption timing stated.
  • Britain → the post-1989 causal chain removed; the LSE report's commissioning body disclosed.
  • New York → the MCI claim promoted to CONFIRMED on HCR's own reports; the $290K→$122K, 40,000-warehoused and "up to 80%" claims all cut and replaced with NYC DOF/RGB and NYCHVS figures.
  • NAR lobbying → $63.5M corrected to $86.4M.
  • Open Philanthropy → ~$7M corrected to ~$27M; California YIMBY $500K corrected to ~$5.5M.
  • Prop 33 → figures corrected and still withheld from air pending primary filings.

Fairness audit

  • Neither side written beyond its evidence? Both sides lost a load-bearing claim in this pass. The pro-control side lost the "no vacancy decontrol" plank and the $2.9B benefit figure; the anti-control side lost the St. Paul 84% statistic, the NAR figure and three of the four New York claims. Neither case is presented at its rhetorical maximum.
  • Straw-manning / cherry-picking? The episode explicitly names window-selection as a shared tactic and demonstrates it against the anti-control side (St. Paul's omitted rebound) after demonstrating it against the pro-supply side in ep07 (Austin's peak baseline). The symmetry is deliberate.
  • Sourcing symmetry: the New York cluster carried an explicit presumption against on the belief it was landlord-sourced. That belief was wrong in both directions — the strongest claim came from the state regulator, the valuation claim originated with a tenant-side nonprofit, and "warehousing" is itself a tenant-advocate framing. Recorded here because judging a claim by which side benefits from it would have gotten three of four wrong, and that is a reusable lesson rather than a one-off.
  • Identity↔stance rotation: not yet applicable — cast rotation is step 4, not run in this pass.

Open questions this episode could not settle

  • Does second-generation stabilization (vacancy decontrol, new-build exemption, moderate cap) actually behave differently from a first-generation hard cap? The New Jersey evidence suggests yes, but it is cross-sectional OLS, and no causal design testing the distinction was found.
  • How much of St. Paul's permit decline is the ordinance and how much is the ~42% construction-cost increase and the national multifamily slowdown? No source separates them.
  • Is the lender-withdrawal mechanism real and measurable, or is it feasibility collapse under another name? Claim 18 remains unresolved.
  • Does social and public housing at scale outperform both options? Every lens accepted the build-versus-control frame without examining it.
