ChatGPT Codex and Your Work: Should You Ever Put Unpublished Work Into a Public AI?

The question: A mathematician fed a year of unpublished drafts into OpenAI's Codex, then watched OpenAI announce a proof of the problem he was closing in on. The lab says no user input after July 3 could have influenced it — and cannot rule out that de-identified usage improved the model. Is the risk of handing your work to an AI lab real, or is the leak always a human?

He typed a year of drafts into the tool. Then the tool's owner announced the result.

Why anyone is asking now

When this episode came out in September 2026, a mathematician had said he put a year of drafts into OpenAI's coding tool, Codex, and then OpenAI announced a proof of a related result. Over five days, OpenAI's written answer on his data moved from "cannot rule out" to a flat no.

The fight

Green arguesThe switch covers you

Every personal plan has a training opt-out, business plans don't train by default, and one switch covers the coding tool. Every documented exposure came from a bug, a checkbox, an open database or a court. No case shows a model handing one user's draft to another. Flip the switch, keep the crown jewels out, and use the tool.

Gold arguesNo setting covers it

The switch turns off training and nothing else. Flagged chats are kept for years, reviewers can read them, and a court ordered 20 million chat logs handed over. Another court held that typing a secret into the tool was the disclosure. And the risk that moved in September was attention, which no setting reaches.

What both sides quietly agree on

Personal plans train by default at all three big companies, with an opt-out that covers the coding tool, and business plans don't. Opting out of training doesn't stop retention: flagged content stays for years and reviewers can read it. No documented case shows a model handing one user's draft to another.

What the shouting gets wrong

Two different risks get argued as one. The data risk, meaning what a company keeps, who can read it and what a court can reach, is real and mostly handled in the settings menu. The attention risk, someone learning what you're close to, is real, rare and in no menu at all, and that was the one in September's story.

The verdict

Watched it already? Read the verdict as it aired

Erin This one doesn't resolve, and neither team wins it. Three things from me: what's settled, what survived, and what's yours.

Erin Both teams accepted the same record. Personal plans train by default at all three companies, with an opt-out that covers the coding tool. Business plans don't. Opting out of training does not opt out of retention: flagged content stays for years, reviewers can read it, and de-identified is a word without a test. Tens of billions of logs exist, and a court reached twenty million of them. And no documented case shows a model handing one user's draft to another.

Erin What survived from green. The switch exists, and it closes the one door with no case behind it. Every documented exposure was a bug, a checkbox, a database or a court, and each is a known door. And in September, by both parties' own documents, the route the company describes was a rumour, not a log.

Erin What survived from gold. The paste itself can be the disclosure, before anything leaks. Retention, review and discovery have no toggle. The written answer to the one question took five days and four versions. And the risk that moved this month was attention, which no setting reaches.

Erin What fell. Green's comfort: her switch is one most people never open, and she asked you to trust the fifth answer over the first. Gold's fear: he cannot name one case of the model talking, and his own best story was a person, not a pipeline.

Erin So here's what the record supports. Two risks, argued as one. A data risk that is real, boring, and mostly in the menu. And an attention risk that is real, rare, and not in any menu at all.

Erin That leaves one dial neither team can set for you. Which risk are you actually carrying? If it's the data, there's a checklist: the plan, the switch, the temporary chat, and what never goes in. If it's the attention, if the work is worth a rival's compute, there is no checklist, only who knows what you're doing.

Erin So the question we're handing you isn't paste or don't. It's this: before you type the next draft, which of the two are you afraid of, and does your fear match the door? That one's yours.
