ChatGPT Codex and Your Work: Should You Ever Put Unpublished Work Into a Public AI?

A mathematician fed a year of unpublished drafts into OpenAI's Codex, then watched OpenAI announce a proof of the problem he was closing in on. The lab says no user input after July 3 could have influenced it — and cannot rule out that de-identified usage improved the model. Is the risk of handing your work to an AI lab real, or is the leak always a human?

Why it was in the news

When this episode came out in September 2026, a mathematician had said he put a year of drafts into OpenAI's coding tool, Codex, and then OpenAI announced a proof of a related result. Over five days, OpenAI's written answer on his data moved from "cannot rule out" to a flat no.

Settled

What both sides accept

Personal plans train by default at all three big companies, with an opt-out that covers the coding tool, and business plans don't. Opting out of training doesn't stop retention: flagged content stays for years and reviewers can read it. No documented case shows a model handing one user's draft to another.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesThe switch covers you

Every personal plan has a training opt-out, business plans don't train by default, and one switch covers the coding tool. Every documented exposure came from a bug, a checkbox, an open database or a court. No case shows a model handing one user's draft to another. Flip the switch, keep the crown jewels out, and use the tool.

Gold arguesNo setting covers it

The switch turns off training and nothing else. Flagged chats are kept for years, reviewers can read them, and a court ordered 20 million chat logs handed over. Another court held that typing a secret into the tool was the disclosure. And the risk that moved in September was attention, which no setting reaches.

What the shouting gets wrong

Two different risks get argued as one. The data risk, meaning what a company keeps, who can read it and what a court can reach, is real and mostly handled in the settings menu. The attention risk, someone learning what you're close to, is real, rare and in no menu at all, and that was the one in September's story.

The verdict

What the record supports
Two risks, argued as one. A data risk that is real, boring, and mostly in the menu. And an attention risk that is real, rare, and not in any menu at all.
What didn't hold up
Green's comfort: her switch is one most people never open, and she asked you to trust the fifth answer over the first. Gold's fear: he cannot name one case of the model talking, and his own best story was a person, not a pipeline.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: Which risk are you actually carrying?

The judge named two risks and declared no winner. Which one you're carrying is yours to say. Open one to see what the record says you can do about it, what it leaves open, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Data risk
    What you can do
    There's a checklist: the plan, the switch, the temporary chat, and what never goes in. The switch exists, and it closes the one door with no case behind it: no documented case shows a model handing one user's draft to another. At OpenAI a personal plan trains unless you opt out, and that one switch covers the coding tool too; Anthropic has you choose at signup. Business plans don't train by default, and temporary and incognito chats are left out of training.
    What it leaves open
    Opting out of training does not opt out of retention: flagged content stays for years, reviewers can read it, and de-identified is a word without a test. Tens of billions of logs exist, and a court reached 20 million of them. The paste itself can be the disclosure: a federal court ended one trade-secrets case because the plaintiff built her frameworks inside ChatGPT and pleaded no measures to keep them secret, and no court has said whether the switch counts as one. And the switch is one most people never open: the written answer to the one question took five days and four versions to become a flat no.
    Not measured
    Whether a model has ever handed one user's draft to another: no case was found, and nobody has published a search that came up empty either.

    What you can do
    There is no checklist, only who knows what you're doing. In September, by both parties' own documents, the route the company describes was a rumour, not a log.
