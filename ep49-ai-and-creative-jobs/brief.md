## The pitch, in one line

**"If anyone can make it for almost nothing, who still gets paid to make it?"** The record says the
cheap, high-volume end of creative work is where the price gap is widest: a 20-minute narration costs about
$1.60 through a commercial AI voice API, against an $800–$1,250 asking rate for a human (B8, B21; our
arithmetic). The measured job losses are real but narrow: salaried graphic designers fell about 8% in a
single year (a survey estimate), and the government's forecast now names AI (A2, A10). And the tools have let loose a flood
of new makers whose output mostly earns nothing: fully AI-made tracks are more than half of Deezer's
uploads on peak days and 1 to 3% of its streams (P9, D2-4). **Whether AI creates more creative work than
it destroys, nobody has counted. One side counts makers; the other counts paid jobs. Both can be right at
once.**

## The viewer's moment

**It starts with a question the viewer already carries: "can a machine do what I do, or what I pay
someone to do?"** The episode answers the plain version first: some of it, cheaply, yes; whether that
leaves fewer people paid to make things is measured only in patches. Then it goes to five people, each
with one number. (The first four are invented and unnamed, each built only from figures in the log. The
fifth is this channel.)

- **The designer: the count that turned.** Salaried graphic designers held flat for three years
  (211,890, 212,720, 214,260) and then fell to 197,830 in May 2025: about 16,400 fewer in one year, about
  1 in 13 (A1, A1b, A2, A1c; our arithmetic). The government's forecast flipped from +2% to −2% a decade,
  and its wording on AI hardened from "may reduce the need… to contract with freelance graphic
  designers" to "reduce the need for these workers" (A11, A10). Median pay rose 8.6% before inflation
  over 2022–25 (A3). ⚖ Not proof AI did it: the count leaves out the self-employed, pools three years of
  survey panels, and the 2023–25 advertising and tech slowdown is a live rival cause (A8).
- **The voice actor: the price at the cheap end.** About one in five voice actors who answered a
  voluntary survey by their advocacy group say they have knowingly lost at least one job to an AI voice,
  up from about one in seven a year earlier (B1, B2, B3, B4). Four in five say it has not happened to
  them. The price pressure is the strongest part of the case: about $1.60 for 20 minutes of AI narration
  against $800–$1,250 for a human (B8, B21; our arithmetic). ⚖ But AI narration was 0.03% of the
  audiobook revenue publishers report, and listeners' willingness to try it fell from 70% to 61% (B5).
  Nobody measures how much voice work exists, so nobody can say it is dying (B27, B33).
- **The game artist: the layoffs and the mood.** 28% of game workers surveyed were laid off in the past
  two years, a third in the US; visual and technical artists are the most negative about generative AI,
  at 64% (A26, A27). ⚖ The reasons they were given were restructuring, falling revenue and market shifts,
  and AI is not on the list (A28). Personal AI use did not rise (36% in both years); only the hostility
  did (A29).
- **The hobbyist: the door that opened.** The tools lift beginners most: in a writing experiment,
  time fell sharply and quality rose, with the gap between workers compressed (D1-2, D1-3); story writers
  with the least creative ideas gained the most (D1-4); on an art platform, artists who adopted AI made
  about 25% more work (D1-7). ⚖ But the hobbyist now competes with everyone else who got the same door:
  on Steam about one in three new games now discloses AI and those games draw about half as many reviews
  (D2-18, D2-23, D2-24), and in self-published ebooks the revenue per selling book fell, even for books
  with no AI text (D2-12).
- **This channel: one person and AI, against the team it would have taken.** (The channel beat, below.)

## The question

**Is AI destroying more creative work than it creates?** Under it sit two the record forces: **who gets
paid now, and for what?** And: **when one person makes with AI what used to take a team, is that a job
destroyed, a job created, or neither?**

## Why now

_Peg hunt run 2026-10-27 (peg-hunt skill), then every peg fact re-checked at the primary by hand pass D2
(rows P1–P21). Re-run the hunt before scripting._

In the four weeks before this episode, the music business showed both halves of the question in public. On
**2026-10-20 Spotify** reported, a year into its AI rules, that it had taken more than **50 million** AI "slop
and spam" tracks out of recommendations in 2026, on top of more than 75 million spam tracks removed outright;
that thousands of artist identities believed to be AI-generated now carry an **"AI Persona"** label and are kept
out of recommendations by default; and that real artists are filing tens of thousands of **AI Credits** a day to
show where they used AI in a song. A week earlier, on **2026-10-13, Deezer** said it now receives nearly 90,000
fully AI-generated tracks a day (more than half of all uploads on peak days) and will let listeners switch them
off. On **2026-10-06** a federal judge in Manhattan sentenced a North Carolina man to 18 months for using bot
accounts to stream hundreds of thousands of AI-made songs billions of times, taking more than $8 million from
royalties shared with real musicians. And the **US Copyright Office** is asking the public, until 2026-11-23, what streaming fraud has to do
with AI-generated music. AI is plainly making more creative work than ever; the open question is who it pays.

Sources (each checked at the primary, rows P1–P21):
- Spotify, "One Year In: Putting Human Artistry First on Spotify", 2026-10-20 (P1–P7; a company's report on its own platform)
- Deezer, "Deezer will let you fully exclude AI-generated music from your streaming experience", 2026-10-13 (P8–P13; self-report)
- US Attorney's Office, Southern District of New York, sentencing release, 2026-10-06 (P14–P17)
- US Copyright Office, "Music Streaming Fraud" notice of inquiry, Federal Register 2026-20537, 2026-10-07 (P19–P21)

**What each leg can carry (hand pass D2):**
- Spotify's 50 million were taken **out of recommendations**, not deleted (P1). The 75 million were
  **removed**, a separate bucket, and Spotify gave the same "75 million" in September 2025 for the
  previous 12 months, so it reads as a running total, not 75 million more this year (P2, D2-7). Never
  add the two into "125 million deleted", and not every one of them is AI.
- "AI Persona" labels an artist **identity** believed to be AI-generated, not every track that used AI
  (P3). Personas stay out of recommendations by default unless a listener follows one (P3).
- Spotify says its AI detection "has limitations" (P7): say so whenever its figures are used.
- Deezer's "more than half" is half of **uploads on peak days**, not of listening (P9); fully AI tracks
  are 1 to 3% of its streams (D2-4).
- The sentencing release says the songs were "generally created using super intelligence", the wording
  federal releases now use under Executive Order 14434 (P17). Quote it as written, or say "AI" in our own
  voice; never imply the government says "AI". "First-ever federal streaming-fraud case" is press only

- The Copyright Office's question 4 asks for data on the link between streaming fraud and AI music

## Settled (not the debate)

Neither team may fight these.

**The government's counts: one clear fall, the rest noisy**
- **Salaried graphic designers: flat for three years, then −7.7% in one year** (211,890 in May 2022;
  212,720; 214,260; 197,830 in May 2025) (A1, A1b, A2, A1c). Median pay $57,990 → $62,960, +8.6% before
  inflation (A3).
- **BLS now names AI for designers.** Projected −2% for 2025–35, "Automated design tools, such as
  artificial intelligence (AI), are projected to make graphic designers more productive and reduce the need
  for these workers" (A10; quote it whole: the "more productive" half is the other side's point); the previous edition projected
  +2% and said only that AI "may reduce the need… to contract with freelance graphic designers" (A11).
  BLS also says AI illustrations "may compete with illustrators" (A12), and that some animation tasks
  can be done faster with AI, which "may dampen demand" (A13).
- **Animators' salaried count fell sharply,** 35,990 (2022) → 19,970 (2025), most of it after May 2023
  (A6). ⚖ About 63% of animators are self-employed and this count misses them; the same years held the
  2023 strikes and the streaming pullback (A7, A8). Never "45%".
- **Radio announcers and DJs fell about a fifth,** 26,820 → 21,240 (A4); BLS blames station consolidation
  first and mentions AI DJs (A14). They are not voice actors.
- **Fine artists and illustrators show no measured decline** (A5): the "−7%" was noise.
- **BLS has no voice-actor count.** Voice work sits inside Actors: 67,500 jobs, projected flat to 2035
  (B27, A9). That is a projection, not a measurement of AI.
- **The forecasts are small declines:** designers −1.7%, animators −0.3%, fine artists −2.9%, announcers
  −7.6%, actors flat, 2025–35, all workers including the self-employed (A9).

**The freelance platforms: writing work down; images split**
- **Writing:** on Upwork, writing freelancers' monthly jobs fell 2% and earnings 5.2% after ChatGPT
  (A16); on another platform, job posts for automation-prone writing and coding work fell 21% within
  eight months (A20); across a large platform's 116 skill clusters, demand for substitutable skills such
  as writing and translation fell 20–50% against trend, sharpest for 1–3 week jobs (A25).
- **Images:** the 2023 Upwork working paper finds image freelancers' jobs −3.7% and earnings −9.4% after
  the image generators (A17), and another platform's image-creation posts fell 17% (A20); a third study
  found **no significant effect** of image generators on image work (A23). Contested across studies.
- **What these studies cannot see:** work that moved off the platform (A18), or whether a job was
  replaced or simply never posted (A22). Experience did not protect freelancers: "suggestive evidence
  that top freelancers are disproportionately affected" (A19).

**Games: heavy layoffs, rising hostility, flat use, no AI on the reasons list**
- **28% laid off in two years (33% in the US); half say their employer cut in the past year** (A26).
- **52% now say generative AI is hurting the industry,** up from 30% and 18%; visual and technical
  artists 64% (A27). Students cite "AI-led displacement" among their worries (A27).
- **Personal use is flat at 36%** (A29). **The layoff reasons given in 2025:** restructuring 22%,
  revenue 18%, market shifts 15%, no reason 19%; AI is not a category (A28). Global layoff tallies peaked
  in 2024 (A30).

**The flood: real supply, thin income, on every platform checked**
- **Music.** Fully AI tracks went from about 10% of Deezer's daily uploads in January 2025 to more than
  half on peak days in June 2026, about 90,000 a day (D2-2, D2-3, D2-1, P9). They are 1 to 3% of
  streams, and up to 85% of those streams were fraudulent in 2025 (D2-4). On Spotify, likely-AI tracks are
  10 times as likely to fall below the 1,000-stream royalty floor (P6).
- **Books.** In self-published genre ebooks, revenue per book that sold at least one copy fell from the
  2023 to the 2025 launch cohorts, in seven of eight genres for books with **no** AI text; the authors say
  it is not just a mix effect (D2-12; a working paper under review, D2-13). Books with substantial AI text
  were 20% of the sample and 11.3% of revenue (D2-11).
- **Games.** AI-flagged Steam launches rose from about 13 a month to about 530; about one in three new
  games carried the disclosure by mid-2026 (D2-18, D2-19), while total 2025 releases grew only 11% (D2-20).
  Disclosing games drew about 52.6% fewer reviews after controls, which cannot automatically be blamed on
  AI (D2-23, D2-24).
- **Self-publishing:** US self-published titles +38.7% in 2025, to more than 3.5 million; Bowker credits
  "new tools", not AI by name (D2-16, D2-17).

**The tools lift beginners most, and every study watched people already doing the work**
- Customer support: +14% on average, +34% for novices, minimal for experts (D1-1). Writing: time −0.8 SD,
  quality +0.4 SD, the gap between workers compressed (D1-2, D1-3). Story writers: the least creative
  gained most (D1-4). Consultants: lower-skilled gained most, "a significant equalizer" (D1-10). Art
  platform: adopters +25% output, +50% favourites per view (D1-7).
- **Each came with a cost:** stories grew more alike (D1-5); average novelty fell on the art platform
  (D1-8); consultants were 19% less likely to be right on a task outside AI's range (D1-9);
  professional artists still beat laypeople with the same tool (D1-11).

**The law (US, as of 2026-10-27)**
- **A human author is required.** Prompts alone do not make you the author with today's tools (C1);
  purely AI-generated material is not protected, while human selection, arrangement and changes are
  (C2, C3). The D.C. Circuit upheld this in Thaler (C5), and the Supreme Court declined review on
  2026-03-02 (C6). It is Office guidance tied to current tools, not statute (C1).
- **A voice as such is not copyrightable** (C20, C21); protection comes from state voice and likeness
  laws and contracts (C33, C34).
- **Training: one district court, one settlement, one appeals ruling on a different kind of tool.**
  Bartz v. Anthropic: training Claude on books was fair use; scanning bought print books was fair use;
  keeping a permanent library of pirated copies was not (C7). It settled (C8, C9; see The law and the
  pay). The Copyright Office's pre-publication view: many training uses are likely transformative, but
  commercial use of vast troves of works to make competing content, especially through illegal access,
  "goes beyond established fair use boundaries" (C10), and, in the Office's view, AI's speed and scale
  pose "a serious risk of diluting markets" (C13). The one federal appeals ruling went against fair use
  for a non-generative search tool and set generative models apart (C15). The image cases will not reach
  a fair-use answer before late 2027 (C16, C19).
- **Unions won consent and pay terms, for members on covered productions:** the WGA (AI is not a writer;
  a writer can't be required to use it), kept and extended in 2026 (C24, C25, C26); SAG-AFTRA's 2025
  video-game contract (informed consent per use, at least 7.5x scale for real-time generation, consent
  suspendable in a strike) (C27, C28); its 2026 TV/Theatrical deal further restricts synthetic performers
  (C30, C31).

**Audiences: they say they mind; with money on the line, they barely act on it**
- People devalue art labelled AI-made, even when they say they can't tell the difference (D2-54).
  Unlabelled, AI often passes or wins: 97% couldn't pick the human song in Deezer's blind test (P13), and
  non-experts were below chance at spotting AI poems and rated them higher (D2-58). Being told a poem was
  human raised its ratings (D2-59).
- In the one incentivised test, the AI label lowered ratings but did not change reading time or
  willingness to pay, even for the 36% who said they prefer human writing (D2-57).
- Coca-Cola's 2024 AI holiday ad scored a perfect 5.9 with an ad-testing vendor while critics were
  furious (D2-60, D2-61).

**History: both precedents are real**
- **Typesetting ended an occupation** when customers did the layout themselves ("desktop publishing"),
  and a smaller new job replaced it (A15, D2-45, D2-46, D2-47).
- **Photography widened a trade:** US census counts of professional photographers went from 938 (1850)
  to 20,040 (1890), as reported by Cornell's library (D2-41, D2-42). In Edinburgh the miniature painters
  collapsed while "artists" grew, and photographers sent work to painters (D2-43, D2-44).
- **Disney's move to computer animation** cut its animation staff from 2,200 (1999) to 600, driven by
  box office and per-film hiring (D2-48, D2-49).
- **New work keeps appearing:** most US jobs today are in job specialties introduced after 1940, but
  automation's demand-eroding effect has grown while augmentation's has not (A36, D1-16).

**The broad labour studies (not about creative work)**
- Denmark: no effect larger than 2% on earnings or hours two years after ChatGPT (A34). US payroll data:
  entry-level hiring in AI-exposed jobs down 13% within firms against less-exposed jobs; overall hiring
  not meaningfully down because of AI (A35). Neither study is about creative work.

## Contested (the real fight)

The record settles the flood and the cheap end. The fight is over what it adds up to: fewer people paid
to make things, or more people making things.

**Side A — "It's taking the work."** (Steelmanned from primaries.)
- **The cleanest government count turned, and the government names AI.** Salaried designers −7.7% in a
  single year after three flat ones; the forecast flipped from growth to decline; the AI wording went
  from freelancers to "these workers" (A2, A10, A11).
- **The causal studies find the losses.** Writing freelancers' jobs −2% and earnings −5.2% (A16); image
  freelancers −3.7% and −9.4% in the 2023 working paper (A17); posts −21% for automation-prone work and
  −17% for image creation (A20); substitutable skills −20–50%, sharpest for short 1–3 week jobs, and demand for novice workers fell
  in general (A25). Being good did not protect you: top freelancers may be hit
  disproportionately (A19). And the authors of the broadest exposure study note that jobs "first become
  more precarious (e.g., writers becoming freelancers)" (A37).
- **At the cheap end, the machine costs a sliver of the human rate.** About $1.60 for 20 minutes of AI narration against
  $800–$1,250 for a human (B8, B21; our arithmetic). Apple makes AI-narrated audiobooks free to produce, and
  so, for now, does Amazon in a US-only beta (B11, B13). Adobe's own survey heard from creatives doing fewer social-media jobs "because some
  clients are now generating that work themselves" (D1-14).
- **Voice actors report it.** The share of NAVA's respondents who say they have knowingly lost a job to
  an AI voice rose from 14% to 21% in a year (B2, B3).
- **The flood dilutes the pool for humans too.** Revenue per selling ebook fell even for books with no
  AI text (D2-12). Spotify warns spam "can dilute the royalty pool" (D2-8). A man with bots and
  hundreds of thousands of AI songs took more than $8 million from royalties shared with real musicians
  (P14, P16).
- **The tools were built on creators' work.** A court found Anthropic's library of pirated books was not
  fair use, and the case settled for $1.5 billion (C7, C8). The Copyright Office warns of market dilution
  (C13). Voice actors sued a voice-AI company over training on their recordings (C20).
- **History's sharpest precedent fits.** Typesetting ended when the customer did it themselves (D2-46),
  and Autor and co-authors find automation now erodes demand more than it used to (A36).
- **The workers closest to it say so.** Game industry visual and technical artists are the most negative
  about generative AI, at 64% (A27); in China, a recruiter estimated game illustrator jobs fell about 70%
  in a year, citing regulation, the economy and AI (A32, colour only).

**Side B — "It's making new work."** (Steelmanned from primaries.)
- **The broad data shows no collapse.** Denmark's null (A34); overall US hiring not meaningfully down
  because of AI (A35); BLS forecasts for creative jobs are small, actors flat (A9, B27).
- **The biggest drops have rival causes.** Game layoffs came with reasons of restructuring and revenue,
  not AI (A28), and peaked in 2024 (A30); the animator fall spans the strikes and the streaming pullback
  (A6); designers held flat through May 2024 (A1). Fine artists rose 12% in the last year (A5). A third
  study finds no measurable effect of image generators on image work (A23), and the platform studies
  cannot see work that moved elsewhere (A18).
- **The tools lift the people who were furthest behind.** Novices +34%, the least creative writers
  gaining most, "a significant equalizer" (D1-1, D1-4, D1-10); on an art platform, adopters made more and
  favourites became less concentrated among them (D1-7, D1-8).
- **New paid streams exist.** ElevenLabs says voice creators have earned over $22 million from more than
  10,400 creators (B15, a vendor self-report); union deals let actors license replicas per use with
  consent (C27, B20). Spotify's artists file tens of thousands of AI Credits a day: AI as a declared
  instrument (P5). US creative job postings rose 8% in six months, with the share naming AI skills up from
  10% to 15% (D1-13, a vendor's count on a small base), and people training for creative work "see AI as
  the price of admission to a craft they are trying to enter" (D1-14).
- **The human premium is holding.** People still value labelled human work more (D2-54, D2-59); Deezer
  is building a switch to hide fully AI music, and 45% of its users surveyed want one (P8, P12); Spotify
  has verified more than 300,000 artists, over 70% independent (P4).
- **The damage is smaller than the headlines.** Deezer says the hit to human royalties on its platform is
  "minimal", because fraudulent streams are stripped from the pool (D2-5, a self-report). Amazon said it
  had "not seen a spike" when it capped uploads (D2-14).
- **History says new work arrives.** Photography took professional photographers from under a thousand
  to about twenty thousand in forty years (D2-41, D2-42); desktop publishing created a new job as it ended
  old ones (D2-47); most of today's jobs are in specialties created after 1940 (A36).
- **And the work that uses AI as a tool stays ownable.** The Copyright Office registered a track that
  used AI as a tool (C3).

### Where the record lands, said plainly (no false balance)
- **"Voice acting is dying": unmeasured, with heavy price pressure at the cheap end.** No dataset counts
  US voice work or income (B27, B33). The one loss figure is a lifetime question in an advocacy
  group's voluntary survey: a real and rising signal at the margin, with four in five saying it hasn't
  happened to them (B1–B4). AI narration is a sliver of reported audiobook revenue (B5). The price gap is
  the strongest part of the claim (B8, B21). Say it as a hypothesis, never a finding.
- **Designers: the one clean count.** Salaried graphic designers −7.7% in one year, with BLS now naming
  AI (A2, A10). The cause is not shown (A8).
- **Freelance work: split.** Three platform studies find writing work down; for images, from a 17% drop
  in postings on one platform to no measurable effect on another (A16, A17, A20, A23, A25).
- **The flood: real supply, thin income.** Every platform checked shows AI-made output growing far
  faster than what it earns (D2-1, D2-4, D2-12, D2-19, D2-23, P6). Nothing here shows AI-enabled
  newcomers earning a living on average.
- **"It creates creators": it lifts beginners most, and that has never been measured as new
  creators.** Every study watched people already doing the work (D1-1 to D1-11).
- **"Jobs that would never have existed": nobody has counted it.** It is a counterfactual about work
  that was never paid for; the nearest evidence is indirect (A36, D1-14, D1-15), and the freelance
  studies cannot tell a replaced job from one never posted (A22).
- **Games: heavy layoffs, rising hostility, and no survey attributes the layoffs to AI** (A26–A29).
- **On the title's net question, the record cannot answer.** This is the genuine 50/50, and it is
  where the teams should spend their time.

## The channel beat (his Ruling A: this channel is the catalyst, ep49 only)

**What it is.** This show is made by one person working with AI: AI does the research desk's digging,
and the cast speak with open, freely available voice models. That is the example, and it cuts both ways.

**What the team would have cost, at published US rates (our estimate, not measured).** Hiring the roles
for one ~20-minute researched episode (a researcher and fact-checker, a writer, a video editor, a
motion-graphics artist, a thumbnail designer, a narrator, six character voices and a sound mixer) comes to
**roughly $4,900 to $18,900 an episode** (D1, "our arithmetic", from rows D1-28 to D1-43):

Assumptions, all ours and all said with the number: the hours per role; 6 character voices with a

whether this contract would apply is not established (D1-28). Several low-end inputs are vendor
reproductions (D1-38, D1-39, D1-43, all APPROXIMATE), which is one more reason the range airs only as our
estimate, rounded. Motion graphics is the biggest swing and the least certain line. The range is before

**The AI side, at list price, for scale (not the channel's own bill):** AI narration through a commercial
API at about $0.08 a minute (D1-24); an AI assistant plan at $20 to $200 a month (D1-27). An open voice
model run locally has no per-minute fee; its hardware and electricity were not costed.

**The rebuttal, in the same breath, every time:**
- **The roles it didn't hire** are exactly the people this episode is about: researchers, writers,
  editors, designers, voice actors.
- **"Open and available" voices were trained on recordings of people.** That is the other side's first
  answer, and it is a live fight: voice actors sued a voice-AI company over training on their recordings;
  that part of their claim was dismissed with leave to amend, a narrower claim over one actor's recording
  survived, and the case is frozen by the company's bankruptcy (C20, C22). ⚠ What the specific open model
  this show uses was trained on was not researched: never claim it either way.
- **And the stake, in one line:** "We have a stake in this answer: the show is our own example."

**The other side of the same example:** without the tools, this channel would not exist; nobody was going
to hire that team for it. ⚖ That is the "jobs that would never have existed" argument, and the record
cannot measure it (Where the record lands).

**Exposure, one line only:** YouTube does not monetise channels whose AI personas present as human
experts advising on health, legal issues, finances or politics (D1-19). That is a rule the show works
under; never say or imply it has been applied to this channel.

⛔ Never the show's verdict. ⛔ Nothing personal: no day job, employer, budget, spending, hours,
hardware, location, surname or background. "One person and AI" is the whole of it.

## The law and the pay (and Anthropic)

- **Anthropic, which makes Claude, the AI model this channel uses, is in the record.** Say it plainly,
  once, where the case is cited (allowed in ep49 under Ruling A). In Bartz v. Anthropic, Judge Alsup ruled
  on 2025-06-23 that training Claude on the books was fair use, that scanning purchased print books was
  fair use, and that keeping a permanent central library of pirated copies was not (C7). The case settled:
  a **$1.5 billion** fund, **about $3,000 per work before costs and fees**, paid in installments ending
  **2027-09-27**; it releases past claims only, releases **no** claims over AI outputs, is not "a license to torrent, scan,
  or train AI models", and requires Anthropic to destroy the books downloaded from the pirate datasets
  (C8). Final approval came on **2026-07-20**; the first payments are expected **on or before
  2026-11-15**, so none had gone out by this check (C9). Neither soften it ("Anthropic was cleared") nor
  inflate it ("found guilty of piracy"): the piracy question was headed to trial and settled.
- **What creators can own:** a human author is required; AI used as a tool keeps the human's work
  ownable (C1–C3, C5, C6).
- **What protects a voice:** not copyright (C21), but state laws such as Tennessee's ELVIS Act, which
  covers simulated voices and tools whose primary purpose is producing a particular person's voice (C34),
  and contracts. The federal NO FAKES Act is still a bill: it cleared Senate Judiciary in June 2026 and has
  had no floor vote (C37, C38).
- **What the unions won** covers members on covered productions only (C24–C30): a non-union creator, or a
  YouTube channel, is not covered.
- **What the payouts look like:** ElevenLabs $22 million+ to 10,400+ creators since early 2024, about
  $2,100 each on average by our division, mixing a cumulative total with a current headcount (B15, B16);
  in NAVA's own survey the most common 2025 earning from a synthetic voice was $0 (B28); Shutterstock's
  fund paid photographers about three-quarters of a cent per image in one survey of 58 (D2-36); Adobe's
  and Getty's formulas publish no totals (D2-34, D2-38).

## The reframe

**The shouting treats creative work as one job market. The record says the hit falls first on a tier:
the short, cheap, high-volume jobs.** The sharpest freelance drop was for 1–3 week jobs (A25); the
price gap is widest at the generic end (B8, B21); clients are making their own low-stakes social-media
work (D1-14). On our reading those short jobs are how many newcomers get in, and the study found demand for novices fell in general (A25); no source counts first jobs directly. ⚠ It is not only the bottom: top
freelancers were not protected either (A19).

**And the two sides are counting different things.** The "it creates" side counts **makers** and
**output**, and those have exploded (D2-1, D2-16, D2-18). The "it destroys" side counts **paid jobs** and
**income**, and where those are measured they are flat to falling, with income per maker thinning
(A2, D2-12, P6). Both can be true at once. This channel is one case of the first: the tools
turned someone who would never have bought creative labour into a producer of it. Whether that took a
job depends on whether channels like it would otherwise have hired anyone, and nobody has measured that.

**The closest precedent is the customer doing it themselves.** When desktop publishing arrived,
typesetting and layout moved to "customers on their computers", and a smaller new job replaced the old
ones (D2-46, D2-47). Photography is the hopeful version: it widened the trade into many more *paid*
jobs (D2-41, D2-42), which is exactly what the AI flood has not yet shown.

**So the useful question is not "more or fewer jobs". It is "who gets paid now, and for what?"**

## Both sides quietly agree

- **The tools help beginners most and experts least** (D1-1, D1-3, D1-10). That is the newcomer's
  opportunity and the paid newcomer's threat in the same finding.
- **Work valued because a particular human made it holds up best.** Even AI-voice vendors sell licensed,
  named voices (B17), and business buyers in a vendor's survey say they prioritise AI voices from real,
  attributed actors (B30). Audiences rate labelled human work higher (D2-54, D2-59).
- **Some consent and pay is owed for the human work the tools learned from.** A $1.5 billion settlement
  (C8), union consent terms (C27, C28), vendors' contributor funds and payouts (D2-32, D2-34, B15), and the
  Copyright Office's suggestion of collective licensing where markets fail (C11) all treat it as owed in
  some form. The disagreement is over how much, to whom, and whether it is enough.
- **Nobody has measured the counterfactual.** Neither side can show the creative work that exists only
  because of AI, or the work that would have been paid for without it.

## Verdict framing

**No winner.** The judge says what the record settles (the cheap end is priced down; the flood is real
and mostly unpaid; one clean count of designers fell; the tools lift beginners most; the net is
uncounted) and hands the viewer **one dial: "When you watch, listen, read or hire, how much does it
matter to you that a person made it?"** Every viewer is a buyer of creative work, and where buyers set
that dial decides who gets paid. The judge names the settings; the judge never sets it for the viewer.

1. **"It doesn't matter. Just make it."** Behind it: AI narration at about $0.08 a minute (B8); Amazon's
   and Apple's free AI-narrated audiobooks (B11, B13). Its documented failure: the flood mostly earns
   nothing, AI tracks are 1 to 3% of Deezer's streams, and Deezer says up to 85% of those streams were fraudulent (D2-4, its own figure), likely-AI tracks on Spotify
   are 10 times as likely to earn nothing (P6), and listeners' willingness to try AI narration fell
   (B5).
2. **"Tell me when it's AI."** Behind it: Spotify's AI Persona labels and AI Credits (P3, P5); YouTube's
   rule that realistic AI content be disclosed (D1-21). Its documented failure: Spotify says detection
   "has limitations" (P7), and in the one test with money at stake the label changed ratings, not what
   people paid (D2-57).
3. **"Pay the people it learned from."** Behind it: union consent-and-pay terms (C27, C28); the Bartz
   settlement (C8); ElevenLabs' voice library payouts (B15); Shutterstock's contributor fund (D2-32). Its
   documented failure: the most common 2025 earning from a synthetic voice in NAVA's survey was $0 (B28);
   fractions of a cent per image (D2-36); the settlement's first payments not yet sent (C9); union terms
   reach members only.
4. **"Only if a person made it."** Behind it: Deezer's switch to hide fully AI-made music, which 45% of
   its users surveyed say they want (P8, P12). Its documented failure: 97% couldn't tell an AI song from
   a human one in a blind test (P13), and AI poems passed as human and scored higher (D2-58).
