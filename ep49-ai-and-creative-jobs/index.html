AI Art vs Artists: Is AI Killing Creative Jobs, or Making New Ones?

Is AI destroying more creative work than it creates?

Last changed 2026-11-01 · See every change

▶ Rewatch on YouTube

AI can make creative work for almost nothing. Who still gets paid to make it?

Settled on the record·Still argued

Why it was in the news

In October, Spotify said it had taken more than 50 million AI slop and spam tracks out of its recommendations this year, and a federal judge in Manhattan sentenced a man to 18 months for using bot accounts to stream AI-made songs billions of times, taking more than $8 million from royalties shared with real musicians. So if you can make a song, a picture or a voice with AI for almost nothing, and you'd never have paid anyone anyway, who are you hurting?

Settled

What both sides accept

Both teams accepted that at the cheap end, a machine now costs a sliver of a person's rate. The flood of AI-made work is real, and most of it earns little. The cleanest count, salaried graphic designers, fell about one in thirteen in a year, though the count can't show why. And nobody has counted the work that exists only because of AI.

Still argued

The fight

Green arguesIt's taking the work

The freelance studies were built to find a cause, and for writing they found losses: demand for skills AI can stand in for fell 20 to 50% against trend, sharpest for jobs of one to three weeks, the quick, cheap work many people start with. The flood thins the pool for people already in it: revenue per selling ebook fell even for books with no AI text. And the new paid work is thin: about $2,000 each from one AI voice company over more than two years, and zero was the most common answer for what a synthetic voice earned.

▶ Hear Green at 4:01 on YouTube
Gold arguesIt's making new work

The broad data shows no collapse: a Danish study found no effect on earnings or hours larger than 2%, game layoffs came with other reasons, and one image study found no effect at all. The tools lift beginners most, and nobody has counted the work that exists only because of them; this show is one example, since nobody was going to hire that team for it. And buyers still care who made it: told a poem was by a person, readers rate it higher.

▶ Hear Gold at 10:54 on YouTube

What the shouting gets wrong

It sounds like one fight over one job market. The record says the first hit lands on a tier: the short, cheap, quick jobs. And the two sides aren't counting the same thing: one counts makers and output, which have exploded; the other counts paid jobs and income, which are flat or falling wherever they're measured. So the question isn't more jobs or fewer. It's who gets paid now, and for what.

The verdict

Where the record leans
Where the record leans: the hit lands first on the short, cheap, high-volume jobs, and AI-made work is growing far faster than what it pays. Whether AI destroys more creative work than it creates, the record can't say.
What didn't hold up
That voice acting is dying: nobody counts voice work. That AI songs are draining royalties everywhere: the case was bots, and Deezer says the hit to human royalties there is minimal. That the label deals pay musicians: the players are suing. That AI creates creators: nobody has measured it.

Still argued · your call

Your dial: When you watch, listen, read or hire, how much does it matter to you that a person made it?

The judge left one dial: how much it matters to you that a person made it. These are the four settings the judge named, from not at all to only if a person made it, and nobody was declared the winner. Open one to see what the record says it buys, what it costs, and what nobody has measured. Nothing is saved or counted.

  1. 1Not at all, just make it
    What it buys
    Cheap. At the cheap end, a machine now costs a sliver of a person's rate: twenty minutes of narration through a commercial AI voice service lists at about a dollar and a half, against a human's asking rate of $800 to over $1,000 for a finished narration that long. And the tools lift beginners most: in a customer-support rollout, novices got 34% more done.
    What it costs
    Cheap, but the flood mostly earns nothing. Fully AI-made tracks are more than half of Deezer's uploads on peak days but 1 to 3% of its streams, and on Spotify, tracks likely to be AI are 10 times as likely to fall below the line where royalties start. Revenue per selling ebook fell even for books with no AI text. And the hit lands first on the short, cheap, high-volume jobs: demand for skills AI can stand in for, like writing and translation, fell 20 to 50% against trend, sharpest for jobs lasting one to three weeks.
    Not measured
    How much creative work exists only because of AI: nobody has counted it.

    On the record: row F6 of the fact-check log, row D1-1 of the fact-check log, row D2-4 of the fact-check log, row P6 of the fact-check log, row A25 of the fact-check log

  2. 2Tell me when it's AI
    What it buys
    Spotify labels AI artists: thousands of artist identities it believes are AI-generated carry an AI Persona label and stay out of recommendations unless you follow them. Artists also file tens of thousands of AI Credits a day, declaring AI the way you'd credit an instrument.
    What it costs
    Spotify admits its detection has limits. And in the one test where people's own money was on the line, the AI label changed how they rated a story, not what they'd pay for it.

    On the record: row P3 of the fact-check log, row P5 of the fact-check log, row P7 of the fact-check log, row D2-57 of the fact-check log

  3. 3Pay the people it learned from
    What it buys
    Union terms and the Anthropic settlement. The video game actors' strike ran 320 days and ended with a contract ratified by 95% of the votes cast: consent for each use of AI on an actor's voice or likeness. Screen and TV writers' terms say a studio can't require a writer to use AI. Authors' suit against Anthropic settled for $1.5 billion, about $3,000 per book before fees.
    What it costs
    Payouts so far are thin. One AI voice company's $22 million to more than 10,000 creators is about $2,000 each on average, by our division, over more than two years, and zero was the most common answer for what a synthetic voice earned in 2025. The label deals pay labels and publishers; the musicians' union is suing. Union terms cover union members on covered work, not a freelancer or a YouTube channel, and the settlement covers past claims only, none over what the AI produces.
    Not measured
    Whether the players see any of the label deals' money: that's in dispute in court.

    On the record: row T1-23 of the fact-check log, row C24 of the fact-check log, row C8 of the fact-check log, row F8 of the fact-check log, row F18 of the fact-check log

  4. 4Only if a person made it
    What it buys
    A person's name on the work still counts: told a poem was by a person, readers rate it higher. Deezer is building that switch, a toggle that keeps fully AI-made music out of search, and 45% in its commissioned survey want to filter it out. Buyers have pushed back: an indie games award was taken back over AI placeholder textures, and Larian dropped generative AI from its concept art after fans pushed back.
    What it costs
    Nearly everyone in Deezer's blind test couldn't tell: 97% couldn't tell fully AI-made music from human-made. And the rating a person's name earns didn't change what people would pay in the one test with real money.

    On the record: row D2-59 of the fact-check log, row P8 of the fact-check log, row P12 of the fact-check log, row P13 of the fact-check log, row T1-15 of the fact-check log

Whichever you pick: Where buyers set this dial decides who gets paid. Whether AI destroys more creative work than it creates, the record can't say.

What our fact-check caught

34 of the 333 claims we checked came back wrong in our drafts. Every one is in the published log.

  1. Draft saidVoice acting is dying: a fifth of voice actors have lost work to AI.

    The record saysNobody counts voice work: the government folds it into actors. The one in five comes from a voluntary survey by voice actors' advocacy group, of people who say they've ever knowingly lost at least one job to an AI voice. Four in five say it hasn't happened to them.

  2. Draft saidAI songs are draining musicians' royalties everywhere.

    The record saysThe big case was fraud: bots streaming songs to collect royalties. On Deezer, fully AI tracks are 1 to 3% of streams, and Deezer says the hit to human artists' royalties there is minimal, because fraudulent streams are removed from the pool. Spotify warns that spam can dilute the royalty pool, but hasn't measured a loss.

  3. Draft saidThe record labels' AI deals pay musicians.

    The record saysThe deals pay the labels and publishers. The musicians' union has sued two of the labels, saying recordings its members played on were licensed to AI music companies without pay or credit. That's in court.

  4. Draft saidAI is creating new creators.

    The record saysNobody has measured that. Every study that found gains watched people already doing the work, and the gains came with costs: AI-assisted stories grew more alike, and on one art platform, average novelty fell.

  5. Draft saidThe man behind the AI streaming fraud faced sixty years in prison.

    The record saysThat added up charges he never pleaded to. The count he pleaded guilty to carried a maximum of five years, and he was sentenced to 18 months.
