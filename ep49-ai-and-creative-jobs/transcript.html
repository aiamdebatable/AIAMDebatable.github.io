The research · Who gets paid now: ownership, voices, the label deals, the strike, the payouts

ErinStart with what you can own. Under the US Copyright Office's current guidance, prompts alone don't make you the author of what an AI makes. Purely AI-made material isn't protected; what a person selects, arranges or changes can be.

ErinNot through copyright. A federal judge in New York ruled that copyright doesn't protect a voice as such. What protects a voice is state law, like Tennessee's, which covers simulated voices, and contracts. The federal No Fakes Act is still only a bill.

RajSo who's actually getting paid by the AI companies?

ErinThe record labels. In late twenty twenty-five, Universal settled with the AI music company Udio, and Warner settled with Udio and with Suno. Now the labels license their catalogues to AI music firms.

RajSo the musicians get paid now.

ErinThe labels and publishers do. In June, the musicians' union sued Universal and Warner, saying recordings its members played on were licensed to those AI companies without pay or credit. That's in court, so whether the players see any of it is in dispute.

RajWhat about actors?

ErinUnion actors fought for terms. The video game actors' strike began in July twenty twenty-four over AI and ran three hundred and twenty days. It ended with a contract ratified by ninety-five percent of the votes cast: consent for each use of AI on an actor's voice or likeness.

RajEvery game?

ErinNo. The strike reached only union games begun after August twenty twenty-three. Screen and TV writers have their own terms: a studio can't require a writer to use AI. All of it covers union members on covered work, not a freelancer or a YouTube channel.

The verdict

ColeNo winner today. First what's settled, then what held up on each side, and then a dial that belongs to you.

ColeSettled: at the cheap end, a machine now costs a sliver of a person's rate. The flood of AI-made work is real, and most of it earns little. The cleanest count, salaried designers, fell about one in thirteen in a year. And nobody has counted the work that exists only because of AI.

ColeWhat held up for green. The freelance studies found writing work down, sharpest for short jobs. Revenue per selling ebook fell even for books with no AI. And the new payouts are thin: zero was the most common answer for what a synthetic voice earned.

ColeWhat held up for gold. The broad data shows no collapse. Game layoffs came with other reasons, and one image study found no effect. The tools lift beginners most. And people still rate work higher when they're told a person made it.

ColeWhat didn't hold up. That voice acting is dying: nobody counts voice work. That AI songs are draining royalties everywhere: the case was bots, and Deezer says the hit to human royalties there is minimal. That the label deals pay musicians: the players are suing. That AI creates creators: nobody has measured it.

ColeWhere the record leans: the hit lands first on the short, cheap, high-volume jobs, and AI-made work is growing far faster than what it pays. Whether AI destroys more creative work than it creates, the record can't say.

ColeSo the dial: when you watch, listen, read or hire, how much does it matter to you that a person made it? Not at all, just make it: cheap, but the flood mostly earns nothing. Tell me when it's AI: Spotify labels AI artists, though it admits its detection has limits.

ColePay the people it learned from: union terms and the Anthropic settlement, though payouts so far are thin. Only if a person made it: Deezer is building that switch, yet nearly everyone in its blind test couldn't tell. Where buyers set this dial decides who gets paid. Where you set yours is your call.
